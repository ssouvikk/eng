# EXCRETE: An Etymological Word-Power Study

Following the vocabulary-building approach of Norman Lewis’s Word Power Made Easy

## 1. EXCRETE

# EXCRETE

Verb · Pronunciation: /ɪkˈskriːt/

Core idea: To discharge waste substances from a living organism.

UPSC relevance: Human physiology, environmental pollution, nitrogen cycle, wastewater management, and public health.

### A. Meaning of the Components

Excrete comes from Latin excernere, meaning “to sift out” or “separate.” It can be analysed into two historical components:

- Ex- = out, away from.
- Cernere = to separate, sift, or distinguish.

The Latin participial form cretus appears in the development of English words such as excrete and secrete. The underlying idea is separating something from a mixture.&#x20;

Etymology, Origin & Meaning

+1



Memory key: Ex- suggests “out”; -crete connects with the historical idea of separating.

### B. Literal Meaning

Excrete = to separate and discharge waste substances from the body.

For example, the kidneys excrete certain metabolic waste products through urine.

### C. Semantic Evolution

Stage 1: Separate or sift out

The Latin root cernere conveyed the idea of separating or sifting.

Stage 2: Separate waste within a living organism

The term became associated with the body's removal of metabolic waste products.

Stage 3: Biological discharge

In modern biology, excretion refers to removing waste substances produced by metabolism or present in excess.

### D. Modern Meaning

Excrete (verb): To eliminate metabolic waste products or excess substances from an organism.

Examples include the kidneys excreting urea and the lungs excreting carbon dioxide.

Important distinction: Excretion is not the same as egestion. Excretion removes substances such as metabolic waste; egestion removes undigested food from the digestive tract.

### E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

| Collocation               | Meaning and application                                         |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Excrete metabolic waste   | Eliminate waste produced by cellular processes.                 |
| Excrete excess salts      | Remove surplus salts from the body; relevant to osmoregulation. |
| Excrete nitrogenous waste | Eliminate nitrogen-containing waste, such as ammonia or urea.   |

UPSC-ready sentence: The kidneys help maintain homeostasis by excreting metabolic waste and regulating the body's water and electrolyte balance.

Memory hook: Excrete = separate waste and send it out.

# 2. THE ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY OF EXCRETE

## The master root: KREI- — “to sieve, separate, distinguish”

The deeper historical root associated with excrete is the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root krei-, carrying the idea of sifting or separating. Through Latin cernere (“to sift, distinguish”) and related forms, this root gave English a surprisingly large vocabulary family.&#x20;

## The root map

## KREI-

To sieve, separate, distinguish

Latin: cernere

To sift, distinguish, separate

Separate and remove

Excrete, excretion, excrement, excretory

Recognise and distinguish

Separate and keep apart

Discrete, discreet, discretion

Judge and evaluate

These branches reflect related historical formations; not every word is formed by attaching a modern prefix to excrete.

The retention principle is simple: separate → distinguish → judge. The biological sense of excrete is one branch of a much larger family associated with sorting and making distinctions.

# Part I. The Direct EXCRET- Family

## 3. EXCRETION

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Ex- = out.
- Cret- = from Latin cernere, “to sift or separate.”
- -ion = a noun-forming suffix indicating an action or process.

### Literal Meaning

The process of separating and discharging substances.

### Semantic Evolution

The word developed from the action of separating or discharging substances into a technical term for the elimination of waste from a living organism.

### Modern Meaning

The biological process by which organisms eliminate metabolic waste products and excess substances.

### Common Usage

- Excretion of nitrogenous waste
- Renal excretion
- Excretion of metabolic by-products

UPSC-ready sentence: Renal excretion helps maintain homeostasis by eliminating waste products and regulating the body's fluid and electrolyte balance.

Memory hook: Excrete is the action; excretion is the process.

## 4. EXCRETORY

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

- Excret- = the stem associated with excrete.
- -ory = relating to, serving, or performing a function.

### Literal Meaning

Relating to the process of excretion.

### Semantic Evolution

The adjective developed to describe organs, systems, and functions involved in the elimination of waste.

### Modern Meaning

Pertaining to the removal of waste products from an organism.

### Common Usage

- Excretory system
- Excretory organs
- Excretory function

UPSC-ready sentence: The human excretory system, particularly the kidneys, plays a central role in maintaining internal chemical balance.

Memory hook: The suffix -ory often indicates a function or relationship, as in respiratory and circulatory.

## 5. EXCRETA

Part of speech: Noun, usually plural in form

### Meaning of the Components

Excreta comes from Latin excretum, “that which is separated or discharged.” It is not formed by adding the English plural suffix -a to excrete in the ordinary modern way.

### Literal Meaning

Things discharged from the body.

### Semantic Evolution

The term came to refer collectively to the waste materials eliminated by organisms.

### Modern Meaning

Waste matter discharged by humans or animals, especially faeces and urine in sanitation contexts.

### Common Usage

- Human excreta
- Safe disposal of excreta
- Excreta management

UPSC-ready sentence: Safe collection and treatment of human excreta are essential to prevent waterborne diseases and protect public health.

Memory hook: Excretion is the process; excreta are the materials discharged.

## 6. EXCREMENT

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Ex- = out.
- -crement comes from the Latin family associated with excernere, “to sift out or discharge.”

The word is historically related to excrete, not merely a word created by adding a suffix to the modern verb.

### Literal Meaning

Material discharged from the body.

### Semantic Evolution

Historically, excrement could refer more broadly to bodily discharges. Its dominant modern meaning narrowed to faecal waste.&#x20;

## 7. EXCRETIVE

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

- Excret- = the stem associated with excretion.
- -ive = tending to, performing, or relating to an action.

### Literal Meaning

Relating to or involving excretion.

### Semantic Evolution

The adjective has been used in technical descriptions of functions associated with the discharge of waste.

### Modern Meaning

Pertaining to excretion; relatively uncommon compared with excretory.

### Common Usage

- Excretive function
- Excretive process
- Excretive activity

UPSC-ready sentence: In standard biological terminology, excretory function is generally preferable to the less common expression excretive function.

Memory hook: Learn the word for recognition, but prefer excretory in most formal scientific writing.

# Part II. The DISCERN Branch — “To Recognise by Separating”

## 8. DISCERN

Part of speech: Verb

### Meaning of the Components

- Dis- = apart or in different directions in this historical formation.
- -cern- = related to Latin cernere, “to sift, distinguish.”

The historical construction is more complex than simply adding the modern prefix dis- to the English word cern.

### Literal Meaning

To distinguish one thing from another.

### Semantic Evolution

The idea of sifting material to separate its parts developed metaphorically into the mental act of distinguishing one thing, quality, or possibility from another.

### Modern Meaning

To perceive, recognise, or identify something, especially when it is difficult to notice.

### Common Usage

- Discern a pattern
- Discern the difference
- Discern the implications

UPSC-ready sentence: Policymakers must discern the structural causes of poverty rather than rely solely on aggregate economic indicators.

Memory hook: Just as a sieve separates grains, the mind discerns differences.

## 9. DISCERNMENT

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Discern = distinguish or recognise.
- -ment = a suffix forming a noun that denotes an action, result, or state.

### Literal Meaning

The ability or act of distinguishing.

### Semantic Evolution

The word developed from the act of recognising differences to the capacity for sound judgement and perceptive understanding.

### Modern Meaning

The ability to judge well, identify significant differences, and understand a situation accurately.

### Common Usage

- Political discernment
- Critical discernment
- Exercise discernment

UPSC-ready sentence: Administrative discernment is essential when officials must balance competing public interests under conditions of uncertainty.

Memory hook: Discern is what you do; discernment is the ability you exercise.

## 10. DISCERNIBLE

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

- Discern = perceive or distinguish.
- -ible = capable of being.

### Literal Meaning

Capable of being perceived or distinguished.

### Semantic Evolution

The word developed to describe differences, patterns, or effects that can be recognised through observation or analysis.

### Modern Meaning

Perceptible or distinguishable.

### Common Usage

- Discernible change
- Discernible pattern
- No discernible impact

UPSC-ready sentence: The policy produced no discernible improvement in service delivery, indicating a need for independent evaluation.

Memory hook: If something is discernible, you can discern it.

# Part III. The DISCRETE–DISCREET Branch — “Separate, Distinct, Careful”

## 11. DISCRETE

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

The word comes through Latin discretus, related to discernere, “to separate or distinguish.”

- The historical idea is separation into distinct parts.
- Discrete is not formed by adding a modern English suffix to crete.

### Literal Meaning

Separated or distinct.

### Semantic Evolution

The sense of separating things developed into the idea of entities that are individually distinct rather than continuous or blended together.

### Modern Meaning

Individually separate and distinct; in mathematics and statistics, consisting of distinct values or units rather than a continuous range.

### Common Usage

- Discrete variables
- Discrete categories
- Discrete units of analysis

UPSC-ready sentence: A statistical analysis must distinguish discrete variables, such as the number of children in a household, from continuous variables, such as income.

Memory hook: Discrete = distinct. Think of separate dots rather than a continuous line.

## 12. DISCREET

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

Discreet also comes from the historical family of Latin discernere, through forms associated with judgement and discernment.

Its development emphasised the ability to distinguish what is appropriate, prudent, or necessary.

### Literal Meaning

Exercising judgement or making careful distinctions.

### Semantic Evolution

The idea of good judgement developed into the modern sense of being tactful, prudent, and careful not to attract unnecessary attention.

### Modern Meaning

Careful in speech or behaviour, especially to avoid embarrassment, indiscretion, or unnecessary disclosure.

### Common Usage

- Discreet inquiry
- Discreet handling of information
- Discreet diplomatic engagement

UPSC-ready sentence: Civil servants must exercise discreet judgement when handling confidential information, while remaining accountable under the law.

Memory hook: Discreet = careful. Discrete = separate.

## 13. DISCRETION

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Discreet = prudent or careful in judgement.
- -ion = a noun-forming suffix.

The noun belongs to the same historical family as discrete and discern.

### Literal Meaning

The capacity to make distinctions and exercise judgement.

### Semantic Evolution

The sense of discerning what is appropriate developed into the modern ideas of prudence, freedom to decide, and authority to make choices within limits.

### Modern Meaning

The freedom or authority to decide according to judgement, within applicable rules and constraints; it can also mean tact or confidentiality.

### Common Usage

- Administrative discretion
- Discretionary powers
- At the discretion of the authority

UPSC-ready sentence: Administrative discretion is necessary for effective governance, but it must be constrained by legality, transparency, and accountability.

Memory hook: Discretion is the judgement used to choose among permissible options.

Important distinction: Discretion does not mean unlimited power. In public administration, discretionary authority remains subject to law and constitutional principles.

# Part IV. The DISCRIMINATE Branch — “To Distinguish Between”

## 14. DISCRIMINATE

Part of speech: Verb

### Meaning of the Components

- Dis- = apart.
- -crimin- = historically related to Latin cernere and the root family associated with distinguishing and judging.

### Literal Meaning

To separate or distinguish one thing from another.

### Semantic Evolution

The idea of distinguishing developed into two important modern senses: identifying differences accurately and treating people differently on the basis of particular characteristics. The second sense often carries a negative meaning.

### Modern Meaning

1. To recognise or identify differences.
2. To treat a person or group unfairly because of characteristics such as caste, sex, religion, or disability.

### Common Usage

- Discriminate between alternatives
- Discriminate against vulnerable groups
- Prohibit discrimination

UPSC-ready sentence: The Constitution and anti-discrimination laws seek to prevent unfair treatment while enabling the State to address historically entrenched social inequalities.

Memory hook: Discrimination can mean distinguishing in a neutral analytical sense or unfair treatment in a social and legal context.

## 15. DISCRIMINATION

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Discriminate = distinguish or treat differently.
- -ion = a noun-forming suffix.

### Literal Meaning

The act or process of distinguishing.

### Semantic Evolution

The term developed from the neutral act of making distinctions to a widely used term for unfair or unequal treatment.

### Modern Meaning

The unjust or prejudicial treatment of individuals or groups, although in technical contexts it can also mean the ability to distinguish.

### Common Usage

- Gender discrimination
- Caste-based discrimination
- Institutional discrimination

UPSC-ready sentence: Addressing caste-based discrimination requires not only legal safeguards but also effective enforcement, public awareness, and institutional accountability.

Memory hook: Discrimination is the act or practice; discriminatory describes something that involves discrimination.

## 16. DISCRIMINATORY

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

- Discriminat- = the stem of discriminate.
- -ory = relating to or characterised by an action.

### Literal Meaning

Characterised by making distinctions or treating categories differently.

### Semantic Evolution

The adjective became especially prominent in legal and social contexts where differential treatment is assessed for fairness, justification, or legality.

### Modern Meaning

Treating a person or group unfairly or unjustifiably on the basis of a particular characteristic.

### Common Usage

- Discriminatory practices
- Discriminatory laws
- Discriminatory treatment

UPSC-ready sentence: Public institutions must ensure that administrative procedures do not result in discriminatory treatment of marginalised communities.

Memory hook: A discriminatory policy is one that discriminates; it is not merely a policy that recognises legitimate differences.

# Part V. The CRITERION–CRITIC Branch — “To Judge and Evaluate”

## 17. CRITERION

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

Criterion comes from Greek kritērion, meaning a means or standard of judging, from kritēs, “judge,” and the Greek verb krinein, “to separate, decide, or judge.” This is a Greek branch of the wider historical root family associated with excrete.&#x20;

Etymology, Origin & Meaning

+1



### Literal Meaning

A standard by which something is judged.

### Semantic Evolution

The idea of separating or judging developed into the concept of a standard used to evaluate evidence, performance, eligibility, or quality.

### Modern Meaning

A principle, standard, or requirement used to make a judgement or decision.

### Common Usage

- Eligibility criteria
- Objective criteria
- Performance criteria

UPSC-ready sentence: Policy evaluation should employ transparent criteria, measurable indicators, and independent evidence.

Memory hook: A criterion is the yardstick used to judge something.

Grammar note: Criterion is singular; criteria is plural.

## 18. CRITICAL

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

- Crit- = from the Greek family of krinein, “to judge.”
- -ical = relating to or characterised by.

### Literal Meaning

Relating to judgement or evaluation.

### Semantic Evolution

The word developed from the idea of making judgements into several modern senses: analytical evaluation, extreme importance, and a dangerous or decisive condition.

### Modern Meaning

Depending on context, critical can mean:

1. Involving careful evaluation or analysis.
2. Extremely important or essential.
3. Relating to a dangerous or unstable condition.

### Common Usage

- Critical analysis
- Critical infrastructure
- Critical condition

UPSC-ready sentence: Critical infrastructure, including power grids and communication networks, requires robust protection against natural hazards and cyberattacks.

Memory hook: Critical does not always mean “negative.” A critical resource may simply be essential.

## 19. CRITIC

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

The word comes through Greek kritikos, “able to judge,” from krinein, “to judge or distinguish.”

### Literal Meaning

A person who judges or evaluates.

### Semantic Evolution

The term developed from a person qualified to evaluate literature, art, or arguments into a broader term for someone who assesses ideas, policies, or institutions.

### Modern Meaning

A person who evaluates, analyses, or expresses objections to a person, policy, work, or idea.

### Common Usage

- Government critic
- Literary critic
- Critics of public policy

UPSC-ready sentence: Constructive critics of public policy can strengthen democratic accountability by identifying gaps in implementation and unintended consequences.

Memory hook: A critic evaluates; criticism is the evaluation or expression of objections.

## 20. CRITICISM

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Critic = a person who evaluates.
- -ism = a suffix that can denote a practice, process, or system of thought.

### Literal Meaning

The act or practice of evaluating.

### Semantic Evolution

The word developed from formal appraisal to the modern senses of analysis, assessment, and the expression of objections.

### Modern Meaning

The act of examining something and identifying its merits or shortcomings; it can also mean disapproval.

### Common Usage

- Constructive criticism
- Public criticism
- Criticism of government policy

UPSC-ready sentence: Constructive criticism is an important element of democratic governance because it can expose implementation failures and improve public-service delivery.

Memory hook: Criticism can be analytical rather than hostile.

## 21. CRITIQUE

Part of speech: Noun / verb

### Meaning of the Components

Critique belongs to the French and Greek-derived family of criticism and judgement.

### Literal Meaning

An evaluation or act of evaluating.

### Semantic Evolution

The term came to refer particularly to a systematic, reasoned examination of the strengths, weaknesses, and assumptions of an argument, policy, or work.

### Modern Meaning

A detailed and reasoned analysis of something, often identifying both strengths and weaknesses.

### Common Usage

- Critique of a policy
- Critical appraisal
- Offer a critique

UPSC-ready sentence: A rigorous critique of a development programme should assess its objectives, implementation, outcomes, and distributional effects.

Memory hook: A critique is usually more systematic than a casual complaint.

Crisis comes from Greek krisis, meaning a judgement, decision, or decisive turning point, from krinein, “to judge or decide.”&#x20;

# Part VI. The SECRET–SECRETE Branch — “To Set Apart”

## 23. SECRET

Part of speech: Noun / adjective

### Meaning of the Components

- Se- = apart or aside in the Latin formation.
- -cret- = related to cernere, “to separate or distinguish.”

Latin secernere meant to separate or set apart; secretus meant separated, withdrawn, or hidden.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.etymonline.com\&sz=32)

Etymology, Origin & Meaning

+1



### Literal Meaning

Something set apart or kept separate.

### Semantic Evolution

The notion of something separated from general knowledge developed into the modern idea of information that is concealed or known only to a limited group.

### Modern Meaning

Information, knowledge, or a matter deliberately kept from public knowledge or from particular people.

### Common Usage

- Official secret
- Confidential information
- Keep a secret

UPSC-ready sentence: Government secrecy may be justified in limited circumstances, but it must be balanced against transparency, accountability, and the public's right to information.

Memory hook: A secret is information kept apart from general knowledge.

## 24. SECRETE

Part of speech: Verb

### Meaning of the Components

The biological verb secrete is related to Latin secernere, “to separate or set apart.” The same historical family also produced secret, although the words developed different modern meanings.

### Literal Meaning

To separate and release a substance.

### Semantic Evolution

The biological sense developed around the release of substances produced by cells or glands. A separate, historically related sense means to conceal or hide something.

### Modern Meaning

1. Biology: To produce and release a substance, such as a hormone, enzyme, or mucus.
2. General use: To conceal or hide something.

### Common Usage

- Secrete hormones
- Secrete digestive enzymes
- Secrete a substance

UPSC-ready sentence: Endocrine glands secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream, enabling chemical communication between different parts of the body.

Memory hook: Secret means something hidden; secrete in biology means to release a substance.

Important distinction: Secretion is not synonymous with excretion. Many secretions, such as hormones and digestive enzymes, perform useful functions rather than simply removing waste.

## 25. SECRETION

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Secret- = the stem associated with secrete.
- -ion = a noun-forming suffix indicating an action, process, or product.

### Literal Meaning

The act or product of separating and releasing a substance.

### Semantic Evolution

The term developed into a biological concept covering both the process of releasing substances and, in some contexts, the substances released.

### Modern Meaning

The production and release of substances by cells or glands; it can also refer to the substance itself.

### Common Usage

- Hormone secretion
- Gastric secretion
- Insulin secretion

UPSC-ready sentence: Insulin secretion by pancreatic beta cells is essential for regulating blood glucose levels.

Memory hook: Secrete is the verb; secretion is the process or its product.

## 26. SECRETORY

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

- Secret- = the stem associated with secrete.
- -ory = relating to or performing a function.

### Literal Meaning

Relating to secretion.

### Semantic Evolution

The adjective developed to describe cells, tissues, glands, and processes involved in producing and releasing substances.

### Modern Meaning

Pertaining to the production and release of substances by cells or glands.

### Common Usage

- Secretory cells
- Secretory glands
- Secretory activity

UPSC-ready sentence: Secretory cells in the pancreas release substances that contribute to digestion and metabolic regulation.

Memory hook: Secretory relates to secretion; excretory relates to excretion.

# Part VII. The ENDOCRINE–EXOCRINE Branch

These terms are especially valuable because they connect the historical idea of separation and release to human physiology.

## 27. ENDOCRINE

Part of speech: Adjective / noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Endo- = within.
- -crine = from Greek krinein, “to separate or secrete.”

### Literal Meaning

Secreting internally.

### Semantic Evolution

The term was developed for glands that release hormones into the internal environment, typically the bloodstream, rather than through ducts to a surface.

### Modern Meaning

Relating to glands that release hormones into the blood or surrounding internal fluid.

### Common Usage

- Endocrine glands
- Endocrine system
- Endocrine disorders

UPSC-ready sentence: The endocrine system regulates growth, metabolism, reproduction, and other physiological functions through hormones.

Memory hook: Endo = within. Endocrine glands release hormones internally.

## 28. EXOCRINE

Part of speech: Adjective / noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Exo- = outside or outward.
- -crine = from Greek krinein, “to separate or secrete.”

### Literal Meaning

Secreting outward.

### Semantic Evolution

The term developed to distinguish glands that release their products through ducts onto a body surface or into a body cavity from endocrine glands, which release hormones internally.

### Modern Meaning

Relating to glands that release their products through ducts.

### Common Usage

- Exocrine glands
- Exocrine secretion
- Exocrine pancreas

UPSC-ready sentence: The pancreas performs both endocrine and exocrine functions: it releases hormones into the blood and digestive enzymes through ducts into the small intestine.

Memory hook: Exo = outward; endo = within.

# Part VIII. Additional High-Value Cognates

The following words belong to the wider historical family associated with krei-. Their meanings are less obviously connected to excretion, but they are useful for advanced vocabulary and competitive examinations.

## 29. CERTAIN

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

Certain comes through French from Latin certus, associated historically with cernere, “to distinguish or decide.”

### Literal Meaning

Settled or determined.

### Semantic Evolution

The idea of reaching a decision developed into the sense of something established, reliable, or beyond reasonable doubt.

### Modern Meaning

Known to be true, definite, or assured; it can also mean particular but unspecified.

### Common Usage

- Certain conditions
- Beyond a certain point
- For certain

UPSC-ready sentence: Certain policy interventions may produce different outcomes depending on regional institutions and socioeconomic conditions.

Memory hook: What has been distinguished and settled can become certain.

## 30. ASCERTAIN

Part of speech: Verb

### Meaning of the Components

The word developed through French from certain, historically associated with the family of Latin cernere.

### Literal Meaning

To establish something as certain.

### Semantic Evolution

The older sense of making something certain developed into the modern sense of finding out or determining something through inquiry or investigation.

### Modern Meaning

To discover or establish a fact with confidence, usually through evidence or investigation.

### Common Usage

- Ascertain the facts
- Ascertain the cause
- Ascertain eligibility

UPSC-ready sentence: An independent investigation is necessary to ascertain the causes of the incident and identify accountability gaps.

Memory hook: To ascertain is to make a matter certain through investigation.

## 31. CRIME

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

Crime comes from Latin crimen, meaning an accusation, charge, or judgement. Its deeper history is associated with the family of cernere, “to distinguish or decide.”

### Literal Meaning

Historically, an accusation or charge.

### Semantic Evolution

The meaning shifted from accusation or judicial judgement toward an act regarded as an offence under law.

### Modern Meaning

An act or omission that violates criminal law and is subject to legal punishment.

### Common Usage

- Organised crime
- Cybercrime
- Crime prevention

UPSC-ready sentence: Effective crime prevention requires reliable investigation, accessible justice, institutional coordination, and protection of citizens' rights.

Memory hook: The connection is historical: crime was associated with accusation and judgement, not with the modern idea of “waste removal.”

## 32. CRIMINAL

Part of speech: Noun / adjective

### Meaning of the Components

- Crimin- = from Latin crimen, “accusation or charge.”
- -al = relating to or characterised by.

### Literal Meaning

Relating to a crime or accusation.

### Semantic Evolution

The word developed to describe offences under criminal law and people who commit them.

### Modern Meaning

As an adjective, relating to crime or criminal law; as a noun, a person who has committed a crime.

### Common Usage

- Criminal justice system
- Criminal liability
- Criminal investigation

UPSC-ready sentence: A fair criminal justice system must balance effective law enforcement with due process, access to legal representation, and protection against arbitrary detention.

Memory hook: Crime names the offence; criminal describes something or someone associated with it.

## 33. INCRIMINATE

Part of speech: Verb

### Meaning of the Components

- In- = in or into.
- Crimin- = the historical stem associated with crime and Latin crimen.
- -ate = a verb-forming ending.

### Literal Meaning

To bring someone into an accusation or charge.

### Semantic Evolution

The term developed into the act of making someone appear implicated in an offence or providing evidence that points to guilt.

### Modern Meaning

To suggest or establish that someone is involved in a crime.

### Common Usage

- Incriminating evidence
- Self-incrimination
- Incriminate a suspect

UPSC-ready sentence: The right against compelled self-incrimination is an important safeguard against coercive investigative practices.

Memory hook: To incriminate is to connect a person with an alleged crime.

### A. Meaning of the Components

Unlike words formed from transparent Latin or Greek elements, graze is an inherited Germanic word, not a modern combination of separable prefixes and suffixes.

Its history is straightforward:

- Old English: grasian — to feed on grass.
- Old English noun: græs — grass.
- Modern English: graze — to feed on grass or, in another sense, to touch or scrape lightly.

Thus, the original meaning of graze is directly connected with grass.&#x20;

### B. Literal Meaning

Graze = to feed on growing grass or other low vegetation.

Example: Cattle graze in a pasture.

The verb has a second common meaning: to touch or scrape something lightly while passing.

Example: The bullet grazed his arm.

### C. Semantic Evolution

Stage 1: Feeding on grass

The original sense referred to animals feeding on grass or herbage.

Stage 2: Light contact or scraping

By around the early modern period, graze also came to mean touching or scraping a surface lightly in passing. The precise link between the two senses is uncertain.

Stage 3: Repeated small-scale eating

The feeding sense extended informally to people who eat small amounts of food repeatedly throughout the day.

### D. Modern Meaning

Graze (verb):

1. Agriculture: To feed on grass or to put livestock out to feed.
2. Physical contact: To touch or scrape a surface lightly while passing.
3. Eating habits: To eat small amounts of food repeatedly rather than consume meals at set times.

Graze (noun): A light scrape or abrasion; informally, an occasion of grazing or a small amount of food.

# NUISANCE: An Etymological Word-Power Study

Following the vocabulary-building approach of Norman Lewis’s Word Power Made Easy

## 1. NUISANCE

# NUISANCE

Noun · Pronunciation: /ˈnjuː.səns/ (British), /ˈnuː.səns/ (American)

Core idea: Something that causes annoyance, inconvenience, harm, or interference.

UPSC relevance: Environmental pollution, public health, urban governance, public nuisance, and tort law.

### A. Meaning of the Components

Nuisance is not formed from a modern English prefix and root. It entered English through Anglo-French nusaunce and Old French nuisance, meaning “harm, damage, or injury.” These forms came from nuire, “to harm,” which derives from Latin nocēre, “to hurt.”&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.etymonline.com\&sz=32)

Etymology, Origin & Meaning

+1



- Latin nocēre = to hurt or harm.
- Old French nuire = to harm.
- Nuisance = originally, harm or injury; later, something that causes annoyance or interferes with others.

Memory key: Nuisance originally meant harm; its meaning gradually softened to include everyday annoyance and inconvenience.

### B. Literal Meaning

Nuisance = a source of harm, trouble, annoyance, or interference.

Examples:

- Persistent loud noise can be a nuisance to residents.
- An obstructed footpath may create a public nuisance.
- A leaking drain can become a nuisance and a public-health risk.

### C. Semantic Evolution

Stage 1: Harm or injury

Around the 14th century, the word referred to injury, damage, or harm.

Stage 2: Interference with others' rights

The term acquired a legal sense for conditions or activities that harm others or interfere with their use of property and shared spaces.

Stage 3: Annoyance or inconvenience

The meaning broadened to include unpleasant smells, excessive noise, pests, eyesores, and irritating people or situations.

### D. Modern Meaning

Nuisance (noun):

1. Everyday use: A person or thing that causes annoyance or inconvenience.
2. Environmental use: A condition, such as noise, smoke, odour, or waste, that interferes with people's comfort or wellbeing.
3. Legal use: An activity or condition that unlawfully interferes with public rights or another person's use and enjoyment of property.

The legal meaning is more specific than the everyday one: something annoying is not automatically a legally actionable nuisance.

### E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

| Collocation            | Meaning and application                                                                     |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Public nuisance        | An act or condition that interferes with rights or interests shared by the public.          |
| Noise nuisance         | Noise that unreasonably disturbs people's comfort, health, or normal activities.            |
| Environmental nuisance | An unpleasant or harmful environmental condition, such as excessive smoke, odour, or waste. |

UPSC-ready sentence: Effective urban governance requires addressing public nuisances such as excessive noise, waste accumulation, and blocked public pathways through proportionate enforcement and better municipal services.

Memory hook: A nuisance is more than a mere inconvenience when it causes significant harm or interferes with others' rights.

# Part I. Words Meaning “Harmful”

## 3. NOXIOUS

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

Noxious comes from Latin noxius, meaning “harmful” or “injurious,” which is related to nocēre, “to harm.”

- Nox- / noxi- = associated with harm or injury in this historical word family.
- -ous = characterised by or full of.

This is a historical breakdown, not a modern English construction that can be freely applied to other words.

### Literal Meaning

Harmful or injurious.

### Semantic Evolution

The word developed from the general idea of causing harm to a term used especially for substances, fumes, conditions, or influences that can damage health or the environment.

### Modern Meaning

Harmful, poisonous, or otherwise injurious to living beings or the environment.

### Common Usage

- Noxious gases
- Noxious substances
- Noxious emissions

UPSC-ready sentence: Strict monitoring of industrial emissions is essential to prevent the release of noxious substances that threaten public health and environmental quality.

Memory hook: A noxious substance causes harm; a nuisance causes trouble or interference.

## 4. NOXIOUSNESS

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Noxious = harmful.
- -ness = a suffix that forms a noun expressing a quality or state.

### Literal Meaning

The quality of being harmful.

### Semantic Evolution

The adjective noxious developed into a noun naming the condition or degree of harmfulness.

### Modern Meaning

The state or quality of being noxious.

### Common Usage

- Noxiousness of industrial emissions
- Assessment of chemical hazards
- Noxiousness to aquatic life

UPSC-ready sentence: Environmental risk assessment must consider the toxicity and noxiousness of pollutants as well as the duration and extent of exposure.

Memory hook: Noxious describes the substance; noxiousness names its harmful quality.

## 5. NOCUOUS

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

Nocuous derives from Latin nocuus, meaning harmful, from nocēre.

- Nocu- = harmful in this historical formation.
- -ous = characterised by.

### Literal Meaning

Harmful or injurious.

### Semantic Evolution

The term retained the sense of causing harm but became much less common than noxious in modern English.

### Modern Meaning

Harmful; the opposite of innocuous.

### Common Usage

- Nocuous substance
- Potentially nocuous effects
- Nocuous exposure

UPSC-ready sentence: Risk assessments should distinguish harmless exposure from exposure to potentially nocuous substances.

Memory hook: Nocuous and noxious are close in meaning, but noxious is far more common.

Vocabulary note: Learn nocuous mainly for recognition; prefer harmful, toxic, or noxious in most formal writing.

## 6. OBNOXIOUS

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

The word comes from Latin obnoxius, meaning exposed or liable to harm, and later developed the sense of harmful or offensive.

- Ob- = a Latin element with meanings including “toward” or “subject to,” depending on the formation.
- Noxious / noxius = harmful.

The historical components should not be interpreted as a simple modern English formula.

### Literal Meaning

Originally, exposed or liable to harm.

### Semantic Evolution

The meaning shifted from being liable or subject to harm toward being harmful, offensive, disagreeable, or highly unpleasant.

### Modern Meaning

Extremely unpleasant, offensive, or objectionable in behaviour, attitude, or effect.

### Common Usage

- Obnoxious behaviour
- Obnoxious remarks
- An obnoxious odour

UPSC-ready sentence: Obnoxious public behaviour can undermine civic cooperation, but administrative action should remain proportionate and consistent with the law.

Memory hook: A noxious substance is harmful; an obnoxious person or behaviour is offensive or extremely unpleasant.

## 7. OBNOXIOUSNESS

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Obnoxious = offensive or extremely unpleasant.
- -ness = a suffix expressing a quality or state.

### Literal Meaning

The quality of being obnoxious.

### Semantic Evolution

The noun developed to refer to the degree or nature of behaviour, a condition, or an object that causes offence or strong dislike.

### Modern Meaning

The quality of being highly unpleasant, offensive, or objectionable.

### Common Usage

- The obnoxiousness of the behaviour
- Obnoxiousness of the remarks
- Socially offensive conduct

UPSC-ready sentence: Public officials should address disruptive conduct through fair procedures rather than allowing personal perceptions of obnoxiousness to determine enforcement.

Memory hook: Obnoxious describes; obnoxiousness names the quality.

## 10. INNOCENT

Part of speech: Adjective / noun

### Meaning of the Components

The word comes from Latin innocens, meaning “not harming” or “harmless,” formed from the negative prefix in- and a form of nocēre, “to harm.”

- In- = not.
- Nocent- = harming or injurious in the historical formation.

### Literal Meaning

Not causing harm.

### Semantic Evolution

The meaning broadened from being harmless to being free from guilt, wrongdoing, or blame. It also came to describe someone who lacks experience or sophistication in a particular matter.

### Modern Meaning

1. Not guilty of a particular offence.
2. Not intending or causing harm.
3. Naïve or inexperienced in a particular context.

### Common Usage

- Presumption of innocence
- Innocent civilians
- An innocent mistake

UPSC-ready sentence: The presumption of innocence is a fundamental principle of criminal justice and requires that guilt be established through a fair legal process.

Memory hook: The original idea is “not harming”; the legal sense is “not guilty.”

## 11. INNOCENCE

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Innocent = not guilty or not harmful.
- -ence = a noun-forming suffix expressing a state or quality.

### Literal Meaning

The state of being harmless or free from guilt.

### Semantic Evolution

The term expanded from harmlessness to moral purity, absence of guilt, and the legal status of not having been proved guilty.

### Modern Meaning

The state of being free from guilt or wrongdoing; in some contexts, simplicity or lack of experience.

### Common Usage

- Presumption of innocence
- Proof of innocence
- Loss of innocence

Memory hook: Innocent describes a person or act; innocence names the state or quality.

## 12. PERNICIOUS

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

Pernicious comes from Latin perniciosus, associated with pernicies, meaning destruction or ruin. It belongs to a related Latin historical family associated with harm and destruction, though it is not a direct derivative of the English word nuisance.&#x20;

+1

Destructive or ruinous.

### Semantic Evolution

The sense of causing ruin developed into a description of influences that cause serious, often gradual or difficult-to-detect harm.

### Common Usage

- Pernicious influence
- Pernicious consequences
- Pernicious social practices

## 13. INTERNECINE

Part of speech: Adjective

### Meaning of the Components

Internecine comes from Latin internecinus, associated with internecare, “to kill or destroy completely.”

- Inter- = between or among in many Latin formations.
- The rest of the word comes from the Latin family associated with killing or destruction.

Its modern meaning is not simply “between nations”; that is a common misconception.

### Literal Meaning

Historically, involving mutual destruction or slaughter.

### Semantic Evolution

The word developed from the idea of mutual killing to describe intense conflict in which opposing sides destroy one another or suffer heavy losses.

### Modern Meaning

Relating to conflict within a group or between opposing sides that causes severe mutual harm or destruction.

### Common Usage

- Internecine conflict
- Internecine warfare
- Internecine rivalries

UPSC-ready sentence: Internecine factional conflict within political organisations can weaken institutional cohesion and divert attention from governance priorities.

Memory hook: Internecine suggests destructive conflict among competing sides.

Usage caution: It does not simply mean “international” or “between countries.”

## 14. NOCICEPTION

Part of speech: Noun

### Meaning of the Components

- Noci- = from Latin nocēre, “to harm.”
- -ception = related to reception and the idea of receiving or detecting.

The term combines a Latin-derived element with a component associated with receiving or detecting stimuli.&#x20;

Etymology, Origin & Meaning

+1



### Literal Meaning

Detection of potentially harmful stimuli.

### Semantic Evolution

The term was developed in physiology and neuroscience to distinguish the neural processing of potentially tissue-damaging stimuli from the broader experience of pain.

### Modern Meaning

The nervous system's detection and processing of potentially harmful stimuli, such as extreme heat, damaging pressure, or certain chemical signals.

### Common Usage

- Nociceptive pathways

Scientific distinction: Nociception and pain are related but not identical concepts.
