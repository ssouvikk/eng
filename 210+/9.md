# WORD POWER MADE EASY: PICKETING

## 1. PICKETING — Organised public protest outside a workplace or institution

# PICKETING

Noun / verb form · Labour relations, industrial disputes, collective action

Core memory idea: Picketing means stationing people at a workplace, entrance, or other location to communicate a protest, persuade people not to enter, or draw public attention to a dispute.

### A. Meaning of the Components

The word picketing belongs to the family picket → picket + -ing → picketing.

| Component | Meaning                                                                            |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Picket    | A person stationed at a place to observe, guard, or participate in a protest       |
| -ing      | A suffix that can form the name of an activity or the present participle of a verb |
| Picketing | The activity of stationing pickets, particularly as a form of protest              |

The word picket has a history connected with a pointed stake or post. Its military use developed to describe a person or small group posted to keep watch. The labour-protest sense developed later.

Memory hook: Imagine workers standing outside a factory gate holding placards. They are not necessarily blocking the entrance; they are making their protest visible. That activity is picketing.

### B. Literal Meaning

The historical image behind picket is a stake or post used to mark, secure, or guard a position.

The word later came to refer to a person stationed at a particular point, and then to organised groups stationed outside workplaces or institutions to protest.

Thus, the memory sequence is:

Stake or post → person stationed at a position → organised protest outside an establishment → picketing.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The development of the word can be remembered in four stages:

1. Physical stake: A pointed post used for securing or marking a position.
2. Military watch: A soldier or small group stationed to observe or guard a position.
3. Stationed personnel: People positioned at a particular place for a specific purpose.
4. Labour protest: People stationed outside a workplace to publicise a dispute, persuade others not to cross a picket line, or encourage solidarity with workers.

The modern labour-relations meaning is especially important in discussions of industrial disputes, trade unions, collective bargaining, and freedom of association.

### D. Modern Meaning

Picketing (noun or verb form)

The act of stationing people at a workplace, entrance, or another location to protest, communicate demands, persuade others not to enter, or draw attention to a dispute.

Picketing is commonly associated with strikes and labour movements, but it can also occur during protests involving businesses, government offices, educational institutions, and other organisations.

Important distinction: Picketing does not automatically mean blocking access or using intimidation. It may be peaceful and informational. Its legality depends on the applicable law and the conduct involved.

### E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

1\. Peaceful picketing — protest by stationed demonstrators without violence or intimidation. Example: Peaceful picketing is one form of collective action through which workers express their grievances.

2\. Picket line — the line or group of protesters positioned outside a workplace or establishment. Example: During the strike, several employees joined the picket line to demand safer working conditions.

3\. Picketing during a strike — the use of pickets to publicise demands or encourage solidarity during a work stoppage. Example: Picketing during a strike can increase public awareness of a labour dispute, provided it does not involve unlawful obstruction or coercion.

UPSC writing tip: Use picketing in answers concerning labour rights, industrial relations, collective bargaining, trade unions, freedom of association, and the balance between protest and public order.

## 2. The PICKET word family

The central word is picket. Its history is associated with French piquet, meaning a pointed stake or post, related to piquer, “to prick” or “pierce.” The word subsequently acquired military, security, and labour-protest meanings.

The family is useful because it connects three areas: physical objects, guarding or surveillance, and organised protest.

Question: How can industrial disputes be managed while safeguarding workers' rights and public order?

Industrial disputes should be addressed through effective collective bargaining, timely conciliation, and accessible grievance-redressal mechanisms. # WORD POWER MADE EASY: STEAMSHIP

## 1. STEAMSHIP — A vessel propelled by steam power

# STEAMSHIP

Noun · Maritime history, transport, industrialisation, global trade

Core memory idea: A steamship is a ship propelled by an engine that uses steam power. It represents the technological shift from wind-dependent sailing vessels to mechanically powered maritime transport.

### A. Meaning of the Components

Steamship is a transparent English compound formed from two words.

| Component | Meaning                                                                       |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Steam     | Water vapour, especially when used under pressure to produce mechanical power |
| Ship      | A large vessel used for transporting people or goods by water                 |
| Steamship | A ship propelled by steam power                                               |

Memory hook: Steam + ship = a ship whose movement is powered by steam.

The word is a compound rather than a word formed from a classical Latin or Greek root. Its family is therefore best understood through the component words steam and ship, together with the historically related vocabulary of steam-powered technology.

### B. Literal Meaning

The literal meaning is “a ship powered by steam.”

Early steamships used steam engines to convert thermal energy into mechanical movement. Depending on the design, this movement turned paddle wheels or a propeller, enabling the vessel to move without relying entirely on wind.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The development of the word can be remembered in four stages:

1. Steam as a natural phenomenon: Water vapour produced by heating.
2. Steam as a source of power: Steam engines convert heat into mechanical energy.
3. Steam-powered navigation: Engines propel vessels using paddle wheels or propellers.
4. Steamship as a historical transport category: The word becomes associated with industrialisation, ocean-going trade, passenger transport, migration, and imperial-era maritime networks.

Steamships helped make maritime transport more predictable than sailing alone, although their performance still depended on fuel supplies, weather, port facilities, and technological limitations.

### D. Modern Meaning

Steamship (noun)

A vessel propelled by a steam engine, generally using steam generated by heating water.

Steamships were especially important during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Many have since been replaced by diesel-powered vessels, although steam-powered ships remain significant in maritime history and heritage.

Important distinction: A steamship is not simply any ship that uses an engine. A modern diesel-powered cargo ship is mechanically powered, but it is not ordinarily called a steamship.

### E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

1\. Steamship technology — the technology used to propel vessels with steam engines. Example: Steamship technology transformed maritime transport by reducing dependence on favourable winds.

2\. Steamship routes — established routes served by steam-powered vessels. Example: The expansion of steamship routes strengthened commercial links between distant ports.

3\. Steamship trade — commercial activity carried out using steam-powered vessels. Example: Steamship trade contributed to the expansion of international commerce and the integration of regional markets.

UPSC writing tip: Use steamship in answers on the Industrial Revolution, colonial expansion, maritime trade, transport technology, and the historical integration of global markets.

## 5. Model UPSC paragraph

Question: Explain how technological developments in transport transformed the world economy.

The development of steam engines revolutionised transport during the Industrial Revolution. Steamships reduced dependence on favourable winds and made more regular maritime services possible. Together with railway expansion, improvements in port infrastructure, and advances in industrial production, steam-powered transport strengthened commercial connections between distant regions. These changes contributed to the expansion of international trade, the movement of migrants, and the integration of markets. However, the same transport networks also facilitated imperial expansion and the extraction of colonial resources. Thus, steamship technology was both an instrument of economic transformation and a significant feature of the unequal global relationships of the industrial age.

# WORD POWER MADE EASY: SPRINKLE

## 1. SPRINKLE — To scatter small drops or particles over a surface

# SPRINKLE

Verb and noun · Distribution, environment, agriculture, everyday formal English

Core memory idea: To sprinkle is to distribute something in small quantities over an area—like sprinkling water on dry soil or sprinkling a few facts throughout an essay.

### A. Meaning of the Components

Sprinkle is an old English verb, historically related to Middle Dutch sprenkelen, meaning to sprinkle or scatter. Unlike a transparent compound such as steamship, it cannot be reliably divided into smaller modern English components with independent meanings.

For learning purposes, focus on the central image: small quantities scattered across a surface or distributed throughout something.

### B. Literal Meaning

To scatter or distribute small drops, particles, or pieces over a surface.

### C. Semantic Evolution

2. Light or sparse distribution: Something is spread in small amounts across a larger area.

### D. Modern Meaning

1. To scatter small drops or particles over a surface.
2. To distribute small quantities of something throughout an area or larger whole.

1\. Sprinkle water on the soil — distribute small quantities of water over the soil. Example: In dry regions, carefully sprinkling water on young plants may help during early growth, although irrigation requirements depend on local conditions.

3\. Sprinkle references throughout an essay — insert references or examples at intervals. Example: A strong UPSC essay should integrate relevant constitutional provisions and examples naturally rather than merely sprinkling references throughout the answer.

Writing tip: Sprinkle is useful in descriptive writing, but in technical or policy-oriented UPSC answers, more precise verbs such as distribute, disperse, apply, or incorporate may be preferable.

# WORD POWER MADE EASY: TUSSLE

## 1. TUSSLE — A vigorous struggle or dispute

# TUSSLE

Noun and verb · Conflict, competition, politics, governance

Core memory idea: A tussle is a struggle in which two or more sides contend with one another. It may be physical, political, institutional, or metaphorical.

### A. Meaning of the Components

Tussle is a single English word, not a transparent compound that can be divided into meaningful modern components.

Its precise origin is uncertain, although it is generally associated with older English dialectal forms meaning to struggle or wrestle. Therefore, it is best learned through its central image rather than through a speculative root breakdown.

Memory hook: Imagine two people wrestling for control of the same object. Neither yields easily. Their struggle is a tussle.

### B. Literal Meaning

The central idea is a vigorous struggle, especially one involving close physical contact or competing efforts.

The word is commonly used metaphorically as well: political parties tussle for power, institutions tussle over jurisdiction, and governments tussle with difficult policy problems.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The meaning can be remembered in three stages:

1. Physical struggle: Wrestling, grappling, or struggling with another person.
2. Competitive contest: A struggle between people or groups seeking the same outcome.
3. Metaphorical conflict: A contest over power, authority, resources, influence, or competing priorities.

The physical image remains useful even in political and administrative contexts: two sides are struggling to gain an advantage.

### D. Modern Meaning

Tussle (noun)

A vigorous struggle, contest, or dispute between opposing sides.

Tussle (verb)

To struggle or grapple physically, or to contend with someone or something over an issue.

The word is often less formal than conflict or confrontation, but it is especially useful in journalism and analytical writing because it conveys an ongoing contest.

### E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

1\. A tussle for power — a struggle between people, parties, or institutions seeking control. Example: A prolonged tussle for power between political factions can weaken institutional stability.

2\. A tussle over jurisdiction — a dispute about which institution or authority has the legal power to act. Example: A tussle over jurisdiction between different levels of government can delay policy implementation.

3\. A tussle between competing interests — a contest between groups or priorities seeking different outcomes. Example: Environmental policymaking often involves a tussle between economic development and ecological protection.

UPSC writing tip: Tussle is particularly useful in discussions of federalism, institutional relations, political competition, and resource allocation. In highly formal legal analysis, dispute, conflict, or jurisdictional disagreement may be more precise.

## 2. The TUSSLE word family

# WORD POWER MADE EASY: REDUNDANT

## 1. REDUNDANT — Unnecessary because it is excessive, repetitive, or no longer needed

# REDUNDANT

Adjective · Governance, administration, economics, logic, communication

Core memory idea: Something is redundant when it is no longer needed, adds unnecessary repetition, or performs a function already adequately served by something else.

### A. Meaning of the Components

Redundant comes through Latin redundare, meaning “to overflow” or “to surge back,” formed from re- (“back”) and undare (“to surge in waves”), related to unda (“wave”).

| Component         | Meaning                                                   |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Latin re-         | Back; again                                               |
| Latin unda        | Wave                                                      |
| Latin redundare   | To overflow or surge back                                 |
| English redundant | Excessive, superfluous, repetitive, or no longer required |

Memory hook: Imagine water overflowing a container. There is more than the container can usefully hold. Likewise, redundant information, procedures, or resources exceed what is necessary.

Important: This is a historical derivation, not a literal breakdown of the modern English adjective into a productive prefix and root.

### B. Literal Meaning

The older Latin image is “to overflow” or “to surge back.”

The modern adjective does not usually refer to water. It describes something that exceeds what is necessary or duplicates an existing function.

For example:

- A sentence repeats an idea already expressed.
- Two administrative procedures perform the same task without adding value.
- A worker's position is no longer required because an organisation has restructured.

Each situation involves something that is excessive, duplicated, or no longer needed.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The development can be remembered in four stages:

1. Overflow: Something exceeds the available capacity.
2. Excess: More of something exists than is required.
3. Duplication: An additional element repeats or duplicates what already exists.
4. Superfluity or loss of necessity: Something is unnecessary, or a role is no longer required.

The word now has several related but distinct uses: redundant language, redundant procedures, redundant systems, and redundant employees.

### D. Modern Meaning

Redundant (adjective)

1. Unnecessary or superfluous: Not needed because it adds little or no value.
2. Repetitive or duplicative: Expressing the same idea more than once.
3. No longer required: Particularly in employment, when a position becomes unnecessary because of organisational or economic changes.
4. Duplicated for reliability: In engineering and computing, a redundant component may provide backup capacity if another component fails. Here, redundancy can be intentional and beneficial.

Crucial distinction: In ordinary writing, redundant often means unnecessary. In engineering, redundancy may mean deliberate duplication that improves reliability.

### E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

2\. Redundant information — information that repeats what is already known or stated. Example: A concise policy brief removes redundant information and highlights the evidence relevant to the decision.

3\. Redundant workforce — employees whose roles are no longer required, often following restructuring or technological change. Example: Automation may create a need to retrain workers whose existing roles become redundant.

UPSC writing tip: In governance answers, redundant procedures and redundant regulations can help explain administrative inefficiency. In employment-related answers, use redundant positions carefully, because it concerns livelihoods and organisational restructuring.

# WORD POWER MADE EASY: HARBINGER

## 1. HARBINGER — A sign or forerunner of something that is about to happen

# HARBINGER

Noun · Prediction, change, history, governance, environment

Core memory idea: A harbinger is something that arrives or appears before another event and signals its coming. It may be a warning of danger, a sign of change, or an indication of something favourable.

### A. Meaning of the Components

Harbinger entered English through Anglo-French and Middle English forms associated with a person who provided lodging or prepared accommodation for someone arriving later.

It is not a modern English compound that can be reliably divided into smaller meaningful components. Its historical image, however, is an excellent memory aid.

- Historical image: A person goes ahead of an important traveller to arrange lodging.
- Extended idea: Someone or something arrives before another person or event.
- Modern figurative meaning: A sign or indication that something is approaching.

Memory hook: Imagine an advance messenger arriving before a king's procession. The messenger announces what is coming. A harbinger similarly signals a future event.

### B. Literal Meaning

Historically, a harbinger was a person who went ahead to arrange lodging or prepare for the arrival of others.

This older sense developed into the figurative meaning of a forerunner or sign of something that follows.

The modern word usually refers to a sign, event, or development rather than to a person arranging accommodation.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The development can be remembered in four stages:

1. Advance preparation: Someone goes ahead of a traveller or group.
2. Forerunner: The person or thing arrives before what is to follow.
3. Announcement: The forerunner signals the arrival of something else.
4. Prediction by indication: An event or condition is interpreted as a sign of a coming change.

For example, an early rise in sea-surface temperatures may be a harbinger of an unusually intense marine heatwave, while the first signs of political instability may be a harbinger of a wider crisis.

Important distinction: A harbinger is an indicator, not proof that a future event will occur. The strength of the implication depends on the evidence and context.

### D. Modern Meaning

Harbinger (noun)

A person, event, development, or phenomenon that signals or foreshadows the arrival of something else.

It is often used in formal writing and journalism. The event being signalled may be favourable, unfavourable, or neutral.

Examples:

- Early warning signs may be a harbinger of economic distress.
- A successful pilot project may be a harbinger of wider administrative reform.
- Unusual migration patterns may be a harbinger of environmental change.

### E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

1\. A harbinger of change — an early sign that a significant transformation may occur. Example: The growing adoption of digital public infrastructure may be a harbinger of changes in how citizens access government services.

2\. A harbinger of crisis — a warning sign that a serious problem may be approaching. Example: Persistent fiscal stress can be a harbinger of a broader economic crisis if corrective measures are delayed.

3\. A harbinger of hope — a sign that a favourable development may follow. Example: Improved learning outcomes in disadvantaged districts may be a harbinger of more equitable educational progress.

UPSC writing tip: Harbinger is particularly useful in essays and analytical answers on climate change, economic indicators, social transformation, geopolitical developments, and institutional reform.

## 2. The HARBINGER word family

The direct family of harbinger is relatively small. Its historical origin is associated with words for lodging, shelter, and preparing for someone's arrival. The English word harbour belongs to a related historical word family.

For vocabulary building, distinguish two groups:

- Historical word family: harbinger, harbour, harbouring, harbourer, harbourage.
- Semantic family of prediction: forerunner, precursor, portent, omen, presage, and foreshadow.

The second group is especially useful for UPSC, but these words are not all derived from the same root.

### The crucial distinctions

- Harbinger vs. precursor: A harbinger signals what is coming; a precursor often precedes something as an earlier stage or contributing condition.
- Harbinger vs. portent: Portent often carries a stronger ominous or foreboding tone.
- Harbinger vs. omen: Omen can suggest a superstitious or subjective interpretation; harbinger is often more natural for evidence-based analysis.
- Harbinger vs. forerunner: A forerunner may simply come first, without necessarily signalling what follows.
- Presage vs. foreshadow: Both can be verbs meaning to indicate something beforehand; presage is more formal and often suggests prediction.

### B. Literal Meaning

The historical meaning is “the act or state of pressing or crowding together.”

Over time, the word acquired a specific medical sense: difficulty evacuating the bowels.

### C. Semantic Evolution

1. Packing tightly: The Latin verb constipare meant to press or crowd together.
2. Obstruction or compression: The idea was applied to bodily conditions involving constriction or difficulty of passage.
3. Medical specialisation: Constipation came to refer to difficult or infrequent bowel movements.
4. Modern clinical usage: The term describes a symptom pattern, rather than one single disease or cause.&#x20;

### D. Modern Meaning

Constipation (noun)

A condition in which bowel movements are less frequent than usual or difficult to pass; stools may be hard, dry, or difficult to evacuate. The person's usual bowel pattern matters, so constipation is not defined solely by how often someone has a bowel movement.&#x20;

2\. Relieve constipation — reduce or ease the condition. Example: Advice on relieving constipation should be based on reliable health guidance and the person's circumstances.

## 2. The CONSTIPATION word family

The direct family is small, because constipation is a specialised medical term. Its most useful forms are constipate, constipated, constipating, and constipation. The related historical word costive is now uncommon.

The family comes from Latin constipare, “to press or crowd together,” formed from con- (“together”) and stipare (“to pack tightly”).&#x20;

## 3. Semantic companions: obstruction, congestion, and compression

These words are useful for expanding vocabulary, but they are not members of the direct CONSTIPATION word family.

### 7. OBSTRUCTION

Part of speech: Noun

Meaning of the Components

From Latin obstruere, meaning to block or build up against.

- Ob- — against or in the way.
- Struere — to build or arrange.

Literal Meaning

The act or result of blocking a passage.

Semantic Evolution

The physical sense of blocking a path developed into broader uses involving barriers to movement, progress, or action.

Modern Meaning

Something that blocks or impedes passage, movement, or progress.

Common Usage

- Intestinal obstruction
- Administrative obstruction
- Obstruction of justice

UPSC application: Administrative obstruction can delay public-service delivery, while legal obstruction of justice undermines the functioning of institutions.

Retention cue: Obstruction means blockage; constipation is a specific bowel condition.

### 8. CONGESTION

Part of speech: Noun

Meaning of the Components

From Latin congerere, meaning to gather or heap together.

- Con- — together.
- Gerere — to carry or bring.

Literal Meaning

A gathering or accumulation in one place.

Semantic Evolution

The idea of accumulation developed into the modern senses of overcrowding, traffic buildup, or an excessive accumulation of fluid in bodily tissues.

Modern Meaning

An excessive accumulation or overcrowding that impedes normal movement or function.

Common Usage

- Urban traffic congestion
- Nasal congestion
- Congestion in transport networks

UPSC application: Urban traffic congestion increases travel time, fuel consumption, air pollution, and the economic costs of commuting.

Retention cue: Congestion means overcrowding or accumulation; it is not synonymous with constipation.

### 9. COMPRESSION

Part of speech: Noun

Meaning of the Components

From Latin comprimere, meaning to press together.

- Com- — together.
- Premere — to press.

Literal Meaning

The act of pressing something into a smaller space.

Semantic Evolution

The physical act of pressing developed into technical meanings in engineering, physics, computing, and medicine.

Modern Meaning

The process of reducing volume or size by applying pressure, or of reducing the size of digital data.

Common Usage

- Data compression
- Compression of air
- Spinal cord compression

UPSC application: Data compression can reduce storage requirements and improve the efficiency of digital public infrastructure.

Retention cue: Compression means pressing or reducing; it shares a broad idea of pressure with the historical image behind constipation, but not the same immediate word formation.

## 5. Model public-health paragraph

Topic: The importance of health literacy

Public-health literacy enables people to recognise common symptoms, understand reliable health information, and seek appropriate care when necessary. Persistent constipation should not automatically be attributed to one cause, because diet, physical activity, medication, and underlying conditions may all play a role. Clear communication can help people distinguish a common symptom from warning signs that require prompt assessment. Health education should therefore combine accessible information with appropriate clinical guidance rather than rely on self-diagnosis.
