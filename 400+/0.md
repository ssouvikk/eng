## **1. DIABOLICAL**

**Pronunciation:** /ˌdaɪ.əˈbɒl.ɪ.kəl/\
**Part of Speech:** Adjective\
**Word Family:** Diabolical – Diabolically – Diabolism – Diabolist

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

The word *diabolical* ultimately comes from the Greek word *diabolos* (διάβολος).

Break it down historically:

| Component  | Meaning                                                  |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| *Dia-*     | Across, through                                          |
| *Ballein*  | To throw                                                 |
| *Diabolos* | One who throws something across; an accuser or slanderer |

The Greek *diabolos* came to denote the Devil, particularly through its use in the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible.

**Memory hook:** Imagine someone throwing accusations across a room, creating division and discord. The image of the malicious accuser eventually becomes associated with the Devil.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

**Diabolical = Devil-like; characteristic of the Devil.**

In its original religious and moral sense, it describes something associated with the Devil, evil, wickedness or malevolence.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word underwent the following semantic development:

**Greek: Diabolos**

Accuser, slanderer, one who causes division.

**Religious association**

The Devil; embodiment of evil.

**Moral extension**

Extremely wicked, cruel, malicious or morally depraved.

**Modern usage**

Extremely evil, outrageously cruel or fiendishly ingenious.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Diabolical (adjective):**

1. Extremely evil, wicked or cruel; morally reprehensible.
2. Characterised by deliberate malice, deception or destructive intent.
3. Extremely cunning, ingenious or devilishly clever (sometimes used figuratively or humorously).

**Important distinction:** Diabolical is stronger than merely *bad*, *unethical* or *immoral*. It conveys an exceptional degree of wickedness or malevolence.

### **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

| Collocation        | Meaning                                            |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Diabolical scheme  | An exceptionally wicked or malicious plan          |
| Diabolical cruelty | Extreme and inhuman cruelty                        |
| Diabolical design  | A deliberately destructive or sinister arrangement |

**UPSC-oriented examples:**

1. The systematic persecution of vulnerable communities reflects the diabolical nature of extremist ideologies.
2. Human trafficking represents a diabolical exploitation of human vulnerability and dignity.
3. The deliberate weaponisation of misinformation can become a diabolical instrument of social polarisation.

**Synonyms:** Fiendish, nefarious, malevolent, heinous, sinister, wicked.

**Antonyms:** Benevolent, virtuous, humane, altruistic, philanthropic.

## **2. The Etymological Word Family of DIABOLICAL**

To build a strong vocabulary, distinguish two levels of word relationships:

- **Core family:** Words derived from *diabolos* or its descendants, retaining the association with the Devil, wickedness or malicious behaviour.
- **Extended root family:** Words derived from the Greek verb *ballein* (to throw), which forms part of *diabolos*. These words share the deeper linguistic root but have developed entirely different meanings.

This distinction is important: sharing a root does not necessarily mean sharing a modern meaning.

# **PART I — CORE FAMILY: DIABOLOS (THE DEVIL / ACCUSER)**

## **1. DEVIL**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- Greek: *Diabolos* = accuser, slanderer.
- Latin: *Diabolus* = the Devil.
- Old English: *Dēofol* = the Devil.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The Devil; the embodiment or personification of evil.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The Greek term for an accuser or slanderer acquired a religious meaning as the adversary of God. Through Latin and Germanic transmission, it entered English as *devil*.

Its meaning subsequently expanded to describe a wicked, cruel or mischievous person.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A supernatural embodiment of evil; figuratively, an extremely wicked or cruel person.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **The devil's advocate** – A person who deliberately argues against an established position to test its validity.
2. **Between the devil and the deep blue sea** – Caught between two equally undesirable alternatives.
3. **A devil of a problem** – An extremely difficult problem.

**UPSC application:** Playing devil's advocate during policy deliberations can expose weaknesses in an otherwise popular proposal.

## **2. DEVILISH**

**Part of Speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Devil* = embodiment of evil.
- *-ish* = resembling, characteristic of or having the qualities of something.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Having the characteristics of the Devil.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

Originally associated with wickedness and evil, the word gradually acquired figurative meanings such as mischievous, cunning, troublesome and exceptionally difficult.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

1. Extremely wicked, cruel or malicious.
2. Mischievous, cunning or playfully troublesome.
3. Exceptionally difficult or challenging.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Devilish intentions** – Malicious intentions.
2. **Devilish cunning** – Extraordinary cleverness used deceptively.
3. **A devilishly difficult task** – An exceptionally challenging task.

**UPSC application:** Devilish cunning in financial frauds enables perpetrators to exploit regulatory loopholes and institutional weaknesses.

## **3. DEVILRY**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Devil* = embodiment of evil.
- *-ry* = a condition, practice, behaviour or collection of activities.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The behaviour, activities or practices of the Devil.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The term evolved from its supernatural association to describe acts of extreme wickedness, cruelty, mischief or malicious conduct.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Wicked, cruel or mischievous behaviour; acts of extraordinary evil.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Acts of devilry** – Acts of extreme wickedness.
2. **Political devilry** – Malicious or unscrupulous political manoeuvring.
3. **Unleash one's devilry** – Engage in mischievous or wicked behaviour.

**UPSC application:** Systematic persecution and organised violence represent the devilry of ideologies rooted in hatred and intolerance.

## **4. DIABOLISM**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Diabolos* = Devil.
- *-ism* = doctrine, belief, practice or system.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The doctrine or practice associated with the Devil.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The term developed in religious contexts to describe the worship of, allegiance to or association with the Devil and practices involving demonic forces.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

The worship of the Devil or the belief and practices associated with Satan or demonic forces.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Religious diabolism** – Religious practices associated with Devil worship.
2. **Cult of diabolism** – A group or practice centred on Devil worship.
3. **Accusations of diabolism** – Allegations of involvement in Devil worship.

**UPSC application:** The study of diabolism can be relevant to the anthropology of religion, particularly when examining beliefs about supernatural beings, evil and ritual practices.

*Note: This is a specialised term, not a routine synonym for evil or immorality.*

## **5. DIABOLIST**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Diabol-* = Devil.
- *-ist* = a person who practises, follows or believes in something.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

A person associated with the Devil.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The term emerged from religious vocabulary describing people believed to worship or practise allegiance to the Devil.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A person who practises or advocates diabolism; a Devil worshipper.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Alleged diabolist** – A person accused of practising Devil worship.
2. **Diabolist practices** – Practices associated with diabolism.
3. **Historical accounts of diabolists** – Historical descriptions of alleged Devil worshippers.

**UPSC application:** Accusations of diabolism in historical societies can be examined through the anthropological concepts of witchcraft, social deviance, scapegoating and moral panic.

## **6. DIABOLICALLY**

**Part of Speech:** Adverb

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Diabolical* = extremely wicked or fiendish.
- *-ly* = in a particular manner.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

In a manner characteristic of the Devil.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

It developed from the religious association of diabolical with the Devil to describe actions performed with extreme cruelty, malice or cunning.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

In an extremely wicked, cruel, malicious or fiendishly ingenious manner.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Diabolically cruel** – Extremely cruel.
2. **Diabolically clever** – Exceptionally cunning or ingenious.
3. **Diabolically designed** – Designed with malicious or destructive intent.

**UPSC application:** Cybercriminals may employ diabolically clever techniques to manipulate unsuspecting citizens and compromise digital infrastructure.

## **7. DIABLERIE**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *-erie* = a practice, activity or characteristic.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Devilish activity or behaviour.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The French word developed to describe demonic mischief, supernatural tricks and devilish behaviour. English adopted it as a literary expression.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Mischievous, devilish or supernatural activity; behaviour involving diabolical tricks or pranks.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Literary diablerie** – Supernatural or devilish mischief in literature.
2. **Gothic diablerie** – Occult or demonic elements in Gothic fiction.
3. **A touch of diablerie** – A hint of devilish mischief.

**UPSC application:** Diablerie may appear in literary and cultural studies examining representations of supernatural evil.

*Note: A relatively uncommon literary word; useful for recognition rather than active Mains usage.*

## **8. DIABOLIZE**

**Part of Speech:** Verb (rare)

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Diabol-* = Devil.
- *-ize* = to make, render or treat as.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

To make something devil-like or represent it as evil.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word developed from the religious and moral association of the Devil to the act of portraying a person, group or idea as inherently evil.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

To portray or condemn someone or something as extremely evil; to demonise.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Diabolise political opponents** – Portray political opponents as inherently evil.
2. **Diabolise dissent** – Represent dissent as wicked or dangerous.
3. **Diabolised public image** – An image deliberately portrayed as evil.

**UPSC application:** Democratic discourse should avoid the tendency to diabolise dissent, as disagreement is an essential component of constitutional democracy.

*Note: Diabolize is rare; the more familiar English equivalent is demonize.*

# **PART II — EXTENDED ROOT FAMILY: BALLEIN (TO THROW)**

Now comes the interesting part of the etymology.

The Greek verb **ballein** (βάλλειν) means *to throw*.

It appears in several Greek-derived English words through different prefixes.

Remember:

- *Dia* + *ballein* = throw across → diabolos.
- *Para* + *ballein* = throw beside → parable, parabola.
- *Hyper* + *ballein* = throw beyond → hyperbole.
- *Pro* + *ballein* = throw forward → problem.
- *Syn* + *ballein* = throw together → symbol.
- *Meta* + *ballein* = throw/change position → metabole, metabolism.

These are not all descendants of *diabolos*. They are members of its deeper Greek verbal-root family.

## **9. PARABLE**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Para-* = beside, alongside.
- *Ballein* = to throw.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

To place or throw one thing alongside another.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The Greek *parabolē* referred to comparison or juxtaposition. It developed into the idea of explaining an abstract moral or spiritual truth through a familiar illustrative story.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A short, simple story that communicates a moral, ethical, religious or philosophical lesson.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Moral parable** – A story conveying a moral lesson.
2. **Biblical parable** – An illustrative story used in biblical teachings.
3. **Teach through parables** – Explain ideas through illustrative stories.

**UPSC application:** Gandhi frequently employed simple narratives and moral illustrations to communicate complex ethical and political ideas to ordinary people.

**Memory hook:** Place one familiar story beside an unfamiliar truth to explain it.

## **10. PARABOLA**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Para-* = beside, alongside.
- *Ballein* = to throw.
- Greek *parabolē* = comparison or juxtaposition.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Something placed alongside another for comparison.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The Greek term for comparison entered mathematical terminology through Latin and later came to denote a particular conic section, characterised by its distinctive curved shape.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A symmetrical, U-shaped or inverted U-shaped curve formed by a quadratic function.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Parabolic trajectory** – The curved path followed by an ideal projectile under gravity.
2. **Parabolic curve** – A curve represented by a quadratic equation.
3. **Parabolic reflector** – A reflector using a parabolic surface to concentrate or direct waves.

**UPSC application:** Understanding parabolic trajectories is useful in the context of space technology, missile systems and projectile motion.

## **11. HYPERBOLE**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Hyper-* = beyond, over or excessively.
- *Ballein* = to throw.
- Greek *hyperbolē* = an overshooting or excess.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

To throw beyond the limit.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The original sense of exceeding a limit evolved into a rhetorical device involving deliberate exaggeration for emphasis or dramatic effect.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

An exaggerated statement that is not intended to be understood literally.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Rhetorical hyperbole** – Deliberate exaggeration in speech or writing.
2. **Political hyperbole** – Exaggerated political claims.
3. **Without hyperbole** – Without exaggeration.

**UPSC application:** Political communication should distinguish legitimate criticism from hyperbole that exaggerates threats and fuels public anxiety.

**Example:** Calling a minor administrative delay a complete collapse of governance is an instance of hyperbole.

**Memory hook:** Hyper = excessive; hyperbole = throwing a statement beyond reality.

## **12. HYPERBOLIC**

**Part of Speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Hyper-* = beyond, excessive.
- *Ballein* = to throw.
- *-ic* = pertaining to or characterised by.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Characterised by throwing beyond the limits.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word developed from the rhetorical sense of exaggeration and also acquired a mathematical meaning associated with hyperbolas.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

1. Exaggerated or overstated.
2. Relating to a hyperbola in mathematics.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Hyperbolic rhetoric** – Exaggerated language.
2. **Hyperbolic claims** – Claims that go beyond reasonable proportions.
3. **Hyperbolic growth** – In mathematics, growth associated with a hyperbolic relationship.

**UPSC application:** Hyperbolic political rhetoric can undermine informed public debate and contribute to polarisation.

## **13. PROBLEM**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Pro-* = forward, before.
- *Ballein* = to throw.
- Greek *problēma* = something thrown forward; an obstacle or challenge.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Something thrown forward in one's path.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

Originally referring to something placed in front of a person as an obstacle or challenge, the term evolved to denote a difficult question, matter or situation requiring a solution.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A difficulty, challenge, undesirable situation or question requiring investigation and resolution.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Structural problem** – A problem rooted in the underlying organisation of a system.
2. **Governance problem** – A difficulty arising in the administration or functioning of government.
3. **Multidimensional problem** – A problem involving several interconnected factors.

**UPSC application:** Unemployment is not merely an economic problem; it is also a social, demographic, psychological and governance challenge.

**Memory hook:** A problem is something metaphorically thrown in front of you, obstructing your progress.

## **14. PROBLEMATIC**

**Part of Speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Problem* = difficulty or challenge.
- *-atic* = pertaining to or characterised by.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Characterised by problems or difficulties.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word evolved from describing matters that presented problems to describing situations, practices or assumptions that were questionable, difficult or potentially harmful.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Difficult to deal with, questionable, controversial or involving significant complications.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Problematic implementation** – Implementation involving significant difficulties.
2. **Problematic assumption** – An assumption that is questionable or flawed.
3. **Problematic social practice** – A practice associated with social harm or controversy.

**UPSC application:** A policy may have sound objectives but problematic implementation because of weak institutional capacity and inadequate accountability.

## **15. SYMBOL**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Syn-* = together.
- *Ballein* = to throw.
- Greek *symbolon* = something brought or put together; a token or identifying mark.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Something brought together or joined.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The Greek term referred to an object or token used for recognition or identification. It subsequently developed into something representing a larger idea, identity or concept.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

An object, sign, image or representation that stands for an idea, institution, identity or abstract concept.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Symbol of national unity** – Something representing national integration.
2. **Symbol of resistance** – Something representing opposition to authority or injustice.
3. **Symbol of sovereignty** – Something representing the authority and independence of a state.

**UPSC application:** The Indian national flag functions as a symbol of sovereignty, constitutional identity and national integration.

**Memory hook:** Different elements are brought together into one sign representing a larger idea.

## **16. SYMBOLIC**

**Part of Speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Symbol* = a sign representing something.
- *-ic* = pertaining to or characterised by.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Having the characteristics or significance of a symbol.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word developed from the representational meaning of a symbol to describe actions, gestures, measures or objects that communicate a deeper significance beyond their immediate practical function.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Representational, expressive or primarily intended to convey a particular meaning rather than produce direct practical results.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Symbolic gesture** – An action expressing a particular sentiment or commitment.
2. **Symbolic representation** – Representation through signs or images.
3. **Symbolic recognition** – Recognition that communicates acknowledgement or respect.

**UPSC application:** Symbolic representation of marginalised communities in political institutions must be complemented by substantive participation and decision-making power.

## **17. SYMBOLISM**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Symbol* = representative sign.
- *-ism* = system, doctrine or practice.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The system or practice of using symbols.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word evolved to describe the use of symbols to communicate abstract ideas and later became an important term in literature, art, religion, politics and cultural studies.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

The use or interpretation of symbols to represent ideas, values, identities or emotions.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Political symbolism** – Use of symbols to communicate political messages.
2. **Religious symbolism** – Use of symbols to express religious beliefs.
3. **National symbolism** – Symbols expressing national identity and collective memory.

**UPSC application:** National symbolism can strengthen collective identity, but excessive politicisation of symbols may create exclusionary forms of nationalism.

## **18. METABOLISM**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Meta-* = change, transformation or alteration.
- *Ballein* = to throw.
- Greek *metabolē* = change or transformation.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

A change or transformation.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The Greek concept of transformation entered scientific vocabulary and came to describe the chemical processes through which living organisms maintain life.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

The sum of chemical reactions occurring within a living organism to sustain life, including the breakdown and synthesis of substances.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Cellular metabolism** – Chemical processes occurring within cells.
2. **Metabolic activity** – Biochemical activity within an organism.
3. **Metabolic processes** – Chemical reactions essential for life.

**UPSC application:** Metabolism is relevant to human biology, nutrition, public health and the anthropology of human adaptation.

## **19. METABOLIC**

**Part of Speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Meta-* = transformation.
- *Ballein* = to throw.
- *-ic* = pertaining to.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Pertaining to transformation or change.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The term acquired a specialised scientific meaning relating to the chemical transformations that take place in living organisms.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Relating to metabolism or the biochemical processes that sustain life.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Metabolic disorders** – Conditions affecting normal metabolic functioning.
2. **Metabolic rate** – The rate at which an organism expends energy.
3. **Metabolic adaptation** – Physiological adjustment of metabolic processes to environmental conditions.

**UPSC application:** Metabolic adaptation to environmental stress is relevant to biological anthropology and the study of human adaptation.

## **20. BALLISTIC**

**Part of Speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- Greek *ballein* = to throw.
- Greek *ballein* gives rise to *ballistikos*, relating to throwing.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Pertaining to throwing or the motion of a projectile.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word developed through the study of projectiles and their motion, eventually becoming associated with the science of projectile trajectories and modern missile technology.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Relating to projectiles, their motion or the science of their flight.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Ballistic missile** – A missile that follows a largely ballistic trajectory during its unpowered flight phase.
2. **Ballistic trajectory** – The path followed by a projectile under the influence of gravity and other forces.
3. **Ballistic defence** – Defence systems designed to detect, track or intercept ballistic missiles.

**UPSC application:** Ballistic missile defence has strategic implications for deterrence, national security and the regional balance of power.

## **21. BALLISTICS**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Ballein* = to throw.
- *-ics* = a field of study or scientific discipline.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The science or study of throwing projectiles.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The term evolved from the study of projectile motion to a specialised scientific discipline covering the behaviour, trajectory and impact of projectiles.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

The scientific study of the motion, behaviour and effects of projectiles, including firearms and missiles.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Forensic ballistics** – Scientific examination of firearms, bullets and related evidence.
2. **External ballistics** – Study of projectile motion after it leaves the launching mechanism.
3. **Terminal ballistics** – Study of a projectile's behaviour and effects upon impact.

**UPSC application:** Forensic ballistics contributes to criminal investigation, scientific evidence and the administration of criminal justice.

# **1. EDIBLE**

# **EDIBLE**

## **/ˈed.ə.bəl/**

Adjective · Food, Nutrition, Biology, Public Health

**Meaning:** Fit or suitable to be eaten; safe or acceptable for consumption.

Example: *Access to safe and edible food is essential for ensuring nutritional security.*

## **A. Meaning of the Components**

The word **edible** comes from the Latin verb *edere*, meaning **to eat**.

| Component   | Meaning                               |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------- |
| ed- / edere | To eat                                |
| -ible       | Capable of being; fit or suitable for |

**Word formation:**

\\[ \text{EDERE (to eat)} + \text{-IBLE (capable of)} \\]

\\[ \boxed{\text{EDIBLE = Fit to be eaten}} \\]

**Important:** The root *ed-* is associated with eating and consumption. It should not be confused with *edit*, which comes from a different Latin root.

## **B. Literal Meaning**

Something that is capable of being eaten.

Think of the distinction:

- A fruit may be edible.
- A poisonous berry may look like food but is not safely edible.
- A food item may be technically edible but nutritionally inadequate.

Thus, *edible* primarily concerns suitability for eating, although in ordinary usage it often implies safety for consumption.

## **C. Semantic Evolution**

1. **Latin: Edere**

   The original verb meant *to eat*.
2. **Latin: Edibilis**

   Developed the sense of something that could be eaten.
3. **Old and Middle English development**

   The Latin-derived vocabulary entered English through French and Latin scholarly usage.
4. **Modern English: Edible**

   Describes something fit for consumption, especially food, plants, oils and other consumable substances.

## **D. Modern Meaning**

**Edible** = Suitable for consumption; fit to be eaten.

Distinguish these three terms:

| Word       | Meaning               | Example                |
| ---------- | --------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Edible     | Fit to be eaten       | Edible mushrooms       |
| Palatable  | Pleasant to taste     | Palatable school meals |
| Nutritious | Providing nourishment | Nutritious food        |

**UPSC analytical distinction:** Food can be edible without being nutritious. Similarly, food can be nutritious in composition but become unsafe due to contamination.

This distinction is relevant to food security, food safety and nutritional security.

## **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

1. **Edible oil** – Oil suitable for human consumption.
2. **Edible crops** – Crops cultivated for human consumption.
3. **Edible food products** – Food products suitable for consumption.

**Mains application:**

"India must strengthen food safety regulations and improve access to affordable, nutritious and edible food to achieve nutritional security."

# **2. THE ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY OF EDIBLE**

The Latin root **EDERE (to eat)** has generated several useful English words.

For UPSC preparation, the most important distinction is between:

- Words directly derived from *edere*.
- Words historically related through the Latin participial stem *esum*.
- Words formed through related Latin expressions concerning consumption.

The following family includes both direct derivatives and historically connected formations.

# **WORD 1. INEDIBLE**

High priority

**Meaning:** Not fit or suitable for eating.

## **A. Meaning of the Components**

| Component | Meaning          |
| --------- | ---------------- |
| In-       | Not; opposite of |
| Edible    | Fit to be eaten  |

## **B. Literal Meaning**

\\[ \text{IN} + \text{EDIBLE} = \text{NOT FIT TO BE EATEN} \\]

## **C. Semantic Evolution**

The negative prefix *in-* reverses the meaning of *edible*. The word describes substances that are unsuitable for consumption.

## **D. Modern Meaning**

Not suitable for human consumption, either because of its nature, toxicity, contamination or other characteristics.

**Example:** The contaminated food was declared inedible by the authorities.

2. Inedible plant species
3. Food rendered inedible by contamination

**UPSC connection:** Food safety, environmental pollution, pesticide residues and public health.

| -ible     | Capable of being            |
| -ity      | State, quality or condition |

## **B. Literal Meaning**

The quality of being capable of being eaten.

## **C. Semantic Evolution**

The adjective *edible* acquired the noun-forming suffix *-ity*, transforming a characteristic into an abstract quality.

# **WORD 3. COMESTIBLE**

High priority

**Meaning:** Something suitable for eating; an edible food item.

## **A. Meaning of the Components**

| Component | Meaning              |
| --------- | -------------------- |
| Com-      | Together; completely |
| Edere     | To eat               |
| -ible     | Capable of being     |

## **B. Literal Meaning**

Something that can be consumed as food.

## **C. Semantic Evolution**

Latin *comedere* developed into the Latin form *comestibilis*, referring to something fit to be eaten. It entered English through French.

An edible substance or food item.

*Comestible* is more formal and less frequently used than *edible*.

**Example:** The region's local markets offer a variety of indigenous comestibles.

## **E. Common Usage**

1. Comestible products
2. Indigenous comestibles
3. Edible and comestible goods

**UPSC connection:** Indigenous food systems, tribal livelihoods, local food economies and food diversity.

**Memory hook:** Comestible = Something you can consume.

Ordinary expression:

"People should avoid excessive eating and drinking."

Improved expression:

"Promoting abstemious dietary practices and discouraging excessive consumption can contribute to the prevention of lifestyle-related non-communicable diseases."

"Tribal people eat different types of forest foods."

"Indigenous communities possess rich ethnobotanical knowledge concerning the edibility, preparation and nutritional properties of wild plants and forest-based comestibles."

"Persistent hunger among esurient and economically vulnerable populations reflects structural inequalities in access to food, livelihood opportunities and nutritional resources."

## **1. STUNTING**

**Word:** Stunting\
**Root word:** Stunt\
**Part of speech:** Noun (gerund) / Present participle of the verb *stunt*

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- **Stunt:** To prevent or restrict the normal growth, development or progress of something.
- **-ing:** A suffix indicating an ongoing action, process or activity.

**Memory association:** Imagine a plant whose growth is restricted because it receives insufficient sunlight or nutrients. Its growth is being *stunted*.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

**Stunting = The act or process of restricting normal growth or development.**

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word *stunt* historically carried the sense of being short, blunt or limited. It subsequently developed the meaning of hindering or preventing normal growth.

The metaphorical extension is important:

- Physical growth → restricted height or development.
- Biological development → impaired growth of a child.
- Intellectual development → restricted cognitive development.
- Economic development → constrained economic progress.
- Institutional development → impediments to institutional capacity.

Thus, the word moved from the idea of physical shortness to the broader idea of arrested or impaired development.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Stunting** refers to the restriction or impairment of normal growth, development or progress.

It is particularly important in public health, nutrition, human development and development economics.

**UPSC-relevant distinction:**

- **Stunting:** Low height for age, reflecting chronic or recurrent undernutrition.
- **Wasting:** Low weight for height, reflecting acute or recent undernutrition.
- **Underweight:** Low weight for age, which may reflect stunting, wasting or both.

Public health terminology: WHO child growth standards.

### **E. Common Usage: High-Frequency Collocations**

| Collocation / Expression      | Meaning                                                                                     |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Childhood stunting**        | Impairment of physical growth during childhood                                              |
| **Stunting of human capital** | Restriction of people's productive capabilities through poor health, education or nutrition |
| **Stunting economic growth**  | Hindering or slowing down economic development                                              |

**UPSC-ready sentences:**

1. Persistent childhood stunting undermines India's demographic dividend by reducing the physical and cognitive potential of its future workforce.
2. Malnutrition, inadequate sanitation and recurrent infections contribute to intergenerational cycles of poverty and stunting.
3. Institutional inefficiencies and regulatory bottlenecks can stunt economic growth and weaken entrepreneurial dynamism.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

The word **horticulture** originates from Latin *hortus*, meaning **garden**, and *cultura*, meaning **cultivation or tending**.

| Component  | Origin          | Meaning                                 |
| ---------- | --------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| *Hortus*   | Latin           | Garden or enclosed plot                 |
| *Colere*   | Latin           | To cultivate, inhabit, tend or care for |
| *Cultura*  | Latin           | Cultivation, tending or care            |
| *-culture* | Latin *cultura* | Cultivation or systematic care          |

**Root idea: HORTUS (GARDEN) + CULTURA (CULTIVATION) = CULTIVATION OF GARDENS.**

An important linguistic distinction:

- *Hortus* gives us the garden-related element **horti-**.
- *Colere* gives us **cult-**, as in cultivate and culture.
- These are two distinct Latin roots combined to form *horticulture*.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

**Horticulture = The cultivation and systematic care of gardens and garden crops.**

Think of a person cultivating a garden by growing fruits, vegetables, flowers, ornamental plants and other useful plants.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

**LATIN: HORTUS**

Garden / Enclosed plot

**LATIN: CULTURA**

Cultivation / Tending / Care

**HORTICULTURE**

Systematic cultivation of garden crops

**MODERN AGRICULTURAL SCIENCE**

Scientific cultivation of fruits, vegetables, flowers, plantation crops and ornamental plants

The original idea of cultivating enclosed gardens gradually developed into a specialised branch of agricultural science involving plant propagation, crop production, post-harvest management, landscaping and commercial cultivation.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Horticulture (noun):** The branch of agricultural science concerned with the cultivation, improvement, production and management of fruits, vegetables, flowers, ornamental plants, spices and other specialised crops.

# **4. THE CULT- BRANCH: THE SECOND ROOT INSIDE HORTICULTURE**

Now comes an important discovery.

The word *horticulture* contains not one but two major Latin roots:

- HORTUS = Garden.
- COLERE = To cultivate, inhabit, tend or care for.

The second root, *colere*, gives us an enormous English vocabulary family.

It is one of the most productive roots for UPSC vocabulary because it connects agriculture, society, civilisation, cultural practices and human development.

**HORTUS**

Garden

**COLERE**

To cultivate / Tend / Care for

**CULTURA**

Cultivation / Care / Development

**HORTICULTURE**

Cultivation of gardens

The *cult-* family is much larger than the *hort-* family. The following words are particularly important.
