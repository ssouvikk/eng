# **DISSIPATE**

WORD POWER • ROOT-BASED VOCABULARY • UPSC EDITION

**Pronunciation:** /ˈdɪsɪpeɪt/\
**Part of speech:** Verb (transitive and intransitive)\
**Word family:** Latin *dis-* + *supare* (to throw)

## **1. Meaning of the Components**

Let us break the word into its historical components.

| Component  | Meaning                                 | Conceptual association |
| ---------- | --------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| **dis-**   | Apart, asunder, in different directions | Separation, dispersal  |
| **supare** | To throw (historical Latin root)        | Throwing or scattering |

The word comes from Latin *dissipare*, meaning **to scatter, disperse or throw apart**.

**Memory hook:** Imagine throwing a handful of seeds in different directions. They scatter, spread out and become difficult to gather again. That is the central idea behind *dissipate*.

## **2. Literal Meaning**

**To throw something apart in different directions; to scatter or disperse.**

Originally, the word conveyed a physical action: scattering something so that it loses its concentration or collective form.

## **3. Semantic Evolution**

The meaning gradually expanded from physical scattering to abstract disappearance.

1. **Physical scattering**

   To scatter or disperse something in different directions.

   *Example:* The wind dissipated the smoke.
2. **Gradual disappearance**

   To spread out until something becomes weaker or disappears.

   *Example:* The morning fog gradually dissipated.
3. **Diminution of abstract things**

   To cause feelings, doubts, fears or tensions to disappear.

   *Example:* Scientific evidence dissipated public fears.
4. **Squandering or waste**

   To spend resources carelessly, thereby making them disappear.

   *Example:* The government dissipated public resources through inefficient expenditure.

## **4. Modern Meaning**

**Dissipate (verb):**

1. To gradually disappear, disperse or become weaker.
2. To cause something to disappear by scattering or dispersing it.
3. To squander or waste resources, energy, wealth or opportunities.

**Important distinction:**

- *Dissipate* = disappear gradually or scatter.
- *Disperse* = spread in different directions.
- *Dissolve* = become incorporated into a liquid or lose a distinct form.
- *Squander* = waste something valuable carelessly.

## **5. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

| Collocation                    | Meaning and application                                              |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Dissipate public funds**     | Waste public money through irresponsible expenditure.                |
| **Dissipate social tensions**  | Reduce or gradually eliminate tensions within society.               |
| **Dissipate doubts and fears** | Remove uncertainty and apprehension through evidence or reassurance. |

**UPSC-ready sentences:**

1. Transparent governance and effective grievance redressal can dissipate public distrust in institutions.
2. Fiscal indiscipline may dissipate scarce public resources without generating corresponding developmental outcomes.
3. Evidence-based communication is essential to dissipate misinformation and public apprehensions.

# **PART II — ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY**

## **The root: LATIN *SUPARE* / *DISSIPARE***

The central historical idea is **throwing, scattering and dispersing**.

An important etymological clarification: not every word that begins with *dis-*, *per-* or *re-* belongs to this family. We must distinguish genuine common ancestry from superficial resemblance.

The following words are arranged into two groups:

- **Group A:** Direct historical family of *dissipate*.
- **Group B:** Closely related Latin-root vocabulary built around the broader idea of throwing, scattering or driving apart. These are useful semantic relatives, but not all descend from *dissipare* itself.

## **GROUP A — Direct Etymological Family**

**Meaning of the Components**

- *dis-* = apart, in different directions.
- *sipation* = the scattering action associated with *dissipare*.

**Literal Meaning**

The act of scattering or throwing apart.

**Semantic Evolution**

Physical dispersal → gradual disappearance → wasteful expenditure or indulgence.

**Modern Meaning**

1. The gradual disappearance or dispersal of something.

# **HYGROPHOBIC — Etymology, Meaning & Word Family**



Vocabulary Builder · UPSC Prelims + Mains + Essay

Greek Root: HYGR- / HYDRO-



## **1. HYGROPHOBIC**

**Pronunciation:** hy-gruh-FOH-bik\
**Part of speech:** Adjective\
**Word formation:** Hygro + phobic

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

| Component | Origin         | Meaning                   |
| --------- | -------------- | ------------------------- |
| Hygro-    | Greek *hygrós* | Moist, wet, damp          |
| -phobic   | Greek *phóbos* | Fear, aversion, avoidance |

**Memory hook:**

- HYGR = Moisture
- PHOBIC = Fear or aversion

### **B. Literal Meaning**

**Hygrophobic = Having an aversion to moisture or water.**

Something that resists contact with water, repels moisture, or does not readily become wet.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

1. Ancient Greek *hygrós* referred to something wet or moist.
2. The Greek-derived combining form *hygro-* came to denote moisture and humidity.
3. The suffix *-phobic*, derived from *phóbos* (fear), acquired a broader scientific meaning of aversion or repulsion.
4. In modern scientific terminology, *hygrophobic* describes materials or surfaces that resist moisture.

**Important distinction:** In scientific contexts, *hygrophobic* generally describes moisture-repelling properties. It does not necessarily imply a psychological fear of water.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Hygrophobic:** Having a tendency to repel or resist moisture; not readily absorbing or being wetted by water.

It is used primarily in material science, chemistry, environmental science and related technical fields.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Hygrophobic coating** — A coating that resists moisture.
2. **Hygrophobic surface** — A surface that repels water or moisture.
3. **Hygrophobic material** — A material that resists wetting or moisture absorption.

**UPSC application:** Useful in discussions of water-resistant materials, sustainable construction, nanotechnology and environmental engineering.

## **2. Understanding the Root System**

To build a meaningful word family, we must distinguish two Greek roots:

# **HYGR- / HYGRO-**

**Greek: hygrós**

Moisture, dampness, humidity

Examples: Hygroscopic, hygrometer, hygrometry, hygrophyte.

# **HYDR- / HYDRO-**

**Greek: hýdōr**

Water

Examples: Hydrology, hydraulic, hydropower, hydroelectric.

**Etymological caution:** HYGR- and HYDR- are related in meaning but are not interchangeable roots. A genuine word family of *hygrophobic* should primarily follow HYGR-. The HYDR- family is a useful associated vocabulary cluster, but not a direct derivational family.

# **3. The HYGR- Word Family: Detailed Etymological Expansion**

The following words share the Greek root *hygrós* (moist/wet) or are formed using the combining form *hygro-*.

## **A. HYGROSCOPIC**

**Pronunciation:** hy-gruh-SKOP-ik\
**Part of speech:** Adjective

### **Meaning of the Components**

| Component | Meaning                                                               |
| --------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hygro-    | Moisture                                                              |
| -scopic   | Relating to observing or examining; from Greek *skopein* (to look at) |

### **Literal Meaning**

Originally, something associated with observing moisture.

### **Semantic Evolution**

The word developed a specialised scientific meaning. A hygroscopic substance attracts and absorbs water vapour from the surrounding atmosphere.

### **Modern Meaning**

**Hygroscopic:** Having the ability to absorb moisture from the air.

Unlike a hygrophobic substance, a hygroscopic substance tends to attract atmospheric moisture.

### **Common Usage**

1. **Hygroscopic substances** — Materials that absorb atmospheric moisture.
2. **Hygroscopic properties** — The moisture-absorbing characteristics of a substance.
3. **Hygroscopic materials** — Materials such as silica gel and certain salts that absorb moisture.

**UPSC relevance:** Chemistry, food preservation, pharmaceutical storage, humidity control and material science.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

Taking away an external layer

**EXPOSURE**

Making something bare or uncovered

**DEPRIVATION**

Taking away possessions, powers, privileges or rights

Removing unnecessary elements to reveal the fundamental core

Historically, the verb *strip* is recorded in Old English in the sense of plundering or despoiling. Over time, its meaning expanded from taking away possessions to removing coverings, layers, privileges and other attributes.

1. **Divested of something:** Deprived of possessions, rights, powers or privileges.

4. **Depleted:** Having resources, capabilities or supporting elements taken away.

### **2.10 STRIP MINING**

**Part of speech:** Noun / Gerund

**Meaning of the Components**

- STRIP: Remove layers.
- MINING: Extraction of minerals and geological resources.

**Literal Meaning**

The process of extracting minerals by removing successive layers of soil and rock.

**Semantic Evolution**

The combination of the two words transformed the general action of stripping into a specialised mining technique.

Its contemporary usage is strongly associated with environmental externalities and the regulation of mineral extraction.

**Modern Meaning**

A form of surface mining in which overburden is removed to expose and extract mineral deposits.

**Common Usage**

1. Ecological consequences of strip mining.

3. Rehabilitation of strip-mined land.

### **2.11 STRIP CROPPING**

**Part of speech:** Noun

**Meaning of the Components**

- STRIP: A long, narrow band.
- CROPPING: Growing agricultural crops.

**Literal Meaning**

Growing crops in alternating long, narrow bands or strips.

**Semantic Evolution**

The noun *strip*, meaning a narrow elongated area, was combined with cropping to describe an agricultural arrangement in which different crops are grown in alternating bands.

**Modern Meaning**

An agricultural soil-conservation practice in which different crops are cultivated in alternating strips, often along the contour of sloping land, to reduce soil erosion.

**Common Usage**

2. Contour strip cropping.
3. Strip cropping as an erosion-control measure.

### **2.12 STRIP TILLAGE**

**Part of speech:** Noun

**Meaning of the Components**

- STRIP: A narrow band.
- TILLAGE: Preparation and cultivation of soil.

**Literal Meaning**

Tilling or disturbing only selected strips of soil rather than the entire field.

**Semantic Evolution**

The meaning of *strip* as a narrow band was applied to agricultural soil preparation. This developed into a conservation-oriented cultivation technique.

**Modern Meaning**

A conservation agriculture practice in which only narrow planting strips are tilled, while the soil between the strips remains largely undisturbed.

**Common Usage**

1. Strip tillage for soil conservation.
2. Adoption of strip-tillage practices.
3. Strip tillage as a conservation agriculture technique.

**UPSC connection:** Conservation agriculture, minimum tillage, soil organic matter, water conservation and sustainable intensification.

**Distinction:** Strip cropping concerns the spatial arrangement of crops; strip tillage concerns the spatial extent of soil disturbance.

### **2.14 STRIP SEARCH**

**Part of speech:** Noun / Verb

**Meaning of the Components**

- STRIP: Remove clothing.
- SEARCH: Examine to locate something.

**Literal Meaning**

A search conducted after requiring an individual to remove some or all clothing.

**Semantic Evolution**

The physical meaning of stripping was combined with searching to describe an intrusive form of personal examination.

**Modern Meaning**

A highly intrusive search involving the removal of clothing to check for concealed objects or substances.

**Common Usage**

## **7. Final Retention Strategy**

Remember the word through one central mental image:

**Imagine removing the outer layers of an object.**

- Remove its covering → STRIP OFF.
- Remove its internal components → STRIP OUT.
- Remove its privileges → STRIP OF.
- Remove its prejudices → STRIP AWAY.
- Reduce it to essentials → STRIPPED-DOWN.
- Remove layers of earth to extract minerals → STRIP MINING.
- Grow crops in alternating bands → STRIP CROPPING.

**One root, multiple applications:**

- **Polity:** Stripped of constitutional powers.
- **Governance:** Stripped-down administrative machinery.
- **Environment:** Strip mining and land degradation.
- **Agriculture:** Strip cropping and soil conservation.
- **Ethics:** Strip away prejudice and discrimination.
- **Essay:** Strip away superficial appearances to understand the underlying reality.

*Etymological note:* The entries above form a practical semantic word family, including inflections, phrasal verbs and compounds. Not every compound is a direct historical derivative of the original verb; some use the related noun sense of *strip*, meaning a long, narrow band or stretch. This distinction matters when studying etymology rigorously.

**The ultimate memory formula: STRIP = REMOVE. Everything else is an application of that central idea.**
