# **HOOLIGANISM — Etymology, Semantic Evolution & Word Family**

WORD POWER MADE EASY · ETYMOLOGICAL VOCABULARY · UPSC EDITION

## **1. HOOLIGANISM**

**Pronunciation:** /ˈhuː.lɪ.ɡən.ɪ.zəm/\
**Part of speech:** Noun\
**Core idea:** Violent, disorderly and antisocial behaviour, particularly by unruly groups.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

The word can be divided into two parts:

| Component | Meaning                                                           |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hooligan  | A violent, rowdy or disorderly person                             |
| -ism      | A suffix denoting a practice, behaviour, system or characteristic |

**Important etymological distinction:** *Hooligan* is not formed from two independently meaningful English roots. Its origin is uncertain. The suffix *-ism* was subsequently added to form *hooliganism*.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Hooliganism literally refers to the behaviour or practice associated with hooligans.

In simpler terms:

**Hooligan + ism = The practice or conduct of behaving like a hooligan.**

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The historical development can be understood in three stages:

1. **Late nineteenth-century emergence:** Hooligan appeared in British slang, particularly in London, as a term for a rough, violent or disorderly person.
2. **Association with group violence:** Its meaning increasingly became associated with gangs, street violence and collective disorder.
3. **Modern institutional usage:** The term expanded beyond street gangs to describe violence in sporting events, political demonstrations, public gatherings and other public spaces.

The precise origin of *hooligan* remains disputed. One popular explanation connects it with an Irish surname such as *Houlihan*, but this is not conclusively established.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Hooliganism** refers to violent, aggressive, unruly or destructive behaviour, particularly in public spaces and often involving groups.

It may involve:

- Physical assault and intimidation.
- Vandalism and destruction of public property.
- Violent clashes between rival groups.
- Disorder during sporting events.
- Mob violence and disruption of public order.

**UPSC-oriented interpretation:**

Hooliganism represents a manifestation of collective disorder that threatens public order, civic discipline, social harmony and the rule of law.

### **E. Common Usage — High-Frequency Collocations**

| Collocation          | Meaning                                                                       |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Football hooliganism | Violence and disorder associated with football spectators or rival fan groups |
| Street hooliganism   | Violent and antisocial conduct in public streets                              |
| Curb hooliganism     | Take measures to restrict or prevent violent, disorderly behaviour            |

**Examples for UPSC answer writing:**

1. Football hooliganism undermines the spirit of sportsmanship and creates challenges for public-order management.
2. Effective policing and community engagement are essential to curb street hooliganism.
3. Mob violence and hooliganism weaken public confidence in the rule of law.

## **2. ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY OF HOOLIGAN**

### **Understanding the family tree**

Unlike words derived from productive Latin or Greek roots, *hooligan* has a relatively small English word family.

Its derivatives are formed through the addition of suffixes rather than through an extensive network of classical roots.

### **1. HOOLIGAN**

**Part of speech:** Noun

**A. Meaning of the Components**

- Hooligan: An individual who engages in violent, disorderly or antisocial behaviour.
- The word itself has no established internal morphological decomposition.

**B. Literal Meaning**

A person characterised by rowdy, aggressive or lawless behaviour.

**C. Semantic Evolution**

Originally associated with unruly young street gangs in late nineteenth-century London, the word gradually acquired a broader meaning.

It subsequently became particularly associated with violent football supporters and organised public disorder.

**D. Modern Meaning**

A violent, aggressive or disorderly person who deliberately disrupts public peace or engages in antisocial activities.

**E. Common Usage**

1. Football hooligan
2. Violent hooligan
3. Gang of hooligans

**UPSC example:** The state must distinguish peaceful protesters from violent hooligans to ensure that the right to peaceful assembly is protected.

### **2. HOOLIGANISM**

**Part of speech:** Noun

**A. Meaning of the Components**

- Hooligan: A violent or disorderly person.
- -ism: A suffix denoting behaviour, practice, system or doctrine.

**B. Literal Meaning**

The behaviour or practice of acting like a hooligan.

**C. Semantic Evolution**

Initially associated with street violence and gang behaviour, the term expanded to encompass collective violence, vandalism and public disorder.

**D. Modern Meaning**

Violent, destructive or antisocial behaviour, particularly when committed by groups in public spaces.

**E. Common Usage**

1. Football hooliganism
2. Organised hooliganism
3. Curbing hooliganism

**UPSC example:** The prevention of hooliganism requires a combination of effective policing, community participation and civic education.

### **3. HOOLIGANIZE**

**Part of speech:** Verb (rare)

**A. Meaning of the Components**

- Hooligan: A violent or disorderly person.
- -ize: A verb-forming suffix meaning to make, cause or behave in a particular manner.

**B. Literal Meaning**

To make someone behave like a hooligan or to engage in hooligan-like conduct.

**C. Semantic Evolution**

The verb emerged during the early popularity of the word *hooligan*. Historical newspaper usage included expressions such as being "hooliganed".

However, it never became an established standard verb in contemporary English.

**D. Modern Meaning**

To behave in a violent, disorderly or hooligan-like manner; a rare and generally avoidable expression.

**E. Common Usage**

This is not a high-frequency modern collocation. Prefer these standard expressions instead:

1. Engage in hooliganism
2. Resort to violent behaviour
3. Indulge in vandalism

**UPSC application:** Prefer *engage in hooliganism* rather than inventing a verb such as *hooliganize* in formal answers.

### **4. HOOLIGANISH**

**Part of speech:** Adjective (informal/rare)

**A. Meaning of the Components**

- Hooligan: A disorderly or violent individual.
- -ish: A suffix meaning somewhat like, resembling or having the characteristics of something.

**B. Literal Meaning**

Somewhat resembling a hooligan or exhibiting hooligan-like characteristics.

**C. Semantic Evolution**

The suffix -ish allows the noun to function as a descriptive adjective, indicating resemblance rather than necessarily establishing that someone is a hooligan.

**D. Modern Meaning**

Displaying rowdy, aggressive, unruly or antisocial characteristics.

**E. Common Usage**

There are no well-established high-frequency collocations. For formal writing, prefer:

1. Hooligan-like behaviour
2. Rowdy conduct
3. Disorderly behaviour

**UPSC application:** Certain hooligan-like activities during public demonstrations can undermine the legitimacy of an otherwise peaceful movement.

### **5. HOOLIGANISTIC**

**Part of speech:** Adjective (rare)

**A. Meaning of the Components**

- Hooligan: A violent or disorderly individual.
- -istic: An adjective-forming suffix indicating characteristics or qualities associated with something.

**B. Literal Meaning**

Possessing the characteristics of hooliganism.

**C. Semantic Evolution**

The adjective follows the morphological pattern of words such as *nationalistic* and *individualistic*, describing qualities associated with a particular phenomenon.

Unlike those common adjectives, *hooliganistic* remains uncommon.

**D. Modern Meaning**

Characterised by violent, aggressive, destructive or disorderly conduct.

**E. Common Usage**

Prefer these established alternatives:

1. Hooligan behaviour
2. Violent conduct
3. Antisocial behaviour

**UPSC application:** The administration must adopt a zero-tolerance approach towards violent and hooliganistic conduct that threatens public order.

*Note: In polished academic writing, "hooliganistic" is usually better replaced with "violent", "disorderly" or "antisocial".*

### **6. HOOLIGANESQUE**

**Part of speech:** Adjective (rare)

**A. Meaning of the Components**

- Hooligan: A violent or disorderly person.
- -esque: A suffix of French origin meaning in the style or manner of something.

**B. Literal Meaning**

In the manner or style of a hooligan.

**C. Semantic Evolution**

The suffix -esque is commonly used to describe resemblance to a person, characteristic or artistic style.

Examples of the suffix include:

- Kafkaesque
- Picturesque
- Romanesque

*Hooliganesque* is a rare derivative that describes conduct resembling hooligan behaviour.

**D. Modern Meaning**

Resembling the aggressive, rowdy or disorderly conduct associated with hooligans.

**E. Common Usage**

There are no established high-frequency collocations. Prefer:

1. Hooligan-like conduct
2. Thuggish behaviour
3. Violent public disorder

## **4. HIGH-VALUE DISTINCTIONS FOR UPSC**

These words may appear interchangeable, but they carry different shades of meaning.

| Word        | Core emphasis                      | Example                         |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Hooliganism | Rowdy, violent public behaviour    | Football violence               |
| Thuggery    | Criminal intimidation and violence | Political intimidation          |
| Vandalism   | Destruction of property            | Damage to public infrastructure |
| Rioting     | Collective public disturbance      | Communal riots                  |
| Ruffianism  | Brutal, lawless conduct            | Street violence                 |
| Hoodlum     | A violent or criminal individual   | Gang member                     |
| Miscreant   | A wrongdoer or criminal            | Habitual offender               |

### **Distinguish three particularly important concepts**

**1. Hooliganism vs Vandalism**

- Hooliganism focuses on disorderly and violent behaviour.
- Vandalism specifically focuses on deliberate damage or destruction of property.

**2. Hooliganism vs Rioting**

- Hooliganism can be committed by an individual or a group.
- Rioting involves collective public disorder and has a specific legal meaning.

In India, the applicable provisions concerning unlawful assembly and rioting are found in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, particularly Sections 189 and 191 respectively.

**3. Hooliganism vs Thuggery**

- Hooliganism generally emphasises rowdy public disorder.
- Thuggery places greater emphasis on criminal intimidation, violence and coercive conduct.

- **Corbula** – a small basket.
- **Corbicula** – a little basket.

These forms generated several descendants in European languages, some of which entered English.

## **1. SWAYED**

**Pronunciation:** /sweɪd/\
**Part of speech:** Verb (past tense and past participle of *sway*)\
**Root word:** Sway\
**Core idea:** Influence that causes a shift in someone's opinion, decision, allegiance or behaviour.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

The word *sway* is not formed from two independent English components. It is an inherited word whose precise deeper origin is uncertain.

Its modern semantic structure can nevertheless be understood through two ideas:

- **Movement:** To move from one side to another.
- **Influence:** To cause someone's mind, opinion or decision to move from its original position.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Originally, *to sway* refers to the physical movement of something from side to side.

Imagine a tree bending from side to side under the force of the wind.

**Physical movement → Mental movement → Change in opinion or decision.**

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The semantic development can be understood in three stages:

1. **Physical movement:** An object oscillates or bends from side to side.
2. **Psychological influence:** A person's thinking is metaphorically moved from its original position.
3. **Decision-making influence:** Someone is persuaded, influenced or induced to change an opinion, preference or decision.

The metaphor is powerful:

Just as the wind sways a tree, arguments, emotions, political pressure or personal interests can sway an individual.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Swayed (verb):** Persuaded, influenced or induced to change one's opinion, decision, judgement or allegiance.

It can also mean physically moved or caused to move from side to side.

**Important distinction:**

- *Persuaded* – influenced through reasoning or argument.
- *Swayed* – influenced, potentially through reasoning, emotions, pressure, interests or other external factors.
- *Coerced* – forced through threats or pressure.

Swaying does not necessarily imply coercion.

### **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

| Collocation / Expression | Meaning                                     | Example                                                   |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sway public opinion      | Influence the collective views of people    | Social media campaigns can sway public opinion.           |
| Sway political decisions | Influence governmental or political choices | Corporate lobbying may sway political decisions.          |
| Be swayed by emotions    | Allow emotions to influence judgement       | Civil servants should not be swayed by personal emotions. |

**UPSC application:**

"An impartial civil servant must not be swayed by political pressure, personal prejudices or vested interests."

# **BOGUS**

VOCABULARY BUILDING • ETYMOLOGY • UPSC MAINS & PRELIMS

# **1. BOGUS**



High-value vocabulary

GS II • GS III • Essay



**Pronunciation:** /ˈbəʊ.ɡəs/ (BOH-guhs)\
**Part of Speech:** Adjective (informal, often used in journalism and public discourse)

## **1. Meaning of the Components**

Unlike words formed from recognisable Latin or Greek roots, *bogus* does not have a reliably established, separable set of meaningful components.

- **Bogus:** An independent word, probably originating in American slang in the early nineteenth century.
- It is not etymologically derived from *bo* + *gus*.

**Historical association:** It has been linked to the word *bogus* used for a counterfeit coin-making machine or apparatus.

## **2. Literal Meaning**

Originally associated with a machine used for producing counterfeit money, the word acquired the sense of something fraudulent, fake or counterfeit.

**Literal conceptual image: Something manufactured to deceive people into believing it is genuine.**

## **3. Semantic Evolution**

1. **Early 19th century – Counterfeiting**

   Associated with an apparatus used to manufacture counterfeit coins or money.
2. **Expansion – Fraudulent or counterfeit**

   Began describing objects, documents and transactions that were not genuine.
3. **Modern figurative usage – False or baseless**

   Expanded to describe claims, arguments, evidence, explanations and justifications that lack authenticity or credibility.

**Semantic progression:**

Counterfeit money

Fake or fraudulent objects

False claims, fabricated evidence and baseless arguments

## **4. Modern Meaning**

**Bogus (adjective):** Not genuine, authentic or legitimate; deliberately false, fabricated or misleading.

It can refer to:

1. **Counterfeit:** Something manufactured to imitate a genuine product or document.
2. **Fraudulent:** Something intended to deceive or obtain an unlawful advantage.
3. **Baseless:** A claim or argument unsupported by credible evidence.

**Important distinction:** Bogus does not always mean merely incorrect. It often carries an implication of deception, fabrication or lack of authenticity.

## **5. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

| Collocation       | Meaning                                              |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Bogus claims      | Claims that are false or fabricated                  |
| Bogus credentials | Falsified educational or professional qualifications |
| Bogus allegations | False or fabricated accusations                      |

**UPSC-oriented examples:**

1. The proliferation of bogus news undermines public trust in democratic institutions.
2. The government must strengthen verification mechanisms to prevent the use of bogus documents in welfare schemes.
3. Bogus allegations can damage the credibility of public institutions and erode citizens' trust in the justice system.

# **6. Etymological Word Family: BOGUS**

**Important etymological clarification:** Unlike a Latin or Greek root, *bogus* does not have a large, well-established derivational family. Words such as *bogus*, *fraudulent*, *fictitious* and *spurious* are semantic synonyms, not descendants of the same root.

For intellectually rigorous vocabulary building, we should distinguish between:

- **True etymological relatives:** Words sharing the same historical root.
- **Semantic relatives:** Words sharing a similar meaning but having different origins.

There are very few reliable direct derivatives of *bogus*. Therefore, the following is an expanded semantic family organised around the central concept of falsification, deception and lack of authenticity.

# **VIRTUE — The Moral Excellence of Character**

WORD POWER • ETYMOLOGY • SEMANTIC EVOLUTION • UPSC VOCABULARY

## **1. VIRTUE**

**Pronunciation:** /ˈvɜːtʃuː/ (VUR-choo)\
**Part of Speech:** Noun\
**Root Family:** Latin *vir* (man) → *virtus* (manliness, excellence, courage, moral worth)

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

| Component    | Meaning                                    |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Latin *vir*  | Man, adult male                            |
| Latin *-tus* | A suffix forming an abstract noun or state |
| *virtus*     | Manliness, courage, strength, excellence   |

**Etymological root:** Latin *vir*, meaning man.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Originally, *virtus* meant **manliness or the qualities expected of an ideal man**, particularly courage, strength, bravery and excellence.

In ancient Roman society, these qualities were associated with the ideal citizen and soldier.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word underwent an interesting transformation:

**Stage 1: Ancient Rome**

*Virtus* = Manliness, courage, military bravery and excellence.

**Stage 2: Classical and Medieval Thought**

Excellence of character, moral goodness and conformity to ethical ideals.

**Stage 3: Modern English**

Moral excellence, goodness, integrity and a commendable quality of character.

**Important semantic observation:** The meaning gradually shifted from physical and martial excellence to ethical and moral excellence.

The modern meaning is no longer restricted to men. Virtue is a universal moral quality.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Virtue:** A morally good quality, behaviour or character trait that makes an individual ethically admirable.

It can also refer to moral excellence as a general quality.

Examples:

- Honesty is a virtue.
- Compassion is a virtue essential to public service.
- Integrity is one of the most important virtues of a civil servant.

**Conceptual distinction:**

- Virtue = A morally desirable quality.
- Vice = A morally undesirable quality.
- Virtuous = Possessing moral goodness.
- Virtuous conduct = Behaviour guided by ethical principles.

### **E. Common Usage (UPSC-Relevant)**

1. **Cardinal virtues** — The four classical virtues: prudence, justice, temperance and courage (fortitude).
2. **Civic virtues** — Qualities such as patriotism, tolerance, public spirit and respect for the rule of law that sustain democratic citizenship.
3. **Virtue ethics** — An ethical approach that emphasises moral character and the cultivation of virtues rather than merely rules or consequences.

**UPSC GS-IV connection:** Virtue ethics, probity, integrity, impartiality, compassion, objectivity and public service values.

# **2. THE ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY OF VIRTUE**

## **Understanding the root before learning the family**

The Latin root *vir* gave rise to several English words through different historical routes.

The most important distinction is:

- **Virtue family:** Words derived from Latin *virtus*.
- **Virile family:** Words derived from Latin *vir*, meaning man.
- **Virtuoso family:** Words that developed from the Italian form of *virtuoso*, historically connected to *virtù* (excellence).

Not every word that begins with *vir* belongs to this family. For example, *virus* comes from an unrelated Latin word.

We will organise the vocabulary into three groups.

The term originally described a person of exceptional knowledge, learning or accomplishments. Later, it became particularly associated with highly accomplished musicians and artists.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**As a noun:** A person with exceptional technical or artistic skill.

**As an adjective:** Demonstrating extraordinary mastery.

**Example:** A virtuoso administrator can coordinate complex governance challenges with exceptional competence.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Musical virtuoso** — An exceptionally skilled musician.
2. **Political virtuoso** — A highly accomplished political operator.
3. **Virtuoso performance** — A display of extraordinary skill.

**UPSC keyword:** Administrative competence.

## **5. Virtuism**

**Part of Speech:** Noun (rare)

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Virtue* = Moral excellence.
- *-ism* = Doctrine, practice or system.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

A doctrine or system centred on virtue.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The suffix *-ism* can create names for ideologies and systems of thought. However, *virtuism* is not an established mainstream term for virtue ethics and has limited standard usage.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A rare and non-standardised term occasionally used for an emphasis on virtue or virtuous conduct.

**Important:** Do not use *virtuism* as a substitute for the established academic term *virtue ethics* in UPSC answers.

### **E. Common Usage**

No widely established UPSC-relevant collocations.

**UPSC keyword:** Prefer *virtue ethics*.

# **GROUP II — WORDS DERIVED FROM THE LATIN ROOT VIR**

These words preserve the historical association with manhood, strength, vigour and masculine qualities.

## **6. Virile**

**Part of Speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- Latin *vir* = Man.
- Latin *-ilis* = Having the characteristics of or relating to.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Having the characteristics traditionally associated with an adult man.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The original association with masculinity gradually expanded to include physical strength, vigour, energy and forcefulness.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Possessing traditionally masculine qualities, particularly strength, energy and vigour.

**Example:** Effective leadership requires a combination of intellectual maturity and decisive action, rather than merely virile displays of authority.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Virile strength** — Traditionally masculine physical strength.
2. **Virile personality** — A personality characterised by masculine qualities.
3. **Virile energy** — Vigour and vitality.

**UPSC keyword:** Physical vigour.

*Note:* This word is gendered and often carries dated cultural associations. Use it carefully in contemporary writing.

## **7. Virility**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Vir* = Man.
- *-ility* = Quality or state.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The quality or state of being a man.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The term initially referred to masculinity and manhood. It later acquired a more specific association with male reproductive capacity and sexual potency.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

The condition of being traditionally masculine; also, male reproductive capacity or sexual potency.

**Example:** Historically, virility was often associated with masculine ideals of strength and authority.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Male virility** — Male sexual or reproductive capacity.
2. **Crisis of virility** — A perceived decline in traditional masculine identity.
3. **Virility symbols** — Symbols culturally associated with masculinity.

**UPSC keyword:** Gender stereotypes and social construction of masculinity.

## **8. Virilism**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Vir* = Man.
- *-ism* = Condition, practice or system.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

A condition or state associated with masculine characteristics.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The term developed in medical terminology to describe the development of male secondary sexual characteristics, particularly in females.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A medical term referring to the development of male-pattern secondary sexual characteristics in a female.

It is a relatively specialised and historically used term; contemporary clinical terminology often prefers *virilisation*.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Virilism in females** — Development of male-pattern characteristics.
2. **Signs of virilism** — Physical manifestations of androgen excess.
3. **Endocrine virilism** — Virilisation associated with hormonal disturbances.

**UPSC keyword:** Biological sex characteristics and endocrine disorders.

## **9. Virilisation**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Vir* = Man.
- *-ise* = To cause or make.
- *-ation* = Process or action.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The process of developing male characteristics.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The term entered medical vocabulary to describe the development of male secondary sexual characteristics, usually due to increased androgen exposure.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

The development of male-pattern physical or physiological characteristics, such as increased facial hair, deepening of the voice and increased muscle mass.

**Example:** Excessive androgen production may cause virilisation in females.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Foetal virilisation** — Development of male characteristics in a foetus.
2. **Virilisation symptoms** — Manifestations of androgen excess.
3. **Rapid virilisation** — Rapid development of male-pattern characteristics.

**UPSC keyword:** Endocrine physiology.

# **GROUP III — IMPORTANT EXTENDED VOCABULARY**

## **10. Virtually**

**Part of Speech:** Adverb

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Virtual* = Existing in essence or effect, though not necessarily in physical form.
- *-ly* = In a particular manner.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

In a manner that is practically or essentially equivalent to something.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word *virtual* developed from Latin *virtus*, through the idea of inherent power, efficacy or potential. Its meaning evolved towards something that is practically true or equivalent in effect.

Later, technological usage gave it the sense of digitally mediated or simulated environments.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

1. Almost or nearly.
2. In effect or for practical purposes.
3. Through a computer-mediated or simulated environment.

**Example:** Digital governance has made several public services virtually accessible to citizens.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Virtually impossible** — Almost impossible.
2. **Virtually every citizen** — Almost every citizen.
3. **Virtual meeting** — A meeting conducted through digital technology.

**UPSC keyword:** Digital governance and virtual institutions.

## **11. Virtual**

**Part of Speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- Latin *virtus* = Power, efficacy or excellence.
- Historical development through Medieval Latin *virtualis* = Effective or possessing power.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Possessing a particular effect or power, even without existing in the conventional physical form.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

Originally associated with inherent power or efficacy, the term gradually acquired the meaning of something that is practically equivalent to a real thing.

With technological development, it became associated with computer-generated or digitally mediated environments.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Existing or occurring in effect, though not necessarily in physical form; also, digitally simulated or mediated.

**Example:** Virtual courts can improve access to justice by reducing geographical barriers.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Virtual courts** — Judicial proceedings conducted through digital platforms.
2. **Virtual reality** — A computer-generated simulated environment.
3. **Virtual public infrastructure** — Digitally mediated infrastructure supporting public services.

**UPSC keyword:** Digital public infrastructure.

## **12. Virtually Certain**

**Part of Speech:** Fixed expression

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Virtual* = Practically equivalent to or true in effect.
- *Certain* = Definite or highly probable.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Almost equivalent to being certain.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The expression emerged from the modern meaning of *virtual*, where something is effectively true even if it is not absolutely or formally so.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Almost certain; extremely likely to happen.

**Example:** Without structural reforms, the fiscal burden is virtually certain to increase.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Virtually certain outcome** — An outcome that is almost guaranteed.
2. **Virtually certain failure** — An almost inevitable failure.
3. **Virtually certain to happen** — Extremely likely to occur.

**UPSC keyword:** Probabilistic reasoning.
