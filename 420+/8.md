## **1. PREROGATIVE**

**Pronunciation:** pri-ROG-uh-tiv\
**Part of Speech:** Noun\
**Origin:** Latin *praerogativa* → Latin *praerogare*\
**Core Idea:** A special right, privilege or power belonging to a particular person or authority.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

Break the word historically into its Latin elements:

| Component      | Meaning                                |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| *prae-*        | Before, in advance                     |
| *rogare*       | To ask, question or propose            |
| *praerogativa* | Something given precedence or priority |

**Memory Hook:** Think of someone who gets to act or decide before everyone else.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Originally, *praerogativa* referred to the privilege of being asked or consulted first.

In ancient Rome, the *centuria praerogativa* was the voting century selected to cast its vote first in certain assemblies.

Thus:

**First to be consulted → Priority → Special privilege.**

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The development of the word can be understood through four stages:

1. **Ancient Roman usage:** A particular voting group enjoyed the privilege of voting first.
2. **Generalisation:** The idea of precedence developed into the idea of a special privilege.
3. **Political and constitutional usage:** The word became associated with powers and privileges exercised by sovereigns and the Crown.
4. **Modern usage:** It denotes a special right, authority, discretion or privilege belonging to an individual, office or institution.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Prerogative** means a special right, privilege or power vested in a person or authority by virtue of their position, office or status.

It can refer to:

- **Constitutional authority:** A power constitutionally or legally assigned to an office.
- **Executive discretion:** A matter left to the judgement of an executive authority.
- **Personal autonomy:** Something that an individual has the right to decide independently.
- **Institutional privilege:** A special power or entitlement enjoyed by an institution.

**Important distinction:** A prerogative is not necessarily an unlimited or arbitrary power. In constitutional democracies, its exercise may be subject to constitutional conventions, statutory provisions and judicial review.

### **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

| Collocation               | Meaning / Context                                                                                                |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Royal prerogative**     | Traditional powers exercised by the British Crown, generally through ministers under constitutional conventions. |
| **Executive prerogative** | Special authority or discretion exercised by the executive within its constitutional or legal limits.            |
| **Sole prerogative**      | A matter falling exclusively within someone's authority or right to decide.                                      |

**UPSC-ready sentences:**

1. The appointment of the Prime Minister is a constitutional prerogative of the President, exercised in accordance with parliamentary conventions.
2. National security cannot become an executive prerogative beyond the scrutiny of constitutional institutions.
3. The choice of an individual's religion is a matter of personal prerogative protected by the constitutional guarantee of freedom of conscience.

**Synonyms:** Privilege, entitlement, authority, discretion, exclusive right.

**Antonyms:** Obligation, compulsion, subordination, restriction.

# **2. THE ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY**

## **Understanding the root: ROG / ROGARE**

# **ROGARE**

## **To ask • To request • To propose**

**Central conceptual progression**

**ROGARE**

To ask or propose

**ROGATION**

Asking or proposing

**ABROGATION**

Taking away a law

**DEROGATION**

Partial reduction

**INTERROGATION**

Asking questions

**Etymological caution:** These words belong to the Latin *rogare* family, but they do not all descend directly from *prerogative*. Their prefixes and historical developments produce different meanings. Also, *rogation* and *rogatory* are much less common in contemporary English than the major legal and political terms below.

## **WORD 1. ABROGATE**

**Pronunciation:** AB-ruh-gayt\
**Part of Speech:** Verb

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

| Component | Meaning                  |
| --------- | ------------------------ |
| *ab-*     | Away, off                |
| *rogare*  | To propose, ask or enact |

### **B. Literal Meaning**

To take away or revoke something previously established.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

Latin *abrogare* referred to repealing or cancelling a law through a formal legislative process.

The meaning subsequently expanded to include formally abolishing an established rule, agreement or authority.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

To formally repeal, annul or abolish a law, rule, agreement or established arrangement.

**Key distinction:**

- Repeal: To revoke a law through legislative action.
- Abrogate: To formally abolish or annul a law, treaty, right or arrangement.
- Revoke: To withdraw an order, permission or privilege.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Abrogate a treaty** – formally terminate or annul a treaty.
2. **Abrogate constitutional provisions** – formally abolish constitutional provisions.
3. **Abrogate an agreement** – annul an existing agreement.

**UPSC Application:**

The unilateral abrogation of an international treaty may raise questions concerning international law, treaty obligations and state responsibility.

## **WORD 2. DEROGATE**

**Pronunciation:** DER-uh-gayt\
**Part of Speech:** Verb

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

| Component | Meaning                  |
| --------- | ------------------------ |
| *de-*     | Away, down or from       |
| *rogare*  | To propose, ask or enact |

### **B. Literal Meaning**

To take away from or diminish something.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

Latin *derogare* referred to partially repealing or modifying a law.

Unlike complete repeal, derogation historically involved reducing or restricting the scope of an existing legal provision.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

To detract from, diminish or depart from an established right, standard, obligation or legal provision.

It is especially important in constitutional and international human rights law.

**Important distinction:**

Derogation does not necessarily mean complete abolition. It can mean a temporary or limited departure from an obligation.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Derogation from fundamental rights** – a legally recognised departure from certain rights under specified conditions.
2. **Derogation from international obligations** – a permissible or claimed departure from treaty obligations.
3. **Without derogating from** – without diminishing or affecting something.

**UPSC Application:**

Article 4 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) permits certain derogations during officially proclaimed public emergencies, subject to strict conditions.

## **WORD 3. INTERROGATE**

**Pronunciation:** in-TER-uh-gayt\
**Part of Speech:** Verb

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

| Component | Meaning        |
| --------- | -------------- |
| *inter-*  | Between, among |
| *rogare*  | To ask         |

### **B. Literal Meaning**

To ask questions, particularly through a systematic process of inquiry.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

Latin *interrogare* meant to question or inquire.

The term developed into a word associated with formal questioning, especially in legal, investigative and administrative settings.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

To question someone systematically to obtain information, investigate facts or test the validity of a claim.

It can also mean critically examining an idea, assumption or social structure.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Police interrogation** – formal questioning during an investigation.
2. **Interrogate assumptions** – critically examine underlying beliefs.
3. **Interrogate evidence** – examine evidence for accuracy, reliability and consistency.

**UPSC Application:**

A democratic society must interrogate institutional practices that perpetuate social exclusion and structural inequality.

**Anthropology connection:** Interrogating ethnocentric assumptions is essential for developing cultural relativism.

## **WORD 4. INTERROGATION**

**Pronunciation:** in-ter-uh-GAY-shun\
**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *inter-* = between or among
- *rogare* = to ask
- *-ation* = process or action

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The process of asking questions.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The Latin idea of questioning evolved into the formal process of systematic inquiry, particularly in judicial and investigative contexts.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A formal and systematic questioning process intended to obtain information, investigate an incident or establish facts.

It can also refer to critical intellectual examination.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Custodial interrogation** – questioning of a person in police custody.
2. **Judicial interrogation** – questioning during judicial proceedings.
3. **Critical interrogation of power** – systematic examination of power structures.

**UPSC Application:**

Safeguards against custodial violence are essential to ensure that interrogation procedures comply with constitutional guarantees and human rights standards.

## **WORD 5. ROGATION**

**Pronunciation:** roh-GAY-shun\
**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *rogare* = to ask or request
- *-ation* = process or action

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The act of asking or making a request.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

In Roman political and legal language, a *rogatio* was a proposal submitted to the people, particularly a proposed law.

The word subsequently acquired religious and ceremonial associations in English.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A formal request, proposal or petition; also, in historical and religious usage, a public supplication or prayer.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Rogation days** – Christian days traditionally devoted to prayers for agricultural prosperity.
2. **Legislative rogation** – historical reference to a proposed law in Roman political practice.
3. **Formal rogation** – a formal request or petition (rare usage).

**UPSC relevance:** Mainly useful for understanding the historical evolution of Roman legislative institutions rather than contemporary constitutional vocabulary.

## **WORD 6. PREROGATIVE**

We have already studied this word, but its place in the family deserves emphasis.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *prae-* = before
- *rogare* = to ask

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The privilege of being asked or consulted first.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

First voting privilege → precedence → special privilege → exclusive right or authority.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A special right, power or privilege belonging to a person or institution.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Royal prerogative**
2. **Executive prerogative**
3. **Personal prerogative**

## **WORD 7. PREROGATIVE POWER**

**Part of Speech:** Legal and constitutional expression

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *prae-* = before
- *rogare* = to ask
- *power* = authority or capacity to act

### **B. Literal Meaning**

An authority associated with a special or privileged position.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The historical special privileges of the sovereign developed into a recognised category of executive authority in the British constitutional system.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

In British constitutional law, prerogative powers are residual powers historically belonging to the Crown, now generally exercised by government ministers.

**Indian constitutional distinction:** India does not simply replicate the British Crown's royal prerogative system. Executive authority is distributed and regulated by the Constitution.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Exercise of prerogative powers**
2. **Limits on prerogative powers**
3. **Judicial review of executive action**

**UPSC Application:** Constitutional supremacy means that executive authority must operate within constitutional boundaries.

## **WORD 8. DEROGATION**

**Pronunciation:** der-uh-GAY-shun\
**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *de-* = away or down
- *rogare* = to enact or propose
- *-ation* = process

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The act of taking away from or diminishing an established provision.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The Latin legal meaning of partial repeal developed into the modern legal concept of departure from an existing obligation or standard.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A partial or temporary departure from a legal obligation, right or provision, especially under exceptional circumstances.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Derogation from human rights obligations**
2. **Temporary derogation**
3. **Non-derogable rights**

**UPSC Application:**

The ICCPR recognises certain non-derogable rights, including the prohibition of torture and the right to life, subject to the precise wording and scope of its provisions.

## **WORD 9. ABROGATION**

**Pronunciation:** ab-ruh-GAY-shun\
**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *ab-* = away
- *rogare* = to propose or enact
- *-ation* = process

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The act of taking away an established law or provision.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The Latin legal concept of formally repealing a law evolved into the broader modern concept of abolishing a legal arrangement or established rule.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

The formal annulment, repeal or abolition of a law, treaty, agreement or legal arrangement.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Abrogation of a treaty**
2. **Abrogation of legal provisions**
3. **Unilateral abrogation**

**UPSC Application:**

The constitutional and political consequences of the 2019 changes concerning Article 370 can be discussed in the broader context of constitutional autonomy, federalism and the abrogation or modification of legal provisions.

Therefore, democratic institutions must continuously interrogate the exercise of state power to uphold constitutional morality and the rule of law.

# **WORD POWER & ETYMOLOGY: ENRAGED**



Emotional vocabulary

UPSC Mains + Essay

Etymological word family



## **1. ENRAGED**

Pronunciation: /ɪnˈreɪdʒd/ · Part of speech: Adjective (past participle of *enrage*)

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

The word **ENRAGED** consists of two components:

| Component | Meaning                                                  | Origin                                 |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| En-       | To make, cause to become, or put into a particular state | Old French *en-* / Latin *in-*         |
| Rage      | Intense anger, fury or uncontrolled passion              | Old French *rage*, from Latin *rabies* |

**Root connection:** Latin *rabies*, associated with madness, fury and intense anger, comes from *rabere*, meaning "to rave or be mad".

### **B. Literal Meaning**

**Enrage = To put someone into a state of rage.**

**Enraged = Having been driven into a state of intense anger.**

Think of the word as:

EN (to cause to become) + RAGE (intense anger) = ENRAGE (to make someone intensely angry).

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The evolution of the word can be understood through the following stages:

1. **Latin – Rabere:** To rave, be mad or behave irrationally.
2. **Latin – Rabies:** Madness, fury or violent agitation.
3. **Old French – Rage:** Intense anger, fury or violent passion.
4. **English – Enrage:** To provoke or make someone extremely angry.
5. **Modern English – Enraged:** A state of extreme anger, often triggered by perceived injustice, provocation or humiliation.

An interesting semantic development: the original association with madness and uncontrolled behaviour gradually became more specifically associated with intense human anger.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Enraged (adjective):** Extremely angry or furious, particularly because of something perceived as unjust, offensive or unacceptable.

It conveys a stronger emotional intensity than ordinary anger.

Compare:

| Word      | Degree / Nuance                                             |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Annoyed   | Slightly irritated                                          |
| Irritated | Experiencing displeasure                                    |
| Angry     | Feeling strong displeasure                                  |
| Furious   | Extremely angry                                             |
| Enraged   | Driven into intense, often uncontrollable anger             |
| Incensed  | Extremely angry, usually because of an injustice or offence |

### **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

1\. **Enraged public:** Citizens expressing extreme anger over an incident or government decision.

*Example:* The incident left the enraged public demanding accountability from the administration.

2\. **Enraged protesters:** Demonstrators whose anger has intensified because of a perceived injustice.

*Example:* Enraged protesters demanded judicial intervention against the alleged human rights violations.

3\. **Enraged by injustice:** Experiencing intense anger because of unfair treatment.

*Example:* Marginalised communities are often enraged by systemic discrimination and institutional apathy.

**UPSC usage:** Essay, GS-II (governance and accountability), GS-III (social unrest), GS-IV (justice, empathy and emotional intelligence).

# **2. THE ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY**

## **Understanding the ROOT: RAB-**

# **RABERE**

Latin root · To rave, be mad or behave furiously

**RABIES**

Latin noun: Madness / fury

**RAGE**

Old French route

**RABID**

Latin direct derivative

The two principal branches are connected through Latin *rabies*, but they developed through different routes into English.

The following vocabulary is arranged from the most useful words for UPSC to relatively specialised derivatives.

## **WORD 1. RAGE**

Very high UPSC utility

**Part of speech:** Noun and Verb

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Rage*: Derived from Old French *rage*, ultimately from Latin *rabies*.
- It does not have a meaningful modern English prefix or suffix to separate.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

A state of madness, fury or intense emotional agitation.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

- Latin *rabies*: Madness or fury.
- Old French *rage*: Violent anger or intense passion.
- Middle English *rage*: Extreme anger, violent excitement or uncontrolled behaviour.
- Modern English: Intense anger; also something that becomes extremely popular or fashionable.

The word has developed two important modern meanings: emotional fury and widespread popularity.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Rage (noun):** Intense, uncontrolled anger.

**Rage (verb):** To express intense anger or continue violently.

It can also describe a powerful social phenomenon or widespread trend.

### **E. Common Usage**

1\. **Public rage:** Widespread anger among citizens.

*Example:* Public rage over corruption exposed the growing crisis of institutional credibility.

2\. **Road rage:** Aggressive or violent behaviour by motorists arising from traffic-related anger.

*Example:* Road rage reflects the consequences of poor emotional regulation and inadequate civic discipline.

3\. **Rage against injustice:** Strong opposition to unfairness or oppression.

*Example:* The freedom struggle represented a collective rage against colonial exploitation.

**UPSC relevance:** Public movements, social unrest, civic behaviour, governance, social justice and Essay.

## **WORD 2. RABID**

High UPSC utility

**Part of speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Rabid*: From Latin *rabidus*, meaning mad, furious or raging.
- *Rabidus* originates from *rabere* (to rave or be mad).

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Mad, raging or affected by rabies.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

1. Latin *rabere*: To be mad or rave.
2. Latin *rabidus*: Mad, furious or raging.
3. Scientific usage: Associated with animals infected with rabies.
4. Figurative English: Describes extreme fanaticism, aggression or irrational enthusiasm.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Rabid (adjective):**

1. Literally, affected by rabies, a viral disease.
2. Figuratively, exhibiting extreme, fanatical or irrational enthusiasm, hostility or aggression.

The figurative meaning is particularly useful in political and ideological discussions.

### **E. Common Usage**

1\. **Rabid nationalism:** Extreme and often intolerant nationalism that may reject criticism or diversity.

*Example:* Democratic patriotism must be distinguished from rabid nationalism that undermines pluralism.

2\. **Rabid communalism:** Extreme communal hostility based on religious identity.

*Example:* Rabid communalism threatens constitutional secularism and social cohesion.

3\. **Rabid opposition:** Extremely hostile or aggressive opposition to a particular idea, policy or institution.

*Example:* Rabid opposition to institutional reforms can obstruct evidence-based policymaking.

**UPSC relevance:** GS-I (communalism), GS-II (secularism and democracy), Essay (nationalism, tolerance and pluralism).

**Important distinction:** *Rabid* is a strong, often pejorative word. Avoid using it casually to describe ordinary disagreement or legitimate political opposition.

## **WORD 3. RABIES**

Science + Public Health

**Part of speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Rabies*: From Latin *rabies*, meaning madness or fury.
- Derived from *rabere*, meaning to rave or be mad.

There is no productive English prefix-suffix division here.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Madness or a state of violent agitation.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

1. Latin *rabere*: To rave or behave irrationally.
2. Latin *rabies*: Madness or fury.
3. Medical terminology: The name of a fatal viral disease associated with neurological dysfunction and behavioural abnormalities.
4. Modern usage: A zoonotic viral disease transmitted primarily through the saliva of infected animals, usually through bites.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Rabies:** A vaccine-preventable viral zoonotic disease that affects the central nervous system and causes acute progressive encephalitis.

Once clinical symptoms appear, rabies is virtually always fatal.

It is an important public health concern, particularly in countries with substantial dog-mediated transmission.

### **E. Common Usage**

1\. **Rabies eradication:** Elimination of human deaths caused by dog-mediated rabies through coordinated interventions.

*Example:* India requires sustained investment in rabies prevention and elimination strategies.

2\. **Rabies vaccination:** Immunisation of animals or humans against rabies.

*Example:* Mass dog vaccination is a critical component of rabies control.

3\. **One Health approach to rabies control:** Coordinated action involving human health, animal health and environmental sectors.

*Example:* A One Health approach can strengthen surveillance, vaccination and post-exposure prophylaxis.

**UPSC relevance:** GS-II (public health), GS-III (science and technology, disease prevention), One Health, zoonotic diseases and SDG 3.

## **WORD 4. ENRAGE**

High utility

**Part of speech:** Verb

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *En-* = To cause to become.
- *Rage* = Intense anger.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

To make someone enter a state of intense anger.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The prefix *en-* developed from French and ultimately Latin *in-*, conveying the idea of putting something into a particular state.

When combined with *rage*, it produces the meaning of causing someone to become furious.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Enrage (verb):** To make someone extremely angry.

It emphasises the act or cause of provoking anger rather than the emotional condition itself.

### **E. Common Usage**

1\. **Enrage the public:** Provoke widespread public anger.

*Example:* Arbitrary administrative decisions can enrage citizens and undermine public trust.

2\. **Enrage marginalised communities:** Trigger intense anger among disadvantaged sections.

*Example:* Persistent discrimination can enrage marginalised communities and intensify demands for social justice.

3\. **Enrage political opponents:** Provoke extreme anger among opposing political groups.

*Example:* The controversial legislation enraged political opponents across the ideological spectrum.

**UPSC relevance:** Governance, public accountability, social justice and political polarisation.

## **WORD 5. ENRAGED**

**Part of speech:** Adjective / Past participle

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *En-* = To cause to become.
- *Rage* = Intense anger.
- *-ed* = Past participle marker, indicating the resulting state.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Someone who has been made intensely angry.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The verb *enrage* describes the process of causing anger; its past participle *enraged* describes the resulting emotional condition.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Extremely angry, furious or emotionally provoked.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Enraged citizens:** Citizens expressing intense dissatisfaction.
2. **Enraged demonstrators:** Protesters driven by intense anger.
3. **Enraged by corruption:** Made furious by corrupt practices.

**UPSC relevance:** Social movements, administrative accountability, public grievance redressal.

## **WORD 6. RAGING**

**Part of speech:** Verb form / Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Rage* = Intense fury or violent force.
- *-ing* = Indicates an ongoing action or a characteristic condition.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Continuing to rage, burn or act with extreme intensity.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word developed from the verb *rage* to describe continuous anger or violent activity. It subsequently acquired wider figurative applications for severe conflicts, diseases and natural phenomena.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Raging:**

1. Showing extreme anger.
2. Occurring with great intensity or violence.
3. Spreading or continuing aggressively.

### **E. Common Usage**

1\. **Raging conflict:** A violent and ongoing armed conflict.

*Example:* The raging conflict has generated a severe humanitarian crisis and mass displacement.

2\. **Raging inflation:** Rapid and persistent price increases.

*Example:* Raging inflation disproportionately affects low-income households by eroding their purchasing power.

3\. **Raging debate:** An intense and heated public or political controversy.

*Example:* The constitutional validity of the legislation has triggered a raging debate among legal scholars.

**UPSC relevance:** GS-II (international relations and constitutional debates), GS-III (inflation, conflict and economy).

## **WORD 7. RABIDITY**

Advanced vocabulary

**Part of speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- *Rabid* = Mad, furious or fanatical.
- *-ity* = A suffix denoting a state, quality or condition.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The state or quality of being rabid.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The adjective *rabid* developed into the noun *rabidity* through the addition of the suffix *-ity*, which converts an adjective into a noun describing its characteristic quality.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Rabidity:** The condition or quality of being rabid; figuratively, extreme fanaticism or irrational intensity.

It is a relatively uncommon word. In formal UPSC writing, *fanaticism*, *extremism* or *intolerance* will usually be more natural alternatives.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Political rabidity:** Extreme political fanaticism.
2. **Ideological rabidity:** Intolerant adherence to an ideology.
3. **Religious rabidity:** Extreme religious fanaticism.

**UPSC relevance:** Political extremism, ideological intolerance, democratic pluralism.

Break the word into:

- **UN-** = not; absence of; opposite of.
- **REQUITED** = repaid, returned or reciprocated.

The key to understanding this word is the verb **REQUITE**.

*To requite* means to return something received, whether it is love, kindness, hostility, assistance or an injury.

Therefore:

\\[ \text{UN}+\text{REQUITED}=\text{NOT RECIPROCATED} \\]

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Something that has not been returned, repaid or reciprocated.

For example:

- Love given but not returned.
- Kindness shown but not acknowledged or reciprocated.
- A service rendered without receiving an equivalent response.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The semantic development can be understood in three stages:

1. **Original sense – Requital:** Returning something received, particularly a favour, service or injury.
2. **Extended sense – Reciprocity:** Returning an emotion, action or treatment in response to another person's behaviour.
3. **Modern sense – Lack of reciprocation:** Something, especially love or affection, that remains unanswered.

The word gradually acquired a strong association with emotional relationships.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Unrequited (adjective):** Not returned, reciprocated or rewarded.

It is particularly associated with love, affection and emotional attachment that are not reciprocated by the other person.

**Examples:**

1. Unrequited love can cause considerable emotional distress.
2. His unrequited devotion to the cause did not diminish his commitment.
3. The citizens' unrequited expectations of justice gradually weakened their trust in institutions.

### **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

| Collocation          | Meaning                                                           |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unrequited love      | Love that is not reciprocated                                     |
| Unrequited affection | Affection that is not returned                                    |
| Unrequited devotion  | Dedication that receives no corresponding recognition or response |

**UPSC application:** The word is particularly useful in essays on human relationships, emotional intelligence, social psychology and literature. It is less suitable for describing ordinary administrative non-performance; use *uncompensated*, *unreciprocated* or *unrewarded* where appropriate.

# **2. THE EXTENDED WORD FAMILY**

The following vocabulary is arranged in three levels:

1. Direct derivatives of REQUITE.
2. Words developed from the QUIT/ACQUIT branch.
3. Words connected through the historical Latin root QUIETUS.

This distinction is important: words in the third category are etymologically related, but not necessarily direct derivatives of *requite*.

## **LEVEL I — Direct derivatives of REQUITE**

### **1. REQUITE (Verb)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- RE- = back; in return.
- QUITE = an obsolete form associated with *quit*, meaning to discharge or repay.

**Literal Meaning**

To give back or repay something received.

**Semantic Evolution**

Repayment of a debt → return of a favour or injury → reciprocation of feelings or actions.

**Modern Meaning**

To return something, especially a favour, kindness, service, love or injury, with an equivalent response.

**Common Usage**

- Requite someone's kindness.
- Requite a favour.
- Requite an injury.

**Example:** A welfare-oriented state should requite the sacrifices made by its citizens through equitable public services.

Word class: Verb | Formal usage

### **2. REQUITAL (Noun)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- REQUITE = repay or return.
- -AL = suffix used to form nouns denoting an action, process or result.

**Literal Meaning**

The act or result of repaying something.

**Semantic Evolution**

Repayment of an obligation → return for a service or favour → reward or retaliation.

**Modern Meaning**

An act of repayment, recompense or retaliation for something received, whether good or bad.

**Common Usage**

- Just requital for one's efforts.
- Requital for past injustices.
- Reward and requital.

**Example:** Social justice requires appropriate requital for historical injustices suffered by marginalised communities.

Word class: Noun | Formal/literary

### **3. REQUITER (Noun)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- REQUITE = repay or reciprocate.
- -ER = one who performs an action.

**Literal Meaning**

One who returns or repays something.

**Semantic Evolution**

A person who repays an obligation → someone who returns a favour or responds to an action.

**Modern Meaning**

A person who requites or reciprocates something. This is a rare, largely literary word.

**Common Usage**

- A requiter of kindness.
- A requiter of favours.
- A requiter of injuries.

**Example:** The term is more appropriate for literary analysis than contemporary administrative writing.

Word class: Noun | Rare

### **4. REQUITABLE (Adjective)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- REQUITE = repay or return.
- -ABLE = capable of being; suitable for.

**Literal Meaning**

Capable of being repaid or reciprocated.

**Semantic Evolution**

Something that can be returned → something for which an equivalent response can be made.

**Modern Meaning**

Capable of being requited or repaid. It is an uncommon word.

**Common Usage**

- A requitable favour.
- A requitable kindness.
- A requitable obligation.

**Example:** Not every sacrifice made in public service is requitable through monetary compensation.

Word class: Adjective | Rare

### **5. REQUITABLE / UNREQUITABLE DISTINCTION**

**UNREQUITABLE (Adjective)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- UN- = not.
- REQUITABLE = capable of being repaid or reciprocated.

**Literal Meaning**

Not capable of being repaid or reciprocated.

**Semantic Evolution**

An obligation or favour that cannot be adequately returned → something beyond ordinary repayment.

**Modern Meaning**

Not capable of being requited; a rare word, often replaceable by *irreparable*, *indebtedness that cannot be repaid* or *inexpressible gratitude*, depending on context.

**Common Usage**

- An unrequitable debt of gratitude.
- Unrequitable kindness.
- An unrequitable sacrifice.

**Example:** The sacrifices of freedom fighters created a moral debt that subsequent generations can never fully repay.

Word class: Adjective | Very rare

### **6. REQUITING (Verb/Noun)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- REQUITE = repay or reciprocate.
- -ING = indicates an ongoing action; can also form a gerund.

**Literal Meaning**

The act of returning something received.

**Semantic Evolution**

The process of repayment → the act of reciprocating a favour, feeling or injury.

**Modern Meaning**

The ongoing act of requiting something.

**Common Usage**

- Requiting kindness.
- Requiting love.
- Requiting an injury.

**Example:** Requiting violence with violence can perpetuate cycles of conflict rather than establish lasting peace.

Word class: Verb form / Gerund | Formal

### **7. UNREQUITED (Adjective)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- UN- = not.
- REQUITED = repaid or reciprocated.

**Literal Meaning**

Not returned or reciprocated.

**Semantic Evolution**

An unpaid or unreturned obligation → an unreciprocated action or feeling → particularly, love that is not returned.

**Modern Meaning**

Not reciprocated, especially in matters of love, affection or devotion.

**Common Usage**

- Unrequited love.
- Unrequited affection.
- Unrequited devotion.

**Example:** Unrequited expectations of social justice can generate alienation and political discontent.

Word class: Adjective | Common

## **LEVEL II — The QUIT / ACQUIT Branch**

These words belong to the broader historical family of *quit*, which is connected to the development of *requite*. This branch is particularly valuable for UPSC because it contains legal, constitutional and administrative vocabulary.

### **8. QUIT (Verb)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- QUIT = an independent verb derived through Old French *quiter*.
- Historically associated with being free or discharged from an obligation.

**Literal Meaning**

To discharge, release or free oneself from something.

**Semantic Evolution**

To settle a debt or obligation → to relinquish something → to leave a place, position or activity.

**Modern Meaning**

To leave, abandon, relinquish or stop doing something.

**Common Usage**

- Quit an office.
- Quit a job.
- Quit smoking.

**Example:** Public servants must not quit their constitutional responsibilities when confronted with political pressure.

Word class: Verb | Common

### **9. QUITCLAIM (Noun / Verb)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- QUIT = release or relinquish.
- CLAIM = a legal assertion of a right or title.

**Literal Meaning**

To relinquish or release a claim to something.

**Semantic Evolution**

Release of a legal claim → formal relinquishment of an interest or title in property.

**Modern Meaning**

A legal instrument or act by which a person releases whatever interest or claim they may have in a property, without necessarily guaranteeing that the title is valid.

**Common Usage**

- Quitclaim deed.
- Quitclaim transfer.
- Execute a quitclaim.

**Example:** A quitclaim deed may be used to transfer an existing interest in property, but it does not provide the same title guarantees as a warranty deed.

Word class: Noun / Verb | Legal terminology

### **10. ACQUIT (Verb)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- AC- = a variant of AD-, meaning to or towards.
- QUIT = discharge or release.

**Literal Meaning**

To discharge someone from an accusation or legal obligation.

**Semantic Evolution**

Release from a legal charge → declaration of innocence in a criminal proceeding.

**Modern Meaning**

To find or declare a person not guilty of a criminal offence.

**Common Usage**

- Acquit an accused person.
- Acquit someone of criminal charges.
- Be acquitted by a court.

**Example:** The principle of presumption of innocence requires that an accused person be treated as innocent until proven guilty.

**UPSC Legal Connection:** Article 21 of the Constitution protects life and personal liberty, while criminal procedure and evidentiary safeguards help ensure fair trials. Acquittal is a judicial outcome, not necessarily a declaration that the alleged incident never occurred.

Word class: Verb | Legal/Constitutional vocabulary

### **11. ACQUITTAL (Noun)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- ACQUIT = declare free from a criminal charge.
- -AL = suffix forming a noun denoting an action or result.

**Literal Meaning**

The act or result of being discharged from an accusation.

**Semantic Evolution**

Release from an accusation → formal judicial finding of not guilty.

**Modern Meaning**

A court's decision that an accused person is not guilty of the offence charged.

**Common Usage**

- Acquittal of the accused.
- Acquittal in a criminal case.
- Acquittal on benefit of doubt.

**Example:** A high acquittal rate may warrant examination of the quality of investigation, prosecution and evidence, rather than automatically indicating judicial inefficiency.

Word class: Noun | High UPSC relevance

### **12. ACQUITTANCE (Noun)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- ACQUIT = discharge or release.
- -ANCE = suffix indicating an action, condition or result.

**Literal Meaning**

The state or act of being discharged from an obligation.

**Semantic Evolution**

Discharge from a debt or liability → written acknowledgement that an obligation has been fulfilled.

**Modern Meaning**

A formal receipt or document acknowledging that a debt or obligation has been paid or discharged.

**Common Usage**

- An acquittance receipt.
- Full acquittance of a debt.
- An acquittance for payment.

**Example:** Documentary evidence of acquittance helps establish that a financial obligation has been discharged.

Word class: Noun | Legal/Financial vocabulary | Uncommon

### **13. QUIT (Adjective — historical/formal sense)**

**Meaning of the Components**

\- QUIT = discharged, free or released.

**Literal Meaning**

Free from an obligation or liability.

**Semantic Evolution**

Being free from a debt → being cleared of an obligation → being free of responsibility for something.

**Modern Meaning**

The adjective is now largely obsolete or archaic, although the idea survives in expressions such as *be quit of something*.

**Common Usage**

- Be quit of a debt.
- Be quit of an obligation.
- Be quit of a burden.

**Example:** In historical legal texts, a person described as quit of a debt was considered discharged from that obligation.

Word class: Adjective | Archaic

## **LEVEL III — The QUIETUS / QUIET Branch**

The next group shares the deeper historical connection with Latin *quietus*, meaning free, at rest or discharged.

Here the semantic emphasis shifts from repayment and discharge towards calmness, rest, silence and the absence of disturbance.

### **14. QUIET (Adjective / Noun / Verb)**

**Meaning of the Components**

\- QUIET = from Latin *quietus*, meaning at rest, calm or free from disturbance.

**Literal Meaning**

A state of rest or freedom from disturbance.

**Semantic Evolution**

Physical rest → absence of noise → mental calmness or social tranquillity.

**Modern Meaning**

Free from noise, disturbance, agitation or conflict.

**Common Usage**

- Social peace and quiet.
- Maintain public order and quiet.
- Quiet diplomacy.

**Example:** Quiet diplomacy can sometimes help resolve international disputes without escalating public confrontation.

**UPSC Application:** International relations, conflict resolution, internal security and public order.

Word class: Adjective / Noun / Verb | Common

### **15. QUIETUDE (Noun)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- QUIET = calm or stillness.
- -TUDE = suffix forming abstract nouns that express a state or condition.

**Literal Meaning**

The state or condition of being quiet.

**Semantic Evolution**

Absence of disturbance → mental tranquillity → a state of peaceful stillness.

**Modern Meaning**

A condition of calmness, tranquillity or peacefulness.

**Common Usage**

- Mental quietude.
- Peaceful quietude.
- Contemplative quietude.

**Example:** Democratic institutions require an environment of political stability and social quietude, without suppressing legitimate dissent.

Word class: Noun | Literary/Academic

### **16. QUIETISM (Noun)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- QUIET = stillness or passivity.
- -ISM = doctrine, belief, practice or ideology.

**Literal Meaning**

A doctrine or philosophy of stillness.

**Semantic Evolution**

Spiritual withdrawal and contemplative stillness → a religious doctrine emphasising passive contemplation and detachment.

**Modern Meaning**

A religious or philosophical doctrine that emphasises inward contemplation, passivity and detachment from active worldly concerns.

**Common Usage**

- Religious quietism.
- Mystical quietism.
- The doctrine of quietism.

**Example:** Excessive quietism in the face of social injustice can raise questions about the relationship between spirituality and social responsibility.

**UPSC Application:** Ethics, religion, social reform, Bhakti movements and the relationship between individual spirituality and collective action.

Word class: Noun | Academic/Religious terminology

### **17. QUIETIST (Noun / Adjective)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- QUIET = stillness or passivity.
- -IST = a person who follows or advocates a doctrine.

**Literal Meaning**

A follower or advocate of quietism.

**Semantic Evolution**

A person pursuing contemplative stillness → an adherent of a doctrine emphasising spiritual passivity.

**Modern Meaning**

A person who advocates or follows quietism.

**Common Usage**

- Religious quietist.
- Quietist philosophy.
- Quietist tradition.

**Example:** A quietist approach to social problems differs from reformist traditions that advocate active intervention.

Word class: Noun / Adjective | Academic terminology

### **18. QUIETUS (Noun)**

**Meaning of the Components**

\- QUIETUS = Latin word meaning at rest, free or discharged.

**Literal Meaning**

A state of rest or release from an obligation.

**Semantic Evolution**

Discharge from a debt or obligation → final settlement or release → figuratively, an end or conclusion.

**Modern Meaning**

A final settlement, release or conclusion; sometimes used to refer to death or the ending of something.

**Common Usage**

- Give something its quietus.
- Put the quietus on a controversy.
- The quietus of an old institution.

**Example:** Institutional reforms may give the quietus to outdated administrative practices.

Word class: Noun | Literary/Formal

### **19. QUIETEN (Verb)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- QUIET = calm or still.
- -EN = suffix used to form verbs meaning to make or become.

**Literal Meaning**

To make quiet or become quiet.

**Semantic Evolution**

To reduce physical noise → to calm agitation → to reduce tension or disturbance.

**Modern Meaning**

To make something quieter, calmer or less intense.

**Common Usage**

- Quieten public unrest.
- Quieten political tensions.
- Quieten public anxiety.

**Example:** Transparent communication can help quieten public anxiety during a public health emergency.

Word class: Verb | Common

### **20. QUIETLY (Adverb)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- QUIET = calm or without disturbance.
- -LY = suffix forming adverbs.

**Literal Meaning**

In a quiet or calm manner.

**Semantic Evolution**

Without making noise → without attracting attention → discreetly or without public display.

**Modern Meaning**

In a calm, silent or discreet manner.

**Common Usage**

- Work quietly.
- Quietly negotiate.
- Quietly implement reforms.

**Example:** Administrative reforms are often most effective when implemented through sustained institutional changes rather than symbolic announcements.

Word class: Adverb | Common

### **21. QUIETENING (Noun / Verb form)**

**Meaning of the Components**

- QUIET = calmness or absence of disturbance.
- -EN = to make or become.
- -ING = ongoing action or process.

**Literal Meaning**

The process of making something quiet.

**Semantic Evolution**

Reduction of noise → reduction of tension or agitation → calming of a disturbed situation.

**Modern Meaning**

The process of reducing noise, tension, anxiety or unrest.

**Common Usage**

- Quieten the unrest.
- Quieten public concerns.
- Quieten political tensions.

**Example:** Effective grievance redressal mechanisms contribute to the quietening of administrative discontent.

# **5. IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS AND COMMON CONFUSIONS**

These distinctions are particularly useful for precise answer writing.

| Word         | Meaning                             | Do not confuse with |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Unrequited   | Not reciprocated                    | Unrewarded          |
| Unrequitable | Incapable of being repaid           | Unrequited          |
| Requital     | Return or repayment                 | Requisition         |
| Acquit       | Declare not guilty                  | Quit                |
| Acquittal    | Judicial finding of not guilty      | Acquittance         |
| Acquittance  | Evidence of discharge of a debt     | Acquittal           |
| Quietude     | State of calmness                   | Quietism            |
| Quietism     | Doctrine of contemplative passivity | Quietude            |

**Three particularly important distinctions:**

1\. **Unrequited vs Unrewarded:** Unrequited refers to something not reciprocated; unrewarded refers to something for which no reward is received.

2\. **Acquittal vs Acquittance:** Acquittal belongs primarily to criminal justice; acquittance belongs primarily to the discharge of financial or other obligations.

3\. **Quietude vs Quietism:** Quietude is a state of tranquillity; quietism is a philosophical or religious doctrine.

**Etymological trap:** *Require, requisition, requisite and requisitionary* belong to a different Latin family, deriving from *requirere* (to seek or ask for). They are not derivatives of *requite* or *quietus*.&#x20;
