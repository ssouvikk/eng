# **1. OWING**

**Pronunciation:** /ˈəʊ.ɪŋ/ (British), /ˈoʊ.ɪŋ/ (American)

**Part of Speech:** Adjective; also used in the fixed prepositional expression *owing to*.

## **A. Meaning of the Components**

The word *owing* comes from the verb **owe**, which can be traced to Old English *āgan*.

- **āgan (Old English):** To possess, own, or have something as one's property.
- **owe (Modern English):** To be under an obligation to pay, repay, or give something to someone.

An interesting semantic shift occurred: the original idea of *having or possessing* gradually developed into the idea of *having an obligation*.

## **B. Literal Meaning**

The original idea behind *owe* was **to possess or have something**.

However, its meaning evolved into having something that one is obligated to return or repay.

Thus:

**Owing = Being under an obligation; being due or unpaid.**

In the expression *owing to*, the meaning shifts towards something happening because of a particular reason.

## **C. Semantic Evolution**

1. **Old English: āgan**

   To possess, own, or have.
2. **Middle English: owen**

   To possess something or be under an obligation.
3. **Later development: owe**

   To be indebted or obligated to someone.
4. **Modern English: owing**

   Something that remains due, or something attributable to a particular cause.

## **D. Modern Meaning**

The word *owing* has two important meanings:

**1. Indebted or unpaid**

Something that is due to another person or entity but has not yet been paid or fulfilled.

Example: The government cleared its owing payments to contractors.

**2. Because of; attributable to (owing to)**

Used to indicate the cause or reason behind an event, condition, or outcome.

Example: Economic growth slowed owing to declining private investment.

**Important distinction:**

- *Owing to* = Because of.
- *Due to* = Caused by / attributable to.
- *Owed to* = Belonging to someone as a debt or obligation.

## **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

| Collocation                          | Meaning                                    |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Owing to economic constraints        | Because of economic limitations            |
| Owing to administrative inefficiency | Because of poor administrative functioning |
| Outstanding amounts owing            | Payments that remain unpaid                |

**UPSC Mains application:**

"Despite considerable policy interventions, the implementation deficit persists, owing to institutional weaknesses, inadequate administrative capacity and poor interdepartmental coordination."

# **3. DETAILED WORD FAMILY**

## **1. Owe**

**Part of Speech:** Verb

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

Derived from Old English *āgan*, meaning to possess or have.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

To have something that creates an obligation towards another person.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The original meaning of possession gradually shifted towards the idea of obligation, especially the obligation to repay money or return something.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

To be under a legal, financial, moral or social obligation to someone.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Owe a debt** – To be financially indebted.
2. **Owe an explanation** – To be obligated to explain something.
3. **Owe allegiance** – To be bound by loyalty or commitment.

**UPSC application:**

"The state owes its citizens an obligation to ensure dignity, equality and social justice."

## **2. Owed**

**Part of Speech:** Verb (past tense and past participle of owe); adjective.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

Owe + past-tense/past-participle formation *-ed*.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Something that is under an obligation to be given, paid or fulfilled.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The past participle came to describe an outstanding obligation or an entitlement awaiting fulfilment.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Due to someone as a result of a financial, legal, contractual or moral obligation.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Wages owed to workers**
2. **Compensation owed to victims**
3. **Duties owed to citizens**

**UPSC application:**

"Compensation owed to displaced communities must be provided in a timely and transparent manner."

## **3. Owner**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

Historically associated with *own*, from Old English *āgan*, meaning to possess.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

A person who possesses something.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The idea of possession developed into a legal and social concept identifying the person who holds the rights over property or assets.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

An individual or entity having legal ownership or recognised control over an asset, property or business.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Landowner rights**
2. **Beneficial owner**
3. **Private property owner**

**UPSC application:**

"Landowners' rights must be balanced against the constitutional objective of equitable land distribution and public welfare."

## **4. Ownership**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- **Own:** Possess.
- **-ership:** A suffix indicating a state, condition or status, through the formation *owner + -ship*.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The state or condition of being an owner.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The concept expanded from simple possession to legally recognised rights, including the right to use, transfer, exclude others and derive benefits from property.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

The legal or recognised right to possess, control, use and transfer property or assets.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Land ownership patterns**
2. **Public ownership of natural resources**
3. **Community ownership of common resources**

**UPSC application:**

"Equitable ownership of productive assets is an important instrument for reducing structural economic inequality."

**GS linkage:** GS-I (land reforms), GS-II (governance), GS-III (inclusive growth).

## **5. Own**

**Part of Speech:** Verb, adjective and pronoun.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

Derived from Old English *āgen*, the past participle of *āgan*.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Possessed by oneself; belonging to oneself.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

From the historical idea of possession, it developed into a marker of personal possession, identity and individual control.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

To possess something; belonging exclusively or particularly to someone.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Own resources**
2. **Own-account enterprises**
3. **Own responsibility**

**UPSC application:**

"Local governments must mobilise their own resources to reduce excessive dependence on higher levels of government."

# **4. IMPORTANT EXTENSIONS: CONCEPTUAL AND ETYMOLOGICAL DISTINCTIONS**

The following words are particularly useful in UPSC vocabulary, but they should not be memorised as direct derivatives of *owe*. They belong to related semantic fields rather than the same etymological family.

## **6. Debt**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

From Old French *dette*, ultimately from Latin *debitum*, meaning something owed.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Something that is owed or due.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The concept developed from an obligation or liability into a formal financial relationship involving borrowing and repayment.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

A sum of money borrowed by an individual, organisation or government that must be repaid.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Public debt**
2. **External debt burden**
3. **Debt sustainability**

**UPSC application:**

"Maintaining debt sustainability is essential to preserve fiscal space for developmental expenditure."

Etymology: Related in meaning to owe, but not a direct derivative of the English root.

## **7. Indebted**

**Part of Speech:** Adjective

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- **In-:** In or under (in this formation, indicating a state).
- **Debt:** An obligation to repay.
- **-ed:** Indicates a state or condition.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Being in a state of debt.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

Originally associated with financial liabilities, it acquired a broader figurative meaning of being grateful or morally obligated.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

Owing money, gratitude or an obligation to another person or institution.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Highly indebted households**
2. **Indebted agricultural farmers**
3. **Indebted to constitutional values**

**UPSC application:**

"Small and marginal farmers often remain highly indebted due to income instability, rising input costs and inadequate institutional credit."

## **8. Indebtedness**

**Part of Speech:** Noun

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- **In-:** Under or in a state.
- **Debt:** Obligation.
- **-ed:** State.
- **-ness:** Condition or quality.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The condition of being in debt.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The term developed from individual borrowing to wider financial conditions involving households, businesses and governments.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

The state or degree of owing money or being financially obligated.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Rural household indebtedness**
2. **Agrarian indebtedness**
3. **Corporate indebtedness**

**UPSC application:**

"Persistent rural indebtedness reflects deeper structural problems such as fragmented landholdings, price volatility and inadequate access to affordable institutional credit."

**Core idea:** The condition of suffering or dying because of an inadequate supply of food or other essential nourishment.

### **1. Meaning of the Components**

The word **starvation** can be broken down historically as:

| Component | Meaning                                                     |
| --------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Starve    | To suffer or die from lack of food                          |
| -ation    | A suffix indicating an action, process, condition or result |

**Etymological root:** Old English *steorfan*, meaning 'to die'.

An important historical distinction: the original Old English verb *steorfan* meant simply 'to die', not specifically 'to die from hunger'.

### **2. Literal Meaning**

Starvation literally denotes the **condition or process of suffering or dying due to deprivation of nourishment**.

It is derived from:

\\[ \text{Starve} + \text{-ation} = \text{Starvation} \\]

### **3. Semantic Evolution**

The evolution of this word is particularly interesting.

1. **Old English: Steorfan**

   Originally meant 'to die' or 'perish'.
2. **Middle English: Sterven / Starven**

   Gradually acquired the more specific meaning of dying or suffering due to hunger.
3. **Modern English: Starve**

   Means to suffer severely or die from insufficient food.
4. **Modern English: Starvation**

   Refers to severe deprivation of food, the resulting physical condition and, in extreme cases, death.

**Conceptual transition:**

\\[ \text{Death} \rightarrow \text{Death from hunger} \rightarrow \text{Severe food deprivation} \\]

### **4. Modern Meaning**

**Starvation** refers to an extreme condition of food deprivation in which the body does not receive sufficient energy and nutrients to maintain normal physiological functioning.

It can be understood through three dimensions:

1. **Physiological:** Severe deficiency of energy and essential nutrients, leading to bodily deterioration.
2. **Socioeconomic:** A consequence of poverty, unemployment, food insecurity, conflict or unequal distribution of resources.
3. **Humanitarian:** An extreme manifestation of food crises, often associated with famine, displacement and conflict.

**UPSC distinction:** Starvation and famine are not synonymous.

- Starvation is an individual or population-level condition of severe food deprivation.
- Famine is a widespread and extreme food crisis involving large-scale deprivation, acute malnutrition and elevated mortality.

### **5. Common Usage: High-frequency Collocations**

| Collocation                 | Meaning / Context                                    |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Death by starvation         | Death resulting from extreme food deprivation        |
| Starvation deaths           | Deaths caused by insufficient food                   |
| Starvation and malnutrition | Severe food deprivation and nutritional deficiencies |

**UPSC-ready sentence:**

"Persistent poverty, livelihood insecurity and exclusion from social protection mechanisms can push vulnerable populations towards chronic food deprivation and, in extreme circumstances, starvation."

# **PART II — THE ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY**

Before proceeding, an important linguistic clarification:

The central historical root is **Old English STEORFAN**, which developed into modern *starve*.

Not every word that describes hunger, poverty or food deprivation belongs to this etymological family. We will distinguish:

- **Direct derivatives:** Words formed from *starve*.
- **Historical and morphological relatives:** Words connected through the same historical stem.
- **Semantic associates:** Related in meaning but not descended from the same root.

This distinction matters because vocabulary learning should not produce false etymological connections.

## **A. DIRECT DERIVATIVES OF STARVE**

### **1. STARVE**

Root verb

**Meaning of the Components**

- *Starve*: From Old English *steorfan*, meaning to die or perish.
- The modern meaning became specifically associated with hunger.

**Literal Meaning**

To perish or suffer because of inadequate nourishment.

**Semantic Evolution**

Originally meant simply to die; subsequently narrowed to dying or suffering from hunger.

**Modern Meaning**

To suffer severely from a lack of food or nourishment. It can also be used metaphorically to indicate deprivation of something essential.

**Common Usage**

1. Starve to death.
2. Starve for food.
3. Starve for attention / resources.

The condition of suffering or dying from hunger.

**Semantic Evolution**

Extreme thinness or wasting of the body, often due to prolonged malnutrition, illness or starvation.

**Common Usage**

1. Severe emaciation.
2. Signs of emaciation.
3. Emaciated children.

**UPSC application:**

"Severe acute malnutrition can manifest through wasting and visible emaciation, requiring immediate nutritional and medical intervention."

### **16. WASTING**

**Meaning of the Components**

- *Waste*: From Old French *waster*, ultimately from Latin *vastare*, meaning to devastate.
- *-ing*: An ongoing process or condition.

**Literal Meaning**

The process of deterioration or loss.

**Semantic Evolution**

Acquired a medical meaning referring to the loss of body tissue, particularly muscle and fat.

**Modern Meaning**

A condition characterised by low weight for height, usually indicating recent or acute undernutrition.

**Common Usage**

1. Child wasting.
2. Severe acute wasting.
3. Prevalence of wasting.

**UPSC application:**

"High levels of child wasting indicate acute nutritional stress and deficiencies in dietary intake or health conditions."

### **17. STUNTING**

**Meaning of the Components**

- *Stunt*: To hinder or prevent normal growth.
- *-ing*: The condition or process.

**Literal Meaning**

The process of restricting growth.

**Semantic Evolution**

Developed into a technical public-health indicator describing impaired linear growth in children.

**Modern Meaning**

Low height-for-age, generally resulting from chronic or recurrent undernutrition, illness and adverse environmental conditions.

**Common Usage**

1. Child stunting.
2. Chronic growth stunting.
3. Prevalence of stunting.

**UPSC application:**

"Childhood stunting reflects not merely inadequate food intake but also persistent deficits in maternal health, sanitation, healthcare and early childhood nutrition."

**Remember:**

- Stunting = Low height for age.
- Wasting = Low weight for height.
- Underweight = Low weight for age.

### **3. Semantic Evolution**

The word developed through the combination of two familiar spatial concepts:

1. **Back** established the location: the rear portion of something.
2. **Yard** established the nature of the space: an enclosed or open area adjoining a building.
3. **Backyard** consequently referred to the open space behind a house.
4. Its meaning subsequently expanded metaphorically to describe an area close to a person, institution, or country, particularly one regarded as belonging to its sphere of influence.

Thus, a physical location gradually acquired a political and geopolitical meaning.

### **4. Modern Meaning**

**A. Literal meaning**

An area of land behind a house, usually used for gardening, recreation, or household activities.

Example: The family grows vegetables in its backyard.

**B. Figurative meaning**

An area close to a particular country, institution, or organisation, especially one where it exercises considerable influence.

Example: A powerful country may seek to maintain strategic influence in its geopolitical backyard.

**C. Political and geopolitical meaning**

The term can describe a neighbouring region that a powerful state considers part of its traditional sphere of influence.

Example: The Monroe Doctrine historically reflected the United States' desire to prevent European interference in its perceived geopolitical backyard.

**UPSC distinction:** The expression *geopolitical backyard* often carries a critical connotation because it can imply hegemonic attitudes, disregard for sovereignty, or the denial of smaller states' strategic autonomy.

## **PART II — THE ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY OF YARD**

# **&#x20;\*gardaz&#x20;**

PROTO-GERMANIC ROOT

## **ENCLOSURE / ENCLOSED SPACE**

**Old English**

*geard*

Enclosure, courtyard

**English**

*yard*

Enclosed land or open space

**Germanic-derived**

*garden*

Cultivated enclosed land

It is particularly important in demographic research, epidemiology, education and social sciences.

### **Common Usage**

2. **Cohort analysis** — Analysis of a group sharing a common characteristic or experience.
3. **Age cohort** — Individuals belonging to a particular age group.

**UPSC application:** Demographic dividend, population ageing, epidemiological research, social mobility and demographic transition.

Example: "Cohort analysis can help policymakers understand variations in educational attainment and labour-market participation across generations."

### **An important etymological distinction**

Do not confuse every word containing *yard* with the enclosure-related root.

**Yard has two historically distinct meanings in English:**

1. **Yard = Enclosed space:** Old English *geard*, related to Proto-Germanic *gardaz*. This is the sense in *backyard*, *farmyard* and *shipyard*.
2. **Yard = Unit of measurement:** Old English *gierd/gerd*, meaning a rod or measuring stick. This is the source of the modern measurement unit.

Consequently, *yardstick* belongs to the measurement-related sense, not the enclosure-related family.

Similarly, words such as *backbencher*, *backwater* and *backchannel* may share the element *back*, but they are not members of the *geard* etymological family.

# **SUBSISTENCE — Complete Etymological Analysis**

ROOT-BASED VOCABULARY • UPSC PREPARATION • PRELIMS + MAINS + ANTHROPOLOGY

**Central idea:** *Subsistence* means the condition of continuing to exist or the minimum resources required to stay alive. In economics and anthropology, it is especially important in expressions such as **subsistence agriculture, subsistence economy, subsistence farming and subsistence livelihood**.

The most useful way to remember this word is:

**SUBSISTENCE = the resources and conditions that enable continued existence.**

Its etymological family is built around the Latin verb *sistere*, meaning “to cause to stand, set or place”, and its compounds. The immediate ancestor of *subsistence* is Latin *subsistentia*, from *subsistere*.

## **1. SUBSISTENCE**

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

**Subsistence = Sub- + Sist + -ence**

| Component | Meaning                                                                                                                        |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Sub-**  | Under, beneath, or in support of; in this compound, it contributes to the sense of maintaining oneself or continuing to stand. |
| **Sist**  | From Latin *sistere*, associated with standing, being placed or continuing in a position.                                      |
| **-ence** | A noun-forming suffix indicating a state, condition or process.                                                                |

**Historical formation:** Latin *subsistere* = *sub-* + *sistere*. It meant “to stand firm, remain, withstand or continue to exist”. Latin *subsistentia* gave rise to the English noun *subsistence*.

**Important caution:** Do not mechanically translate *sub-* as “below” and conclude that *subsistence* literally means “standing below”. Prefixes develop different shades of meaning in compounds.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

**To subsist = to continue to exist or maintain oneself.**

**Subsistence = the state or means of continuing to exist.**

Think of it as:

**Basic necessities**

Food • Water • Shelter • Clothing • Essential income

**Subsistence**

**Continued existence**

Survival • Maintenance of life • Basic livelihood

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

1. **Latin *sistere*:** To cause to stand, place or set.
2. **Latin *subsistere*:** To stand firm, remain, withstand or continue to exist.
3. **Latin *subsistentia*:** The state or condition of continuing to exist.
4. **English *subsistence*:** The means or resources necessary to maintain life.
5. **Economic and anthropological usage:** The term came to describe livelihood systems primarily oriented towards meeting the basic needs of individuals or households rather than generating large marketable surpluses.

The semantic journey is:

**Standing / remaining → continuing to exist → maintaining life → means of survival → subsistence-based economy.**

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Subsistence** has two major meanings:

**1. General meaning:** The condition of continuing to exist or the means necessary for survival.

Example: *The family earns just enough for subsistence.*

**2. Economic and anthropological meaning:** A livelihood system or economic activity primarily directed towards meeting the basic consumption needs of the producer and their household.

Example: *Subsistence agriculture is primarily oriented towards household consumption rather than commercial sale.*

### **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

1. **Subsistence agriculture** — farming primarily intended to meet the food requirements of the farmer's household.
2. **Subsistence economy** — an economic system in which production is largely directed towards meeting basic needs, with limited surplus or market exchange.
3. **Subsistence livelihood** — a livelihood that provides only the resources necessary for basic survival.

**UPSC-ready sentence:**

> Subsistence agriculture continues to sustain a significant section of rural households, particularly where access to capital, irrigation, technology and remunerative markets remains limited.

## **6. SUBSISTENCE: IMPORTANT ECONOMIC AND ANTHROPOLOGICAL TERMINOLOGY**

The word *subsistence* has special importance in Anthropology and GS-III. These expressions are not all etymological derivatives of *subsist*, but they are essential conceptual extensions of the word.

### **6.1 Subsistence Agriculture**

Agriculture primarily undertaken to meet the consumption needs of the farmer's household.

**Key characteristics:**

- Small and fragmented landholdings in many contexts.
- Limited marketable surplus.
- High dependence on family labour.
- Vulnerability to monsoon variability and crop failure.
- Lower access to capital, irrigation and modern technology in many settings.

**UPSC linkage:** Food security, agrarian distress, small and marginal farmers, agricultural diversification and rural development.

### **6.2 Subsistence Farming**

A farming system in which the main objective is household consumption rather than commercial profit.

**Common forms:**

- Primitive subsistence farming.
- Intensive subsistence farming.

**Important distinction:** Subsistence farming does not necessarily mean that farmers never sell crops. A household may consume most of its output and sell the surplus.

### **6.3 Subsistence Economy**

An economic system in which production is primarily oriented towards meeting basic consumption requirements.

**Key features:**

- Production for direct use.
- Limited accumulation of surplus in many cases.
- Strong dependence on local natural resources.
- Household and community-based labour arrangements.
- Exchange through reciprocity and redistribution in some societies.

## **7. IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS FOR PRELIMS AND MAINS**

### **A. Subsistence vs Commercial Agriculture**

| Basis             | Subsistence agriculture                        | Commercial agriculture                           |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Primary objective | Household consumption                          | Market sale and profit                           |
| Production        | Mainly for own use                             | Mainly for the market                            |
| Surplus           | Usually limited, though not necessarily absent | Generally larger                                 |
| Market dependence | Relatively lower                               | Relatively higher                                |
| Scale             | Often small-scale                              | Can range from small to large                    |
| Technology        | Varies; often resource-constrained             | Often more market-oriented and capital-intensive |

**Prelims trap:** Subsistence agriculture does not mean zero market exchange. It means that household consumption is the primary orientation.

### **B. Subsistence vs Sustainable Livelihood**

| Subsistence                                    | Sustainable livelihood                                   |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Focuses on meeting immediate basic needs       | Focuses on long-term livelihood security                 |
| May involve survival with very limited surplus | Seeks resilience and capacity to withstand shocks        |
| Can remain vulnerable to disasters             | Emphasises adaptive capacity and resource sustainability |
| May not ensure upward mobility                 | Seeks improved well-being and livelihood opportunities   |

**Mains insight:** A policy that merely ensures subsistence may prevent starvation but may not eliminate poverty or create sustainable development.

### **C. Subsistence vs Sustainability**

These words may appear together, but their meanings differ.

- **Subsistence:** The ability or means to continue living.
- **Sustainability:** The ability to maintain a system or process over time without undermining its future viability.

Example:

> A farming system may provide immediate subsistence to a household but may not be environmentally sustainable if it depletes soil fertility and groundwater.
