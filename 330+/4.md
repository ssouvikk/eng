**Central idea:** TAR = a dark, thick, sticky substance obtained from wood or coal, historically used for waterproofing and preservation.

An important etymological clarification: **TAR is not a productive Latin or Greek combining root.** It is an inherited Germanic word. Consequently, its genuine word family is relatively small. We should not artificially include unrelated words merely because they contain the letters *tar*.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- **Tar:** A dark, thick, sticky substance produced by the destructive distillation of organic materials such as wood or coal.
- Its historical association is with wood-derived resinous substances.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

A sticky, dark substance used to coat, seal, waterproof or preserve materials.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

| Stage                  | Development                                                                                   |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Old Germanic languages | Words referring to resinous or tar-like substances                                            |
| Old English            | *Teoru* — tar, bitumen-like substance                                                         |
| Middle English         | *Ter / tar* — sticky waterproofing material                                                   |
| Modern English         | Tar derived from wood or coal, with industrial, constructional and environmental applications |

**Etymological origin:** Old English *teoru*, related to Old High German *ter* and Old Norse *tjara*.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Tar (noun):** A dark, viscous material produced by heating organic substances in the absence of sufficient oxygen.

Its applications include:

- Road construction and surfacing.
- Waterproofing and protective coatings.
- Industrial applications.
- Historical preservation of wooden ships and structures.

### **E. Common Usage**

1. **Coal tar:** A by-product of coal carbonisation, historically important in chemical industries.
2. **Tar-coated roads:** Roads treated with bituminous or tar-based materials.
3. **Tar extraction:** Recovery of tar through thermal processing of organic materials.

## **1. PROWESS**

Pronunciation: PROW-iss | Part of speech: Noun | Word family: Old French *proeche* / *prouesse*

**Meaning:** Exceptional skill, expertise, courage or ability demonstrated in performing a particular activity, especially one requiring strength, intelligence, strategy or competence.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

The word *prowess* does not have two independently functioning English components. It comes from the Old French word *proeche*, related to *prud* (valiant, brave, capable), which ultimately traces to the Latin *prodesse* (to be useful, benefit).

Its historical association is with:

- **Capability:** The ability to accomplish something.
- **Valour:** Bravery and courage, particularly in battle.
- **Excellence:** Demonstration of superior ability.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Originally, *prowess* conveyed the quality of being brave, capable and valiant.

Its earliest sense was closely associated with the bravery and martial excellence of a warrior.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

1. **Medieval usage — Valour**

   The bravery, courage and fighting ability of a knight or warrior.
2. **Intermediate usage — Superior capability**

   The meaning expanded beyond physical courage to include exceptional ability and competence.
3. **Modern usage — Demonstrated excellence**

   Today, it denotes exceptional proficiency in almost any field, including technology, administration, diplomacy, sports and intellectual pursuits.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Prowess = Exceptional ability + Demonstrated excellence.**

It generally implies a high degree of competence, often established through performance or achievement.

It is stronger than ordinary *skill* or *ability*.

**Example:**

India's growing technological prowess has strengthened its position in the global digital economy.

### **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

| Collocation           | Meaning                           |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Military prowess      | Exceptional military capability   |
| Technological prowess | Advanced technological competence |
| Diplomatic prowess    | Exceptional diplomatic skill      |

**Additional examples:**

- Economic prowess of emerging economies.
- Intellectual prowess of scientific institutions.
- Administrative prowess in crisis management.

### **F. Retention Technique**

Remember:

**PROWESS = PROficiency + EXcellence**

This is a mnemonic, not the actual etymology.

Think of a warrior displaying extraordinary courage and skill. Over time, the same idea expanded to describe excellence in every field.

**UPSC sentence:**

"India's growing economic and technological prowess provides it with greater strategic autonomy and enhances its influence in an increasingly multipolar world."

## **2. THE ETYMOLOGICAL WORD FAMILY OF PROWESS**

### **First, understand the root connection**

The historical word family is considerably smaller than that of a productive Latin root. Its central connection runs through Old French *prou* / *prud*, meaning **brave, valiant, good or capable**.

**ETYMOLOGICAL ROOT MAP**

**Late Latin *prode* / Vulgar Latin *prodem***

Advantageous, useful, profitable; proposed historical ancestry

**Old French *prou* / *prud***

**Old French *proesce* / *proece***

Bravery, excellence, valour

**Important etymological distinction:** *Prowess* does not come from the English construction PRO + WESS, and its ending is not the feminine suffix *-ess*. Also, *prudence* and *prudent* are not members of this family merely because they look similar. They have a different Latin etymology.&#x20;

# **FAMILY I — PROUD AND ITS DERIVATIVES**

## **3. PROUD**

Adjective | Pronunciation: PROWD

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- Old French *prud*: brave, valiant, excellent.
- Late Latin *prode*: advantageous, profitable.

There is no meaningful modern English prefix-suffix division.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Originally, it conveyed the idea of being excellent, brave, valiant or distinguished.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

1. **Original sense:** Brave, valiant and excellent.
2. **Medieval development:** Having an elevated status or a sense of honour.
3. **Modern development:** Feeling pleasure or satisfaction because of one's achievements, qualities or associations.
4. **Negative development:** Having an excessively high opinion of oneself; arrogant or haughty.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Proud = Feeling deep satisfaction or self-respect; alternatively, displaying excessive self-importance.**

Two important shades of meaning:

- Positive: Dignity, achievement and self-respect.
- Negative: Arrogance, conceit and excessive self-importance.

### **E. Common Usage**

| Collocation    | Meaning                                  |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| National pride | Collective sense of dignity and identity |
| Take pride in  | Feel satisfaction in an achievement      |
| Proud legacy   | Distinguished historical inheritance     |

**UPSC example:**

"India's scientific achievements have become a source of national pride and reflect the country's growing technological prowess."

## **4. PRIDE**

Noun | Pronunciation: PRYD

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

Derived from Old English *prȳte*, associated with *prūd* (proud).

It is historically related to the word *proud*, rather than being a combination of two independent English components.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

The quality or condition of being proud.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

1. Originally associated with excellence, distinction and high standing.
2. Developed into a sense of honour, dignity and self-respect.
3. Acquired a negative meaning of arrogance, vanity and excessive self-esteem.
4. Today, it can denote both a positive sense of dignity and a negative sense of conceit.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Pride = A feeling of satisfaction, dignity or self-respect arising from one's achievements, identity or associations; excessive self-esteem when used negatively.**

### **E. Common Usage**

| Collocation         | Meaning                                           |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| National pride      | Collective dignity and attachment to one's nation |
| Pride and prejudice | Excessive self-esteem and preconceived judgement  |
| Pride of place      | A position of special importance or prominence    |

**UPSC example:**

"Constitutional morality requires citizens to balance legitimate national pride with respect for diversity, pluralism and individual rights."

**Conceptual distinction:**

| Pride                                   | Prowess                           |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| A feeling or attitude                   | An ability or quality             |
| Relates to self-respect or satisfaction | Relates to exceptional competence |
| Can be positive or negative             | Generally positive                |

## **9. PRUDE**

# **FAMILY III — HISTORICAL AND LESS COMMON RELATIVES**

## **14. PROW (ARCHAIC)**

Adjective / Noun | Historical usage

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

Derived from Old French *prou*, meaning brave, valiant or capable.

It is historically related to *prowess* and *proud*.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

Valiant, brave or advantageous.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

In Middle English, *prow* appeared as an adjective meaning brave or valiant and as a noun conveying advantage or profit.

These senses have become obsolete.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Prow (archaic) = Brave, valiant or capable.**

It is not a normal word in contemporary UPSC writing.

### **E. Common Usage**

There are no meaningful high-frequency modern collocations. Recognise it mainly when reading historical or literary English.

# **4. Important Etymological Distinction: PROWESS vs PRUDENCE**

UPSC Vocabulary Trap

These words look similar, but they do not belong to the same etymological family.

| Word     | Origin                                | Meaning                                       |
| -------- | ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Prowess  | Old French *proesce*, from *prou*     | Exceptional ability or valour                 |
| Proud    | Old French *prud*                     | Having pride; self-respecting or arrogant     |
| Pride    | Old English *prȳte*, related to proud | Self-respect, satisfaction or arrogance       |
| Prude    | French *prude*                        | A person excessively concerned with propriety |
| Prudish  | Derived from prude                    | Excessively concerned with modesty            |
| Prudence | Latin *prudentia*                     | Careful judgement and foresight               |
| Prudent  | Latin *prudens*                       | Wise, cautious and judicious                  |

**Why is this important?**

*Prudence* comes from a different Latin word family associated with foresight and practical wisdom. Its similarity to *prude* and *prudish* is misleading.

For UPSC answer writing, remember:

- **Prowess:** Exceptional capability.
- **Pride:** Self-respect or arrogance.
- **Prudishness:** Excessive concern with propriety.
- **Prudence:** Wisdom and careful judgement.

## **1. SPRINKLING**

# **SPRINKLING**

**Pronunciation:** /ˈsprɪŋklɪŋ/\
**Part of speech:** Noun (also the present participle of *sprinkle*)\
**Root word:** Sprinkle\
**Core idea:** Scattering or distributing something in small quantities or at intervals.

### **A. Meaning of the Components**

- **Sprinkle:** To scatter or distribute small drops, particles or quantities over a surface or among something.
- **-ing:** A suffix indicating an action, process or its result.

*Historical note:* The word's formation is Germanic rather than a transparent combination of two independent modern words. The suffix *-ing* forms the noun referring to the action or process.

### **B. Literal Meaning**

To scatter something in small drops, particles or quantities.

Imagine sprinkling water over a plant or scattering a handful of seeds over a field.

### **C. Semantic Evolution**

The word developed from the physical act of scattering small particles or droplets to the broader idea of distributing something in small quantities.

**Semantic progression**

**Physical action**

Scattering water, dust or small particles.

**Distribution in small quantities**

Adding small amounts of something across a larger whole.

**Figurative application**

Including occasional or limited elements of an idea, quality or characteristic.

### **D. Modern Meaning**

**Sprinkling** refers to:

1. The act of scattering small quantities of a substance over something.
2. A small quantity or number of something distributed among a larger group.
3. The occasional inclusion of particular elements in a larger whole.

**Examples:**

- The sprinkling of rain provided temporary relief from the heat.
- The report contains a sprinkling of statistical errors.
- The policy document includes a sprinkling of progressive ideas but lacks structural reforms.

### **E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations**

| Collocation              | Meaning / Context                       |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| A sprinkling of rain     | A small amount of rainfall              |
| A sprinkling of dissent  | A few expressions of disagreement       |
| A sprinkling of evidence | A small quantity of supporting evidence |

**UPSC answer-writing application:**

"The policy incorporates a sprinkling of welfare-oriented provisions; however, its structural approach remains predominantly market-driven."

**Retention hook:** SPRINKLING = SMALL AMOUNTS SCATTERED ACROSS A LARGER WHOLE.

## **2. The Etymological Root: SPRINKLE → SPRENGE → SPRING**

Before building the word family, understand the historical connection.

The English word *sprinkle* is recorded in Middle English as *sprenklen* (late 14th century). It is associated with Middle Dutch *sprenkelen*, Low German *sprenkeln* and German *sprenkeln*.

One historical explanation connects it with Middle English *sprenge*, related to Old English *sprengan*, the causative form of *springan* (to spring, leap or burst forth).

The deeper Indo-European connection is less certain: etymological dictionaries suggest a possible Proto-Indo-European root *(s)preg-* meaning **to jerk or scatter**, also associated with Latin *spargere* (to scatter).&#x20;

## **20. Commonly confused expressions**

**A. Sparse vs Scarce**

- Sparse = Thinly distributed or low in density.
- Scarce = Insufficient in availability.

Example:

- Sparse population in mountainous regions.
- Scarce availability of drinking water.

**B. Dispersion vs Dispersal**

- Dispersion = The distribution pattern or extent of spread.
- Dispersal = The process of moving or spreading away.

Example:

- Population dispersion influences regional planning.
- Population dispersal may result from migration.

**C. Sprinkle vs Intersperse**

- Sprinkle = Distribute small quantities over something.
- Intersperse = Place different elements among other elements at intervals.

Example:

- Sprinkle water over the soil.
- Intersperse constitutional examples throughout an essay.

**D. Aspersion vs Assertion**

- Aspersion = A damaging or disparaging remark.
- Assertion = A confident statement or claim.

Example:

- Cast aspersions on someone's character.
- Make an assertion about constitutional morality.

# 1. COEFFICIENT

## Meaning of the Components

**Word:** Coefficient\
**Pronunciation:** /ˌkoʊ.ɪˈfɪʃ.ənt/\
**Origin:** Medieval Latin *coefficiens*, from *co-* + *efficere*.

Break the word into its historical components:

- **Co-** = together, jointly, in combination.
- **Efficere** = to accomplish, bring about, produce or achieve.
- **Facere** = to do or make (the underlying Latin root of *efficere*).

Notice the transformation:

**Co- + efficere → Coefficient**

## Literal Meaning

Something that works together in bringing about a result.

In other words, a contributing or operative factor.

## Semantic Evolution

The original idea of something jointly producing an outcome gradually acquired a more specialised mathematical meaning.

In mathematics, a coefficient is the numerical quantity that operates on a variable or another mathematical expression by multiplying it.

For example:

In **5x + 3 = 18**:

- 5 is the coefficient of x.
- x is the variable.
- 3 is the constant.

The number 5 determines the multiplicative contribution of x to the expression.

The term subsequently became important in statistics, economics, physics, chemistry and social sciences.

## Modern Meaning

**Coefficient (noun):**

1. Mathematics: A numerical or algebraic factor multiplying a variable or expression.
2. Statistics: A numerical value representing the magnitude and direction of a relationship or an estimated effect.
3. Science: A numerical quantity representing a particular property or relationship.

## Common Usage

1. **Correlation coefficient** – A statistical measure of the strength and direction of association between two variables.
2. **Regression coefficient** – A numerical estimate representing the expected change in the dependent variable for a unit change in an independent variable, holding other variables constant.
3. **Gini coefficient** – A statistical measure of income or wealth inequality.

**UPSC Application:**

- A high correlation coefficient does not necessarily establish causation.
- The regression coefficient helps estimate the impact of public expenditure on economic growth.
- The Gini coefficient is used to assess the extent of income inequality.

**Memory Hook:** A coefficient is a number that contributes to an outcome by multiplying another quantity. Think of it as a numerical contributor.

Historically, *disaffected* developed through the idea of being alienated or estranged in one's feelings or loyalties.

### Literal Meaning

Separated in feelings or loyalty.

### Semantic Evolution

The term came to describe people who have become dissatisfied, alienated or hostile towards an institution, authority or system.

### Modern Meaning

**Disaffected (adjective):** Dissatisfied, alienated or resentful, especially towards an established authority or institution.

### Common Usage

1. **Disaffected youth** – Young people alienated from mainstream institutions or social structures.
2. **Disaffected sections of society** – Groups experiencing dissatisfaction or alienation.
3. **Politically disaffected citizens** – Citizens who have lost confidence in political institutions or leadership.

**UPSC Application:** Persistent unemployment, social exclusion and lack of political representation may contribute to the alienation of certain sections of society.

## 30. MALFEASANCE

### Meaning of the Components

- **Mal-** = bad, wrong or evil.
- **Feasance** = doing or performance, from Old French *faisance*, associated with Latin *facere*.

### Literal Meaning

Wrongdoing or doing something improperly.

### Semantic Evolution

The term developed into a legal and administrative concept describing wrongful conduct, particularly by someone exercising official authority.

### Modern Meaning

**Malfeasance (noun):** Wrongful or unlawful conduct, particularly by a public official while exercising official responsibilities.

### Common Usage

1. **Official malfeasance** – Wrongful conduct by public officials.
2. **Corporate malfeasance** – Illegal or unethical conduct by corporate actors.
3. **Malfeasance in public office** – Misconduct involving the abuse of official authority.

**UPSC Application:** Institutional accountability, vigilance mechanisms and independent oversight help prevent malfeasance in public administration.

# GROUP G: RELATED WORDS WORTH REMEMBERING

## 31. SUFFICIENT

### Meaning of the Components

- Latin *sub-* = under or up to a required level.
- *Facere* = to do or accomplish.
- Latin *sufficere* = to supply, provide or be adequate.

### Literal Meaning

Enough to meet a requirement.

### Semantic Evolution

The concept of supplying what is required developed into the modern meaning of adequacy or completeness.

### Modern Meaning

**Sufficient (adjective):** Adequate in quantity, quality or degree to meet a particular requirement.

### Common Usage

1. **Sufficient evidence** – Enough evidence to support a conclusion.
2. **Sufficient resources** – Adequate resources to achieve a specified objective.
3. **Sufficient safeguards** – Adequate protective measures against potential risks.

**UPSC Application:** Sufficient institutional safeguards are necessary to prevent the misuse of discretionary powers.

## 32. SUFFICIENCY

### Meaning of the Components

- **Sufficient** = adequate or enough.
- **-cy** = state or condition.

### Literal Meaning

The condition of having enough.

### Semantic Evolution

The concept expanded into a general measure of adequacy in economic, social, administrative and legal contexts.

### Modern Meaning

**Sufficiency (noun):** The condition of having an adequate quantity or degree of something.

### Common Usage

1. **Food sufficiency** – Availability of adequate food supplies.
2. **Income sufficiency** – Having sufficient income to meet basic needs.
3. **Sufficiency of evidence** – Adequacy of evidence to support a finding or conclusion.

## 33. DEFECT

### Meaning of the Components

- **De-** = away or down.
- Latin *facere* = to do or make.
- Latin *defectus* = failure, deficiency or defect.

### Literal Meaning

A failure or shortcoming in something made or performed.

### Semantic Evolution

The meaning evolved from failure or falling short to a flaw, imperfection or deficiency in an object, process or system.

### Modern Meaning

**Defect (noun):** A flaw, imperfection or shortcoming that reduces the quality or functionality of something.

### Common Usage

1. **Structural defect** – A flaw affecting the stability or integrity of a structure.
2. **Institutional defects** – Weaknesses in institutional design or functioning.
3. **Defect in implementation** – A flaw in the execution of a policy or programme.

**UPSC Application:** Institutional defects in accountability mechanisms can create opportunities for corruption and arbitrary exercise of power.

## 34. DEFECTIVE

### Meaning of the Components

- **Defect** = flaw or imperfection.
- **-ive** = having the nature or quality of.

### Literal Meaning

Having a flaw or imperfection.

### Semantic Evolution

The term evolved into describing products, systems or processes that do not function according to expected standards.

### Modern Meaning

**Defective (adjective):** Having a fault, flaw or imperfection.

### Common Usage

1. **Defective products** – Products that fail to meet prescribed standards.
2. **Defective institutional mechanisms** – Mechanisms that fail to perform their intended functions.
3. **Defective implementation** – Execution that is flawed or incomplete.

# 36. HIGH-VALUE DISTINCTIONS FOR UPSC

## A. Efficiency vs Effectiveness vs Efficacy

| Concept       | Central question                                                              | Example                                                               |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Efficiency    | Are resources being used optimally?                                           | Delivering MGNREGA wages with minimum administrative cost and delay   |
| Effectiveness | Are objectives being achieved?                                                | Whether MGNREGA improves livelihood security                          |
| Efficacy      | Can the intervention produce the intended outcome under specified conditions? | Whether a tested intervention can improve a particular health outcome |

**Memory formula:**

- Efficiency = Input versus Output.
- Effectiveness = Objective versus Achievement.
- Efficacy = Capacity to produce the intended result.

## B. Deficit vs Deficiency vs Defect

| Word       | Meaning                | Example                |
| ---------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Deficit    | Quantifiable shortfall | Fiscal deficit         |
| Deficiency | Inadequacy or lack     | Nutritional deficiency |
| Defect     | Flaw or imperfection   | Institutional defect   |

## C. Effect vs Affect

| Effect                                  | Affect                                |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Usually a noun                          | Usually a verb                        |
| Result or consequence                   | To influence                          |
| Effect of inflation                     | Inflation affects purchasing power    |
| To effect reforms = bring about reforms | To affect reforms = influence reforms |

## D. Facilitate vs Enable

| Facilitate            | Enable                                       |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Make a process easier | Make something possible                      |
| Facilitate trade      | Enable citizens to exercise rights           |
| Reduce obstacles      | Provide the necessary capacity or conditions |

# 38. HOW TO USE THIS FAMILY IN UPSC ANSWER WRITING

The real advantage of learning word families is not merely improving English vocabulary. It is developing precision in expressing analytical arguments.

Consider the following transformations:

**Ordinary statement:**

The government should use resources properly and implement policies successfully.

**UPSC-quality statement:**

The government must ensure efficient resource allocation and effective policy implementation to maximise developmental outcomes.

---

**Ordinary statement:**

There is a lack of infrastructure in rural areas.

**UPSC-quality statement:**

Deficient rural infrastructure remains a structural impediment to inclusive growth and livelihood diversification.

---

**Ordinary statement:**

Government schemes do not always achieve their objectives.

**UPSC-quality statement:**

The effectiveness of welfare interventions is often constrained by institutional deficiencies, implementation gaps and inadequate monitoring mechanisms.

---

**Ordinary statement:**

Trade procedures should be simplified.

**UPSC-quality statement:**

Streamlining regulatory procedures and facilitating trade can reduce transaction costs, enhance competitiveness and strengthen integration into global value chains.

## THE ONE-LINE MEMORY FORMULA

**FACERE = TO DO / TO MAKE**

- Do something well → Efficient.
- Do something with minimum waste → Efficiency.
- Possess the ability to produce results → Efficacy.
- Produce the desired result → Effective.
- Make something easier → Facilitate.
- Fall short → Deficient.
- Fall short financially → Deficit.
- Make progress in a skill → Proficient.
- Something made → Fact / Artifact.
- Something that contributes to a result → Factor.
- Make goods → Manufacture.

**The ultimate lesson:** Once you understand the root *FACERE*, you can decode an entire vocabulary family rather than memorising dozens of unrelated English words.

## **Additional retention strategy: How to master this family**

For UPSC, I recommend learning this family in three passes:

1. **First pass – Etymology:** Memorise the root and its three forms: FAC, FIC and FACT.
2. **Second pass – Semantic distinctions:** Particularly master efficiency, effectiveness, efficacy, deficit, deficiency and defect.
3. **Third pass – Application:** Use 5–7 of these words in GS answers, Essay and Ethics answers wherever contextually appropriate.

**One important linguistic caution:** Not every word that looks similar necessarily belongs to the same root family. For example, *progress* comes from Latin *gradi* (to step), not *facere*. This distinction matters when studying etymology systematically.
