# Word Power Made Easy: PREROGATIVE

Etymology • Word families • Constitutional vocabulary • UPSC Prelims, Mains, Essay & Interview

The easiest way to remember prerogative is to imagine a constitutional authority that possesses a special power unavailable to an ordinary citizen.

For example, the Indian President has certain constitutionally assigned functions. The word prerogative may describe a special right or power, but it does not mean that every such power is personal, unrestricted or exercisable independently.

That distinction is especially important in UPSC Polity.

## 1. PREROGATIVE — understand the word from its roots

WORD ANATOMY

# PREROGATIVE

## prae-

Before

Latin prefix meaning “before”

## rogare

To ask, propose or put a question

Latin verb associated with asking or proposing

Latin praerogativa → French prérogative → English prerogative

Core image: A special position or privilege arising from an earlier choice or priority.

### Meaning of the components

- prae-: Before, in advance.
- rogare: To ask, propose or put a question.

The historical connection is more interesting than simply translating these components separately. In ancient Rome, the praerogativa was associated with the century of voters called upon to vote first in certain assemblies. Its early vote could carry particular significance.

The term subsequently developed into the idea of a special privilege or right.

Retention hook: Think of someone being given a special position before others. The idea of priority gradually connects to the idea of a special privilege.

### Literal meaning

Historically, the term referred to priority in being asked to vote. Its later, broader sense is a special right, privilege or power.

### Semantic evolution

Stage 1

Priority in voting

A particular Roman voting group was called upon first.

Stage 2

Special privilege

The association with priority developed into a broader sense of a special right or advantage.

Stage 3

Special authority or entitlement

In modern usage, a prerogative may be a distinctive power, privilege or right attached to an office, status or person.

### Modern meaning

Prerogative (noun)

1. A special right, privilege or power belonging to a particular person, office or institution.
2. An entitlement or discretion associated with a particular position or role.
3. More broadly, a matter considered to be someone's choice or responsibility.

Examples:

- Constitutional: The exercise of executive prerogative is subject to constitutional limitations.
- Institutional: The appointment of a committee chair may fall within the prerogative of the competent authority.
- Everyday: Choosing one's career is a personal prerogative.

### Common usage: UPSC-relevant collocations

| Collocation                | Meaning and application                                                      |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Royal prerogative          | Special powers historically associated with the British Crown.               |
| Executive prerogative      | Special authority or discretion associated with the executive.               |
| Constitutional prerogative | A special power or entitlement recognised within a constitutional framework. |

UPSC answer-writing upgrade

Instead of writing, “The executive can do whatever it wants under its prerogative,” write:

“Executive discretion must be exercised within the limits of constitutional authority, statutory provisions and judicially enforceable safeguards.”

A prerogative is not synonymous with arbitrary power.

# 2. The ROGARE family: discover the hidden connections

The most productive way to expand prerogative is to remember the Latin verb rogare = to ask, propose or request.

This root entered several English words through Latin compounds. Different prefixes transformed the idea of asking or proposing into concepts involving repeal, questioning, claiming, substitution and the suspension of legislative sessions.

## Word 1. ABROGATE

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

- ab- = away, off
- rogare = to propose or enact a measure

Literal meaning: To do away with a measure or enactment.

Semantic evolution: The Latin abrogare referred to repealing or cancelling a law. The meaning developed into formally abolishing or rescinding a law, rule or agreement.

Modern meaning: To repeal, abolish or formally revoke a law, rule or established arrangement.

Common usage

1. Abrogate a law — formally repeal a law.
2. Abrogate a treaty — terminate or cancel a treaty obligation through the applicable legal process.
3. Abrogate constitutional rights — remove or nullify rights, subject to the relevant legal context.

Retention hook: Abrogate = do away with a legal measure.

UPSC application: Distinguish abrogation from amendment. An amendment changes a legal provision; abrogation ordinarily means its repeal or abolition. The exact legal effect depends on the instrument and applicable law.

## Word 2. ABROGATION

Part of speech: noun

Meaning of the components

- ab- = away or off
- rog- = the root associated with proposing or enacting
- -ation = action or process

Literal meaning: The act of doing away with a measure.

Semantic evolution: The action expressed by abrogate developed into a noun describing formal repeal or cancellation.

Modern meaning: The formal abolition or repeal of a law, rule, agreement or established provision.

Common usage

1. Abrogation of a law — formal repeal of legislation.
2. Abrogation of treaty obligations — termination of obligations under a treaty through the applicable process.
3. Abrogation of rights — removal or nullification of rights.

Retention hook: Abrogate is the action; abrogation is the act or process.

UPSC application: When analysing a legal or constitutional change, identify the authority responsible, the instrument used and the legal consequences rather than treating every change as an abrogation.

## Word 3. DEROGATE

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

- de- = away or down
- rogare = to propose or enact

Literal meaning: To take away from or diminish.

Semantic evolution: The Latin derogare referred to taking away from, modifying or partially repealing a law. The English verb also developed the broader sense of diminishing something's authority, value or standing.

Modern meaning: To diminish, detract from or depart from an established right, rule or obligation.

Common usage

1. Derogate from a right — diminish or impair a right.
2. Derogate from a principle — undermine or depart from a principle.
3. Derogate from statutory requirements — depart from or diminish requirements, where legally permitted.

Retention hook: Derogate suggests taking something away from its full force or value.

UPSC application: A statutory authority cannot assume an unrestricted power to derogate from legal requirements merely because it possesses administrative discretion.

## Word 4. DEROGATORY

Part of speech: adjective

Meaning of the components

- derog- = diminish or detract
- -atory = relating to an action or effect

Literal meaning: Having the effect of diminishing or detracting from something.

Semantic evolution: The sense of diminishing standing or value developed into the modern meaning of expressing disrespect, disparagement or an unfavourable judgement.

Modern meaning: Disrespectful, belittling or damaging to someone's reputation or dignity.

Common usage

1. Derogatory remarks — comments that belittle someone.
2. Derogatory language — language expressing contempt or disrespect.
3. Derogatory reference — a disparaging reference to a person or group.

Retention hook: A derogatory remark detracts from someone's dignity or standing.

UPSC application: In Ethics and governance, derogatory language directed at vulnerable communities can reinforce stigma, exclusion and unequal treatment.

## Word 5. DEROGATION

Part of speech: noun

Meaning of the components

- derog- = diminish or take away from
- -ation = action or process

Literal meaning: An act of diminishing or taking away from something.

Semantic evolution: The word developed from the legal sense of modifying or taking away from a law to the broader sense of an exception, departure or impairment. In legal and human-rights contexts, it can refer to a formal departure from certain obligations under specified conditions.

Modern meaning: A reduction, exception or departure from an established rule, right or obligation.

Common usage

1. Derogation from rights — a legally qualified departure from certain rights or obligations.
2. Derogation from a rule — an exception to or departure from a rule.
3. Non-derogation clause — a clause intended to prevent certain protections from being reduced or displaced.

Retention hook: Derogation is a reduction or departure; derogatory is disparaging.

UPSC application: Under international human-rights law, derogation from certain treaty obligations may be permitted only under specified conditions. It does not imply that all rights can be suspended at will.

## Word 6. INTERROGATE

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

- inter- = between
- rogare = to ask

Literal meaning: To ask questions; historically associated with questioning between parties.

Semantic evolution: The Latin interrogare developed into the sense of asking questions formally or systematically, particularly to obtain information or establish facts.

Modern meaning: To question someone closely or formally, especially in an investigation.

Common usage

1. Interrogate a suspect — question a suspect during an investigation.
2. Interrogate the evidence — critically examine the evidence.
3. Interrogate assumptions — question the assumptions underlying an argument.

Retention hook: Interrogate means to question; its modern use extends beyond questioning people to critically examining ideas.

UPSC application: In a Mains answer, “We must interrogate the assumption that economic growth automatically reduces inequality” means we must critically examine that assumption.

## Word 7. INTERROGATION

Part of speech: noun

Meaning of the components

- inter- = between
- rog- = ask or question
- -ation = action or process

Literal meaning: The act of questioning.

Semantic evolution: The action of asking questions developed into the formal process of questioning a person or systematically examining a claim.

Modern meaning: Formal or intensive questioning, especially during an investigation; more broadly, critical examination.

Common usage

1. Police interrogation — questioning conducted during a police investigation.
2. Interrogation of evidence — critical scrutiny of evidence.
3. Critical interrogation of assumptions — systematic examination of underlying beliefs.

Retention hook: Interrogate is the verb; interrogation is the process.

UPSC application: Effective investigation must be consistent with legal safeguards, procedural fairness and protection against coercion. The exact safeguards depend on the applicable law and circumstances.

## Word 8. INTERROGATIVE

Part of speech: adjective and noun

Meaning of the components

- inter- = between
- rog- = ask
- -ative = relating to an action or function

Literal meaning: Relating to questioning.

Semantic evolution: The questioning sense developed into a grammatical term for forms that ask questions and, more broadly, into a description of an enquiring or questioning attitude.

Modern meaning: Expressing or relating to a question.

Common usage

1. Interrogative sentence — a sentence that asks a question.
2. Interrogative pronoun — a pronoun such as who, what or which used to ask questions.
3. Interrogative approach — an approach based on questioning and inquiry.

Retention hook: Interrogative describes the question; interrogation describes the questioning process.

UPSC application: A strong analytical answer adopts an interrogative approach: What is the problem? Why does it persist? Which institutions are responsible? What evidence supports the proposed solution?

## Word 9. INTERROGATORY

Part of speech: noun and adjective

Meaning of the components

- inter- = between
- rog- = ask
- -atory = associated with an action or function

Literal meaning: Something relating to questioning.

Semantic evolution: The term developed into a specialised legal word for written questions used in formal proceedings.

Modern meaning: In legal procedure, a written question or set of questions that a party is required to answer under the applicable rules.

Common usage

1. Written interrogatories — formal written questions in civil proceedings.
2. Answer interrogatories — respond to formal questions.
3. Interrogatory procedure — a process involving formal written questioning.

Retention hook: Interrogation often evokes questioning in an investigation; interrogatories are formal written questions in certain legal proceedings.

UPSC relevance: This is a specialised legal term, useful when discussing civil procedure or methods of obtaining evidence, rather than ordinary governance answers.

## Word 10. ARROGATE

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

- ad- = to or towards; assimilated in the formation
- rogare = ask, claim or propose

Literal meaning: To claim or take something to oneself.

Semantic evolution: The Latin arrogare developed the sense of claiming something for oneself. In English, arrogate acquired the particularly negative sense of assuming a right, authority or status without adequate justification.

Modern meaning: To claim or assume a power, right or authority that one is not entitled to exercise.

Common usage

1. Arrogate powers to oneself — assume powers without proper authority.
2. Arrogate authority — claim authority without sufficient legal or institutional basis.
3. Arrogate the right to decide — claim an exclusive right to make a decision.

Retention hook: Arrogate = claim for oneself, often presumptuously.

UPSC application: “An unelected authority must not arrogate to itself powers that the Constitution vests in a democratically accountable institution.”

This is one of the most useful words in the family for Polity, Ethics and Essay.

## Word 11. ARROGATION

Part of speech: noun

Meaning of the components

- arrog- = claim or assume for oneself
- -ation = action or process

Literal meaning: The act of claiming something for oneself.

Semantic evolution: The act expressed by arrogate developed into a noun describing the unjustified assumption of authority, entitlement or power.

Modern meaning: The act of claiming or assuming a right, power or authority without adequate justification.

Common usage

1. Arrogation of power — assumption of power without proper authority.
2. Arrogation of authority — unjustified assumption of decision-making authority.
3. Arrogation of constitutional powers — claiming powers beyond the institution's constitutional mandate.

Retention hook: Arrogate is the act; arrogation is the act or process.

UPSC application: The arrogation of power undermines constitutionalism because public authority is limited by law, institutional competence and accountability.

Do not confuse: Arrogation is the act of claiming power; arrogance is an attitude of excessive self-importance. They are related in form but are not interchangeable.

## Word 12. SURROGATE

Part of speech: noun and adjective

Meaning of the components

- sub- = under or in place of; the form changes in the historical compound
- rogare = ask, appoint or propose

The word derives through Latin surrogare, also associated with subrogare, meaning to substitute or appoint in another's place.

Literal meaning: One appointed or acting in place of another.

Semantic evolution: The idea of substituting one person or thing for another developed into the general modern sense of a replacement or substitute.

Modern meaning: A person or thing acting as a substitute for another; in specific contexts, a person undertaking a role on another's behalf.

Common usage

1. Surrogate decision-maker — a person authorised to make decisions for someone else in specified circumstances.
2. Surrogate indicator — a proxy measure used when the variable of interest cannot be measured directly.
3. Surrogate representation — a substitute form of representation.

Retention hook: Surrogate = substitute.

UPSC application: GDP growth is sometimes used as a surrogate indicator of economic progress, but it cannot independently measure health, equality, environmental sustainability or subjective well-being.

Anthropology connection: Be careful when using surrogate measures in research. An indicator may approximate a phenomenon without fully capturing the underlying cultural or social reality.

## Word 13. SUBROGATION

Part of speech: noun

Meaning of the components

- sub- = under or in place of
- rogation = historically associated with proposing or appointing
- The term comes from Latin subrogare and its derivatives.

Literal meaning: Substitution or appointment in another's place.

Semantic evolution: The idea of replacing one party with another developed into a technical legal concept, especially in insurance and obligations.

Modern meaning: A legal process through which one party acquires certain rights of another party, commonly after paying a claim or debt, subject to the applicable law or agreement.

Common usage

1. Right of subrogation — the right to pursue another party's liability after satisfying a claim.
2. Insurance subrogation — an insurer's right to pursue recovery from a responsible third party after paying the insured.
3. Subrogation of rights — substitution of one party into another party's relevant legal rights.

Retention hook: Surrogate is a substitute; subrogation is a legal process of substituting one party into another's rights.

UPSC relevance: Useful for legal and insurance contexts, but less central than abrogate or arrogate for general Mains vocabulary.

## Word 14. PROROGUE

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

- pro- = forward or onward
- rogare = propose or enact

Literal meaning: To extend or carry something forward.

Semantic evolution: The Latin prorogare could mean to extend or defer. In parliamentary usage, prorogue developed into the term for formally ending a legislative session without dissolving the legislature.

Modern meaning: To formally discontinue a parliamentary session.

Common usage

1. Prorogue Parliament — formally end a parliamentary session.
2. Prorogation of a session — the formal ending of a session.
3. Prorogue a legislative assembly — formally end its session.

Retention hook: Prorogue is a parliamentary procedure, not a synonym for dissolving a legislature.

UPSC Polity application: Under Article 85 of the Indian Constitution, the President prorogues either House of Parliament. Under Article 174, the Governor prorogues a State Legislature. These powers operate within the constitutional framework, including the applicable system of ministerial advice.

## Word 15. PROROGATION

Part of speech: noun

Meaning of the components

- pro- = forward or onward
- rog- = the historical root associated with proposing or extending
- -ation = action or process

Literal meaning: An act of extending or carrying forward; in parliamentary usage, the formal ending of a session.

Semantic evolution: The historical sense of extending or deferring developed into the specialised constitutional meaning of formally terminating a legislative session.

Modern meaning: The formal ending of a parliamentary or legislative session, distinct from dissolution of the legislature.

Common usage

1. Prorogation of Parliament — formal ending of a parliamentary session.
2. Order of prorogation — the formal instrument or order effecting prorogation, according to the applicable procedure.
3. Constitutional validity of prorogation — whether the exercise of prorogation complies with constitutional requirements.

Retention hook: Prorogue is the verb; prorogation is the constitutional act.

UPSC application: Prorogation ends a session, whereas dissolution ends the life of the Lok Sabha or a State Legislative Assembly. The Rajya Sabha and State Legislative Councils are not subject to dissolution in the same way because they are continuing chambers.

## 4. Three distinctions to master

## A. Prerogative vs arrogation

Prerogative: A special power or right associated with an office or status.

Arrogation: The unjustified assumption of a power or right.

Example: A constitutional authority exercises its prerogative within the law; it arrogates power if it assumes authority it does not possess.

## B. Abrogation vs derogation

Abrogation: Repeal or abolition.

Derogation: Reduction, exception or departure from a rule, right or obligation.

Example: Repealing a law differs from creating a legally permitted exception to a general obligation.

## C. Prorogation vs dissolution

Prorogation: Ends a legislative session.

Dissolution: Ends the life of a dissolvable legislative chamber, triggering the process toward a new House.

UPSC trap: The terms are not interchangeable. The Rajya Sabha is a continuing House and is not dissolved.

# Word Power Made Easy: BASHING

Etymology • Word families • Political discourse • UPSC Mains, Essay, Ethics & Anthropology

The key to understanding bashing is to picture someone striking an object repeatedly. Now transfer that image from the physical world to language: instead of striking a person, someone attacks their reputation through harsh criticism.

That transition—from physical blows to verbal attacks—is central to the modern word bashing.

However, there is an important distinction: bashing has more than one meaning, and not every word that looks or sounds similar belongs to the same etymological family. We will begin with its actual history and then expand into related vocabulary that is useful for UPSC.

WORD ANATOMY

# BASHING

## bash

strike or attack

## -ing

action or activity

English bash + -ing → bashing

Core image: Striking repeatedly, physically or figuratively.

### Meaning of the components

- Bash: An informal English verb meaning to strike or hit forcefully. It can also mean to criticise someone or something harshly.
- -ing: A suffix that can form a noun describing an activity or an adjective describing something. In bashing, it often names the act of attacking or harshly criticising.

Unlike words such as chronology, whose Greek root is readily identifiable, bash has an uncertain ultimate etymology. It is safer to learn its documented English meaning than to invent a Latin or Greek decomposition.

### Literal meaning

The act of striking or hitting forcefully.

In figurative usage, the same image becomes an attack through words, accusations or hostile public commentary.

### Semantic evolution

Stage 1

Physical impact

To hit, strike or damage something forcefully.

Stage 2

Verbal attack

To attack a person, institution or idea through harsh criticism.

Stage 3

Public and political discourse

Repeated, often hostile criticism of a public figure, institution, group or country.

### Modern meaning

Bashing (noun / verb form)

1. Physical: Hitting or striking something forcefully.
2. Figurative: Harsh or persistent criticism of a person, institution or group.
3. Informal compound usage: An activity or event, as in a birthday bash or a bit of computer bashing in certain contexts. These expressions do not all carry the negative sense of hostile criticism.

Examples:

- Political: The debate deteriorated into government-bashing rather than a discussion of policy outcomes.
- Social: Community-bashing can reinforce prejudice and social exclusion.
- Media: The programme was criticised for becoming personality-bashing instead of examining evidence.

### Common usage: UPSC-relevant collocations

| Collocation        | Meaning and application                                                                         |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Government-bashing | Harsh criticism of the government, sometimes used dismissively to suggest excessive negativity. |
| India-bashing      | Hostile or persistently negative criticism of India; the term can itself be politically loaded. |
| Politician-bashing | Harsh criticism of politicians, often in public or media discourse.                             |

UPSC answer-writing upgrade: Instead of writing “The debate became politician-bashing,” write:

“The debate became increasingly personalised, with ad hominem attacks displacing evidence-based scrutiny of policy performance.”

This is more precise, analytical and suitable for formal answers.

Important nuance: Criticism is not automatically bashing. In a democracy, evidence-based criticism of government policies is a legitimate form of accountability. The term bashing often implies that the criticism is excessive, hostile or unfair, but that implication may itself be contested.

Modern meaning: To hit hard; to criticise harshly; or, as a noun, a blow, party or attempt, depending on context.

2. Bash a policy — criticise a policy harshly.
3. Have a bash at something — make an attempt at something.

## Word 2. BASHING

Part of speech: noun and verb form

Meaning of the components

- bash = strike or attack
- -ing = ongoing action or activity

Literal meaning: The act of striking.

Semantic evolution: Repeated physical blows developed into the idea of sustained verbal attacks, particularly against people, groups, institutions or countries.

Modern meaning: Physical beating or harsh, often repeated criticism.

Common usage

1. Government-bashing — harsh criticism of a government.
2. Media-bashing — hostile criticism of the media.
3. Country-bashing — hostile or persistently negative criticism of a country.

Retention hook: Bash is the action; bashing is the activity or attack.

UPSC application: Distinguish legitimate public criticism from prejudicial attacks. The former scrutinises evidence and performance; the latter may rely on stereotypes, personal hostility or selective evidence.

## Word 3. BASHER

Part of speech: noun

Meaning of the components

- bash = strike or attack
- -er = a person or thing that performs an action

Literal meaning: One who bashes.

Semantic evolution: The agent noun first referred to someone who strikes or beats; figuratively, it came to describe a person who habitually attacks or criticises someone or something.&#x20;

Modern meaning: A person who attacks physically or, more commonly in political compounds, someone who persistently criticises a target.

Common usage

3. Media-basher — someone who habitually attacks the media.

Meaning of the components

- bash = strike forcefully
- -ed = past tense or past participle

Literal meaning: Struck forcefully.

Semantic evolution: The physical sense extended to damage caused by impact and then to figurative contexts in which a person, policy or institution has been harshly attacked.

Modern meaning: Hit, damaged or subjected to severe criticism.

Common usage

1. Bashed in the face — struck in the face.
2. Bashed by the media — subjected to harsh media criticism.
3. Bashed for a decision — heavily criticised for a decision.

Retention hook: Bashed describes what has happened to the target.

UPSC application: “The proposal was criticised by several stakeholders” is generally more precise and neutral than “The proposal was bashed.”

## Word 5. BASH UP

Part of speech: phrasal verb

Meaning of the components

- bash = strike violently
- up = a particle that can intensify an action or indicate damage

Literal meaning: To strike repeatedly or forcefully.

Semantic evolution: The combination came to mean beating someone severely or damaging something through repeated blows.

Modern meaning: To beat someone up or damage something badly. It is informal and may describe violence.

Common usage

1. Bash someone up — beat someone severely.
2. Get bashed up — suffer a beating.
3. Bash up a vehicle — damage a vehicle badly.

Retention hook: Bash up usually conveys substantial physical damage.

UPSC relevance: The expression is informal. In answers about crime, law and order, or human rights, prefer precise terms such as physical assault, grievous injury or mob violence, as appropriate to the facts.

Meaning of the components

- bash = strike forcefully
- in = inward, indicating the direction or result of the blow

Semantic evolution: Physical impact developed into a phrase for breaking or crushing a surface through a forceful blow.

Modern meaning: To damage or crush something inward by striking it.

Common usage

2. Bash in a window — break the window with a blow.
3. Bash someone's head in — a violent expression referring to a severe attack.

# 4. The UPSC vocabulary upgrade: words that express criticism more precisely

The direct bash family is relatively small. To expand your usable vocabulary, learn the following semantic relatives as well. They are not derivatives of bash; they are alternative words for different kinds of criticism. This distinction matters because etymological relationships should not be invented merely to make a word family larger.

## Word 17. CRITICISE

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

The word comes through Greek kritikos and Latin criticus, associated with judging or distinguishing. Its historical root is different from bash.

Literal meaning: To judge or evaluate.

Semantic evolution: The act of judging developed into the systematic evaluation of the merits and faults of a person, idea, work or policy.

Modern meaning: To express an assessment of strengths and weaknesses; depending on context, the assessment may be favourable or unfavourable.

Common usage

1. Criticise a policy — evaluate its weaknesses or shortcomings.
2. Constructive criticism — feedback intended to improve performance.
3. Criticise the government — scrutinise government actions or decisions.

Retention hook: Criticism can be reasoned and constructive; bashing often implies hostile or excessive attack.

UPSC application: “The policy should be critically evaluated against its stated objectives, implementation record and distributional outcomes.”

## Word 18. LAMBASTE

Part of speech: verb and noun

Meaning of the components

Lambaste entered English through earlier forms associated with beating or thrashing; it is not derived from bash.

Literal meaning: To beat or thrash.

Semantic evolution: The physical sense developed into the figurative meaning of attacking someone verbally with severe criticism.

Modern meaning: To criticise or reprimand someone very severely.

Common usage

1. Lambaste the administration — criticise the administration harshly.
2. Lambaste a proposal — attack a proposal with strong criticism.
3. Face a lambasting — receive severe criticism.

Retention hook: Lambaste is stronger and more dramatic than criticise.

UPSC application: Use this word mainly when describing the tone of a debate or a public reaction. For a neutral analytical answer, criticise, scrutinise or question is usually better.

## Word 19. DENIGRATE

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

- Latin de- = down or away
- Latin niger = black

The word historically developed through a Latin formation meaning to blacken or make dark.

Literal meaning: To blacken or darken.

Semantic evolution: The image of blackening developed metaphorically into damaging someone's reputation or lowering their perceived worth.

Modern meaning: To unfairly belittle, disparage or damage the reputation of a person, group or achievement.

Common usage

1. Denigrate a community — disparage a community.
2. Denigrate an achievement — belittle an accomplishment.
3. Denigrate opposing views — dismiss or disparage views rather than engaging with them.

Retention hook: Denigrate suggests blackening someone's reputation.

UPSC application: “Public discourse should challenge discriminatory practices without denigrating entire communities.”

## Word 20. VILIFY

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

The word derives from Latin vilis, meaning cheap, low or worthless, through vilificare, associated with making something seem worthless.

Literal meaning: To make someone appear worthless.

Semantic evolution: The idea of lowering someone's worth developed into portraying a person or group as wicked, contemptible or deserving of hatred.

Modern meaning: To portray someone as evil or contemptible, often through hostile public statements.

Common usage

1. Vilify a minority — portray a minority group as contemptible.
2. Vilify political opponents — portray opponents as inherently bad rather than contesting their arguments.
3. Public vilification — public denunciation intended to damage someone's reputation.

Retention hook: Vilify goes beyond ordinary criticism: it attacks the target's character or moral standing.

UPSC application: “Political disagreement should not become the vilification of opponents, as this weakens deliberative democracy and encourages polarisation.”

## Word 21. DISPARAGE

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

The word came through Old French desparagier, historically associated with treating someone as unequal in rank or status.

Literal meaning: To treat as inferior.

Semantic evolution: The idea of regarding someone as lower in status developed into the modern sense of belittling or speaking slightingly of someone or something.

Modern meaning: To belittle, undervalue or speak dismissively of a person, group, idea or achievement.

Common usage

1. Disparage traditional knowledge — dismiss traditional knowledge as inferior.
2. Disparage an institution — belittle an institution.
3. Disparaging remarks — comments that express contempt or disrespect.

Retention hook: Disparage means to make something seem lesser or inferior.

Anthropology application: Avoid disparaging indigenous knowledge systems merely because they differ from modern scientific frameworks. Evaluate their claims, contexts and limits carefully.

## Word 22. DENOUNCE

Part of speech: verb

Meaning of the components

The word derives through Latin denuntiare, meaning to announce, declare or proclaim.

Literal meaning: To announce or declare publicly.

Semantic evolution: The act of making a formal declaration developed into the modern sense of publicly condemning conduct, actions or policies.

Modern meaning: To condemn something openly and strongly.

Common usage

1. Denounce human-rights violations — publicly condemn violations.
2. Denounce corruption — openly condemn corrupt practices.
3. Strongly denounce an attack — express formal condemnation.

Retention hook: Denounce focuses on openly declaring condemnation; bash suggests attack or harsh criticism.

UPSC application: Governments, international organisations and civil-society groups may denounce violations, but the credibility of a denunciation depends on consistency, evidence and follow-through.

## 1. Remission: the five-step analysis

### 1. Meaning of the components

- re- = back, backwards, away.
- miss- = from Latin mittere, “to send” or “let go.”
- -ion = a noun-forming suffix indicating an action, process or result.

Etymological caution: miss- here belongs to the Latin mittere family. It is not the English word miss meaning “fail to notice” or “feel the absence of.”

### 2. Literal meaning

Remission ≈ the act of sending back, letting go, or easing something.

This is a memory aid, not the complete modern dictionary definition.

### 3. Semantic evolution

1. Sending back or releasing — the original Latin idea.
2. Forgiving or waiving — releasing someone from blame, a debt or a penalty.
3. Diminishing or subsiding — the intensity of a condition or effect becomes less severe.
4. Modern specialised meanings — improvement in disease activity, reduction or cancellation of a penalty, or remission of an obligation.

The word was used for forgiveness in English by around 1200; its senses expanded to release from obligations, the easing of disease symptoms and the reduction of penalties.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.etymonline.com\&sz=32)

Etymology, Origin & Meaning



### 4. Modern meaning

Remission (noun)

1. Medicine: a reduction or disappearance of signs and symptoms of a disease. In cancer, remission does not necessarily mean cure.
2. Law and public administration: cancellation, reduction or forgiveness of a penalty, punishment or liability.
3. General use: a lessening or temporary cessation of something unpleasant or intense.

### 5. Common usage: UPSC-relevant collocations

- Cancer remission — the disease's signs and symptoms have reduced or disappeared.
- Remission of sentence — reduction of a sentence or punishment, subject to the applicable legal framework.
- Remission of a debt — forgiveness or cancellation of an amount owed.

UPSC distinction: In Indian constitutional law, remission is commonly discussed in relation to reducing the period or severity of a sentence without necessarily changing its character. It is distinct from commutation, which substitutes a punishment with a different, lighter form, and reprieve, which temporarily postpones execution of a sentence.

## 2. The etymological family of remission

The most useful family is built around REM- / MISS- ← Latin remittere. The words divide into two groups:

- Direct family: remit, remittance, remiss, remissness, remissive, remittent, unremitting.
- Wider root family: mission, transmit, transmit, submit, admit, emit, permit, commit, dismiss, omit, and intermit—all associated with Latin mittere, “to send” or “let go,” often with different prefixes and meanings.&#x20;

The first group is the priority for understanding remission. The second helps you decode a much wider range of UPSC vocabulary.

### A. The direct family

## 1. Remit (verb)

Meaning of components

re- = back; mittere = send or release.

Literal meaning

Send back, release, or let something go.

Semantic evolution

The idea of releasing developed into forgiving a penalty, reducing a punishment, referring a matter to another authority, and sending money as payment.

Modern meaning

To cancel or reduce a penalty or debt; to send money; or, in formal contexts, to refer a matter to an appropriate authority.

Common usage

- Remit a sentence
- Remit payment
- Remit the matter to a lower court

Retention hook

Remission is the noun; remit is the action.

## 2. Remittance (noun)

Meaning of components

Remit = send money or make a payment; -ance = action, process or result.

Literal meaning

The act or result of sending something, especially money.

Semantic evolution

The act of transmitting money became the standard financial meaning.

Modern meaning

Money sent by one person or party to another, often across regions or national borders.

Common usage

- Remittances from overseas Indians
- Inward remittances
- Remittance flows

UPSC connection

Remittances are important for India's external sector, household consumption and foreign-exchange inflows. Remittance is related to remit, but it does not mean a reduction in punishment.

## 3. Remiss (adjective)

Meaning of components

From Latin remissus, the past participle of remittere, meaning relaxed or slackened.

Literal meaning

Slack, relaxed, or not sufficiently strict.

Semantic evolution

Physical slackness developed into a figurative sense of failing to exercise due care or fulfil a duty.

Modern meaning

Negligent, careless, or insufficiently diligent in carrying out a responsibility.

Common usage

- Remiss in one's duties
- Remiss in enforcing the law
- It would be remiss of the government to ignore…

Retention hook

Remission means easing or reducing; remiss describes someone whose effort or vigilance has slackened.&#x20;

## 4. Remissness (noun)

Meaning of components

Remiss = negligent; -ness = state or quality.

Literal meaning

The state of being remiss.

Semantic evolution

The quality of slackness became a term for negligence in fulfilling obligations.

Modern meaning

Negligence, carelessness or failure to exercise the expected diligence.

Common usage

- Administrative remissness
- Remissness in the discharge of duty
- Institutional remissness

UPSC usage

Useful in ethics and governance answers when describing a failure of responsibility or due diligence.

## 5. Remissive (adjective; uncommon)

Meaning of components

Remiss- = from remittere, to slacken or abate; -ive = tending to or producing an effect.

Literal meaning

Tending to pardon or lessen.

Semantic evolution

The word developed senses connected with pardoning or abatement.

Modern meaning

A rare word meaning inclined to pardon or associated with abatement.

Common usage

- Remissive power (rare/formal)
- Remissive effect (rare)
- Remissive action (rare)

Retention note

Recognise it when reading, but prefer the more common words remission, remit and reduction in your own UPSC answers.&#x20;

## 6. Remittent (adjective; chiefly medical)

Meaning of components

From Latin remittens, “slackening” or “abating,” from remittere.

Literal meaning

One that lessens or subsides.

Semantic evolution

The sense of abating came to describe fever whose intensity falls but does not return fully to normal.

Modern meaning

In medicine, describing a fever that fluctuates or subsides without the temperature returning to normal between peaks.

Common usage

- Remittent fever
- Remittent pattern
- Remittent temperature

Retention hook

A remittent fever abates but does not fully disappear between peaks. This is a specialised medical term, not a synonym for remission in every context.

## 7. Unremitting (adjective)

Meaning of components

un- = not; remitting = slackening, stopping or easing.

Literal meaning

Not letting up or easing.

Semantic evolution

The idea of something not slackening came to describe sustained effort, pressure or intensity.

Modern meaning

Continuous, persistent or relentless; not diminishing in force.

Common usage

- Unremitting efforts
- Unremitting pressure
- Unremitting hardship

Retention hook

Remission = easing; unremitting = not easing.&#x20;

### B. The wider MITTERE family: “send, let go, release”

These words are etymological relatives, not all direct derivatives of remission. Their prefixes change the direction or nature of the “sending.” This is an especially productive pattern for decoding unfamiliar words.&#x20;

## 8. Mission (noun)

Meaning of components: mittere = send; -ion = action or result.

Literal meaning: An act of sending.

Semantic evolution: Sending an agent or group developed into an assigned task, purpose or organised undertaking.

Modern meaning: A task, objective or assignment; also an organisation or group sent for a particular purpose.

Common usage:

- Mission-mode implementation
- Diplomatic mission
- Mission-oriented approach

UPSC link: National missions, diplomatic relations and public-policy implementation.

## 9. Transmit (verb)

Meaning of components: trans- = across; mittere = send.

Literal meaning: Send across.

Semantic evolution: Physical transfer broadened into the communication or passage of information, energy and disease.

Modern meaning: To convey or pass something from one place, person or system to another.

Common usage:

- Transmission of infectious diseases
- Transmit information
- Transmit data electronically

Retention hook: transmit sends across; remit sends back.&#x20;

## 10. Transmission (noun)

Meaning of components: transmit = send across; -ion = process or result.

Literal meaning: The process of sending across.

Semantic evolution: The act of passing something developed into a general term for the transfer of disease, information, power or signals.

Modern meaning: The process by which something is transferred or conveyed.

Common usage:

- Disease transmission
- Intergenerational transmission of inequality
- Transmission of power

UPSC link: Public health, social stratification, culture and technology.

## 11. Submit (verb)

Meaning of components: sub- = under; mittere = send or let go.

Literal meaning: Place or send under.

Semantic evolution: Placing something under another's authority developed into yielding, presenting a document and referring a proposal for consideration.

Modern meaning: To yield to authority; to present something formally; or to put forward an argument.

Common usage:

- Submit a memorandum
- Submit to judicial scrutiny
- Submit that the policy requires reform

UPSC link: The expression “I submit that…” is a formal way of presenting an argument.&#x20;

## 12. Submission (noun)

Meaning of components: submit = place under or present; -ion = act or state.

Literal meaning: The act of placing under or presenting.

Semantic evolution: The word came to mean both yielding to authority and formally presenting material for consideration.

Modern meaning: The act of yielding, or a document or argument formally presented.

Common usage:

- Submission of a report
- Voluntary submission
- Written submissions before a court

Retention note: A submission can mean either yielding or a formal presentation; context determines which.

## 13. Admit (verb)

Meaning of components: ad- = to or toward; mittere = send or let go.

Literal meaning: Let in or allow entry.

Semantic evolution: Allowing entry developed into accepting a fact as true or acknowledging a mistake.

Modern meaning: To allow entry, accept as valid, or acknowledge something.

Common usage:

- Admit a patient to hospital
- Admit a mistake
- Admit evidence

UPSC link: Distinguish admit a fact from concede an argument; they can overlap but are not always interchangeable.&#x20;

## 14. Emission / Emit (noun / verb)

Meaning of components: e- (from ex-) = out; mittere = send.

Literal meaning: Send out.

Semantic evolution: Releasing something outward came to describe the release of gases, radiation, sound or light.

Modern meaning: Emit means to give out or release; emission is the act or amount released.

Common usage:

- Greenhouse-gas emissions
- Carbon emissions
- Emit radiation

UPSC link: Environment, climate change and energy policy.&#x20;

## 15. Commit / Commitment (verb / noun)

Meaning of components: com- = together; mittere = send, entrust or let go.

Literal meaning: Entrust or place into an undertaking.

Semantic evolution: Entrusting something developed into undertaking an obligation, dedicating oneself to a cause, or perpetrating an act.

Modern meaning: Commit means to undertake, pledge, entrust or carry out; commitment is an obligation, pledge or dedication.

Common usage:

- Commit a crime
- Commitment to constitutional values
- Political commitment to reform

Retention hook: commitment is what you bind yourself to.&#x20;

## 16. Permit / Permission (verb / noun)

Meaning of components: per- = through; mittere = send or let go.

Literal meaning: Let through.

Semantic evolution: Allowing something to pass developed into allowing an action or granting authorisation.

Modern meaning: Permit means allow or authorise; permission means consent or authorisation.

Common usage:

- Permit public assembly
- Environmental clearance and permits
- Seek permission from the competent authority

UPSC link: Regulation, administrative discretion and environmental governance.

## 17. Dismiss (verb)

Meaning of components: dis- = away or apart; mittere = send.

Literal meaning: Send away.

Semantic evolution: Sending someone away developed into ending employment, rejecting a claim or bringing proceedings to an end.

Modern meaning: To send away, reject, discharge or terminate.

Common usage:

- Dismiss a petition
- Dismiss an employee
- Dismiss concerns as unfounded

UPSC link: Judicial proceedings, public administration and critical evaluation.

## 18. Omit / Omission (verb / noun)

Meaning of components: Latin omittere combines ob- (with a form assimilated in the compound) and mittere = let go or send; the historical development is more complex than simply “send away.”

Literal meaning: Let go, leave out or fail to include.

Semantic evolution: Letting something pass developed into leaving it out, deliberately or accidentally.

Modern meaning: Omit means leave out; omission is the act or instance of leaving something out.

Common usage:

- Omission of relevant facts
- An omission in the report
- Acts of commission and omission

UPSC link: Ethics and governance—an omission may be as consequential as an action.

## 19. Intermittent / Intermit (adjective / verb)

Meaning of components: inter- = between; mittere = send or let go.

Literal meaning: Let something stop or pass between intervals.

Semantic evolution: The idea of intervals or interruptions developed into something that starts and stops rather than continuing steadily.

Modern meaning: Intermittent means occurring at irregular or separated intervals; intermit is a much rarer verb meaning to interrupt or cease temporarily.

Common usage:

- Intermittent rainfall
- Intermittent power supply
- Intermittent conflict

Retention hook: intermittent = happening with gaps; unremitting = continuing without easing.

## 3. One root, many directions

Use this mental map to retain the family. The prefix supplies the direction; mittere supplies the idea of sending or releasing.

## MITTERE

Send · release · let go

re- → back

Remit, remission, remittance

trans- → across

Transmit, transmission

sub- → under

Submit, submission

ad- → to / toward

Admit, admission

e- / ex- → out

Emit, emission

dis- → away

Dismiss, dismissal

This is a memory map of the Latin compounds, not a claim that every modern meaning can be derived literally from its prefix.
