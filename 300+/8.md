# WORD POWER & ETYMOLOGY

## 1. STEEPER



UPSC Vocabulary

Adjective · Comparative Degree



Word: Steeper\
Root word: Steep\
Pronunciation: /ˈstiːpər/\
Part of Speech: Adjective (comparative form of steep)

### 1. Meaning of the Components

| Component | Meaning                                                            |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Steep     | Having a sharp or almost vertical slope; rising or falling sharply |
| -er       | Suffix indicating comparison (more)                                |

Word formation: Steep + -er = Steeper

### 2. Literal Meaning

A slope or surface that rises or falls at a greater angle than another.

Example: The road becomes steeper as it approaches the mountain.

### 3. Semantic Evolution

The word steep comes from Old English stēap, meaning high, lofty or elevated.

Its meaning gradually developed through the association between height and the sharpness of an incline.

The semantic progression can be understood as:

STĒAP (Old English)

High, lofty, elevated

STEEP

Having a sharp or precipitous slope

FIGURATIVE MEANING

Excessive, difficult, demanding or sharply increasing

The comparative suffix -er makes it denote a greater degree of steepness.

### 4. Modern Meaning

Steeper means:

1. Having a sharper or more pronounced incline than something else.
2. Increasing or decreasing more rapidly.
3. Requiring greater effort, cost or difficulty.
4. Being more excessive or demanding than something else.

Illustrative examples:

- The Himalayan terrain becomes steeper at higher elevations.
- India faces a steeper demographic challenge in certain regions.
- Developing countries often face steeper borrowing costs.
- The transition towards a green economy may involve a steeper initial investment.

### 5. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

| Collocation            | Meaning / Context                                                      |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Steeper learning curve | Greater difficulty in acquiring a skill or understanding a subject     |
| Steeper fiscal deficit | A more pronounced increase in fiscal deficit (context-dependent usage) |
| Steeper economic costs | Higher or more substantial economic costs                              |

Additional high-value expressions:

- A steeper decline in GDP
- Steeper tariff barriers
- Steeper tax burden
- Steeper opportunity costs
- Steeper developmental challenges

UPSC Mains application:

"The transition towards sustainable development may impose steeper short-term economic costs, but it can generate substantial long-term environmental and social benefits."

From maximum physical inclination to the highest degree of difficulty, cost or intensity.

Adverb

To make something steeper or to become steeper.

Semantic Evolution

The physical process of increasing inclination developed into a metaphor for intensifying economic, social and environmental trends.

Modern Meaning

An ongoing increase in the severity, intensity or rate of change of something.

Common Usage

1. Steepening yield curve

"The steepening demographic decline in certain developed economies raises concerns regarding labour-force participation and pension sustainability."

One of the most useful ways to retain steep is to understand how a physical word becomes an analytical expression.

| Economy                 | Steep inflation                 | Rapid or substantial rise in prices       |
| Public finance          | Steep borrowing costs           | High cost of borrowing                    |
| Governance              | Steep administrative challenges | Considerable implementation difficulties  |

| Education               | Steep learning curve            | High initial learning difficulty          |
| International relations | Steep tariff barriers           | High tariff rates                         |
| Demography              | Steep population decline        | Rapid or substantial population reduction |

### Important conceptual distinction

Do not use steep indiscriminately as a synonym for high.

- Steep rise: A sharp or substantial increase.
- High rise: A large numerical level or value.
- Steep decline: A sharp decrease.
- Rapid decline: A decline occurring quickly.
- Steep cost: An unusually high or burdensome cost.

The word steep frequently conveys an impression of sharpness, intensity or disproportionate burden.

# PARADIGM — Complete Etymological Analysis & Word Family



UPSC Vocabulary Builder

## 1. PARADIGM

# PARADIGM

Pronunciation: /ˈpær.ə.daɪm/ (PAR-uh-dाइम)

Part of speech: Noun

### A. Meaning of the Components

The word paradigm comes from the Ancient Greek word parádeigma (παράδειγμα).

It can be broken down into two components:

| Component | Origin                | Meaning                               |
| --------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Para-     | Greek para (παρά)     | Beside, alongside, in comparison with |
| -deigma   | Greek deigma (δεῖγμα) | Example, specimen, something shown    |

The second component comes from the Greek verb deiknynai, meaning to show, point out or demonstrate.

Root memory: DEIK = TO SHOW / TO POINT OUT.

### B. Literal Meaning

Paradigm literally means:

An example shown alongside something to serve as a model or pattern for comparison.

Think of it this way:

An example is presented → others observe it → they use it as a model → a standard pattern emerges.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The word underwent the following development:

1. Ancient Greek: Parádeigma

   An example, pattern or model used to demonstrate something.
2. Classical Latin: Paradigma

   A model or example, particularly in grammatical and rhetorical contexts.
3. English: Paradigm

   A typical example, pattern or established way of understanding something.
4. Modern intellectual usage

   A comprehensive framework of assumptions, concepts, values and practices through which people understand and interpret reality.

An important development occurred in the philosophy of science, particularly through Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962).

Kuhn popularised the modern understanding of a paradigm as a shared framework of scientific assumptions, theories, methods and standards that guides a scientific community.

### D. Modern Meaning

Paradigm (noun):

1. A typical example or model of something.
2. A conceptual framework or established way of thinking that shapes how people understand and approach a subject.
3. An accepted pattern of ideas, practices and assumptions within a particular discipline or society.

UPSC-oriented interpretation:

A paradigm is not merely an individual idea or theory. It is a broader intellectual framework that influences how problems are identified, interpreted and addressed.

For example:

- A shift from state-centric governance to citizen-centric governance.
- A transition from a traditional economy to a knowledge-based economy.
- A shift from fossil-fuel dependence towards renewable energy.
- A transition from a welfare-oriented administrative approach to an empowerment-oriented approach.

### E. Common Usage: High-frequency UPSC Collocations

| Collocation          | Meaning                                                                          |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Paradigm shift       | A fundamental change in an established way of thinking or doing something.       |
| Dominant paradigm    | The prevailing framework of ideas or assumptions within a discipline or society. |
| Development paradigm | The conceptual framework guiding the understanding and pursuit of development.   |

UPSC-ready sentences:

1. India's transition towards citizen-centric administration represents a paradigm shift in public service delivery.
2. Sustainable development requires a departure from the conventional growth-oriented development paradigm.
3. The dominant paradigm of economic development must incorporate environmental sustainability and social equity.

# 2. Understanding the Etymological Root: DEIK

Before studying the word family, understand the underlying root.

# DEIK

## TO SHOW · TO POINT OUT

Proto-Indo-European root: \*deik- — to show, pronounce solemnly or point out.

This ancient root developed into related words in different Indo-European languages.

Greek branch

DEIK → deiknynai → deigma → paradigm, deictic, deixis.

Latin branch

DEIK → Latin dīcere (to say, declare) → diction, dictate, dictum, predict, contradict, edict, verdict.

Latin indicative branch

Latin index (a pointer, informer or indicator) → index, indicate, indication, indicator, indexation.

Important etymological distinction: These words belong to the same deeper Indo-European root family, but they did not all develop directly from the Greek word paradigm. Some travelled through Greek, others through Latin.

This distinction is important for accurate etymological learning.

# 3. The Extended Word Family of PARADIGM

We will organise the family into three branches for easier retention:

1. Greek branch: Words directly connected with showing, pointing out and demonstrating.
2. Latin branch – DIC/DIC(T): Words associated with saying, declaring, proclaiming and expressing.
3. Latin branch – INDIC/INDEX: Words associated with indicating, identifying and pointing out.

This is a conceptual family tree, not a claim that every word is a direct derivative of paradigm.

## BRANCH I — Greek-derived words: Showing, Demonstrating and Representing

### 1. PARADIGMATIC

High UPSC relevance

Meaning of the Components

- Paradigm = a model, pattern or conceptual framework.
- -atic = pertaining to or relating to.

Literal Meaning

Pertaining to a model, pattern or conceptual framework.

Semantic Evolution

Derived from paradigm, the adjective came to describe something that exemplifies a particular model or belongs to an established intellectual framework.

Modern Meaning

Relating to a paradigm; characteristic of a particular conceptual framework or serving as a typical example of something.

Common Usage

1. Paradigmatic example.
2. Paradigmatic shift.
3. Paradigmatic approach.

UPSC application:

The Indian Constitution represents a paradigmatic shift from colonial authoritarianism towards constitutional democracy.

### 2. PARADIGMATICALLY

Meaning of the Components

- Paradigm = model or framework.
- -atic = pertaining to.
- -ally = in a particular manner.

Literal Meaning

In a manner relating to a paradigm or conceptual framework.

Semantic Evolution

The adjective paradigmatic acquired an adverbial form to describe how something is understood, interpreted or demonstrated within a particular model.

Modern Meaning

In a way that exemplifies a particular model, pattern or conceptual framework.

Common Usage

1. Paradigmatically different.
2. Paradigmatically distinct.
3. Paradigmatically significant.

UPSC application:

The constitutional vision of India is paradigmatically different from the colonial conception of governance.

### 3. DEICTIC

Anthropology + Linguistics

Meaning of the Components

- Greek deiktikos = capable of showing or pointing out.
- Root deik- = to show or point.

Literal Meaning

Something that points out or indicates.

Semantic Evolution

The Greek root developed into a linguistic term describing expressions whose meanings depend on the context in which they are used.

For instance, the meaning of words such as I, you, here, there, now and then depends on the speaker, location and time.

Modern Meaning

In linguistics, deictic refers to words or expressions whose interpretation depends on the context of communication.

Common Usage

1. Deictic expressions.
2. Deictic reference.
3. Deictic markers.

UPSC application:

Language is not merely a system of abstract symbols; deictic expressions demonstrate how meaning is shaped by social and situational context.

Memory example:

\- "I am here now."

- I = speaker-dependent.
- Here = location-dependent.
- Now = time-dependent.

### 4. DEIXIS

Anthropology + Linguistics

Meaning of the Components

- Greek deixis (δεῖξις) = pointing out, showing or indicating.
- Root deik- = to show.

Literal Meaning

The act of pointing out or indicating.

Semantic Evolution

The term evolved from the general idea of demonstrating something to its specialised linguistic meaning: identifying people, places, times or relationships through context-dependent expressions.

Modern Meaning

A linguistic phenomenon in which the meaning of an expression depends on the context of its use.

Common Usage

1. Personal deixis.
2. Spatial deixis.
3. Temporal deixis.

UPSC application:

The study of deixis helps anthropologists and linguists understand the relationship between language, culture and social context.

Important distinction:

| Term    | Meaning                                                   |
| ------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Deixis  | The linguistic phenomenon of context-dependent reference. |
| Deictic | An expression or linguistic feature exhibiting deixis.    |

## BRANCH II — Latin-derived words: Saying, Declaring and Expressing

The Latin verb dīcere means to say, speak or declare.

It is historically related to the Proto-Indo-European root \*deik-.

This branch is particularly valuable for UPSC because it generates words frequently encountered in polity, governance, law, international relations, philosophy and essay writing.

### 5. DICTION

Meaning of the Components

- Latin dīcere = to say or speak.
- Diction developed through Latin dictio = speaking, expression or manner of speaking.

Literal Meaning

The act or manner of saying something.

Semantic Evolution

The original sense of speaking developed into the manner in which words are selected and expressed, particularly in speech and writing.

Modern Meaning

The choice and use of words in speech or writing; the manner of verbal expression.

Common Usage

1. Precise diction.
2. Formal diction.
3. Political diction.

UPSC application:

Effective answer writing requires precise diction, conceptual clarity and logically structured arguments.

### 6. DICTATE

Meaning of the Components

- Latin dictare = to say repeatedly, declare or prescribe.
- Derived from dicere = to say.

Literal Meaning

To say something for another person to write down, or to declare authoritatively.

Semantic Evolution

The original meaning of repeated or formal speaking expanded into the idea of issuing authoritative instructions or imposing conditions.

Modern Meaning

1. To give instructions or orders authoritatively.
2. To determine or influence how something must be done.
3. To speak words for another person to write down.

Common Usage

1. Dictate terms.
2. Dictate policy.
3. Economic compulsions dictate policy choices.

UPSC application:

Constitutional morality must guide governance rather than allowing political expediency to dictate administrative decisions.

Important distinction:

- Dictate = impose or determine.
- Dictation = the act of speaking for someone to write down.
- Dictator = a ruler exercising absolute or authoritarian power.

### 7. DICTATOR

Meaning of the Components

- Latin dictare = to declare or prescribe.
- Latin dictator = an appointed authoritative ruler.

Literal Meaning

A person who issues authoritative declarations or commands.

Semantic Evolution

In ancient Rome, a dictator was a magistrate appointed temporarily during extraordinary emergencies with extensive powers.

Over time, the term acquired its modern association with authoritarian rule and concentrated political power.

Modern Meaning

A ruler who exercises absolute or authoritarian power, generally without effective constitutional limitations.

Common Usage

1. Military dictator.
2. Authoritarian dictator.
3. Dictatorial regime.

UPSC application:

The concentration of executive authority without adequate institutional checks and balances can undermine constitutional democracy.

### 8. DICTATORSHIP

Meaning of the Components

- Dictator = an authoritarian ruler.
- -ship = state, condition or position.

Literal Meaning

The condition or system of rule by a dictator.

Semantic Evolution

The term evolved from the office of an authoritative ruler to a political system characterised by concentrated power and limited political accountability.

Modern Meaning

A system of government in which political power is concentrated in the hands of one individual or a small group, with limited democratic accountability.

Common Usage

1. Military dictatorship.
2. Authoritarian dictatorship.
3. Transition from dictatorship to democracy.

UPSC application:

Democratic institutions, judicial independence and civil liberties act as safeguards against the emergence of dictatorship.

### 9. DICTUM

Meaning of the Components

- Latin dictum = something said or declared.
- Derived from dicere = to say.

Literal Meaning

Something that has been said.

Semantic Evolution

The word came to refer to a memorable statement, authoritative pronouncement or established expression of wisdom.

In law, it also developed a specialised meaning relating to judicial observations.

Modern Meaning

A formal or authoritative statement; a well-known saying or principle.

In legal terminology, obiter dictum refers to a judicial observation that is not essential to the decision in a case.

Common Usage

1. Famous dictum.
2. Judicial dictum.
3. Obiter dictum.

UPSC application:

The constitutional dictum of equality before the law reinforces the principle of non-arbitrariness in state action.

Important legal distinction:

- Ratio decidendi: The legal principle essential to the court's decision; it has precedential force.
- Obiter dictum: An observation made in passing; generally persuasive rather than binding.

### 10. EDICT

Meaning of the Components

- Latin e- / ex- = out or forth.
- Latin dicere = to declare or say.

Literal Meaning

Something formally declared or proclaimed.

Semantic Evolution

The term originally referred to an official public proclamation, particularly one issued by a ruler or magistrate.

It later came to signify an authoritative order or command.

Modern Meaning

An official order, proclamation or directive issued by an authority.

Common Usage

1. Royal edict.
2. Government edict.
3. Administrative edict.

UPSC application:

In a constitutional democracy, executive edicts must operate within the limits of legislative competence and constitutional provisions.

### 11. PREDICT

Meaning of the Components

- Latin prae- = before.
- Latin dicere = to say or declare.

Literal Meaning

To say something beforehand.

Semantic Evolution

The word developed from the idea of declaring something in advance to its modern meaning of forecasting a future event based on evidence, patterns or reasoning.

Modern Meaning

To forecast or state what is likely to happen in the future.

Common Usage

1. Predict future trends.
2. Predict electoral outcomes.
3. Predict climate patterns.

UPSC application:

Demographic projections help policymakers predict future pressures on employment, healthcare and social infrastructure.

### 12. PREDICTION

Meaning of the Components

- Pre- = before.
- Diction / dict- = saying or declaring.
- -ion = act or process.

Literal Meaning

The act of saying something beforehand.

Semantic Evolution

The act of making a prior declaration evolved into the process of estimating future events using available information.

Modern Meaning

A statement or estimate about a future event or outcome.

Common Usage

1. Accurate prediction.
2. Climate prediction.
3. Economic predictions.

UPSC application:

Evidence-based policymaking relies on demographic and economic predictions, although uncertainty must be acknowledged.

Distinction:

- Predict = verb.
- Prediction = noun.
- Predictive = adjective.

### 13. CONTRADICT

Meaning of the Components

- Latin contra- = against.
- Latin dicere = to say or declare.

Literal Meaning

To say something against another statement.

Semantic Evolution

The word developed from expressing an opposing statement to denying, disputing or demonstrating the inconsistency of another claim.

Modern Meaning

To state the opposite of something; to oppose or dispute a statement, argument or position.

Common Usage

1. Contradict evidence.
2. Contradict established facts.
3. Contradict constitutional principles.

UPSC application:

Discriminatory social practices contradict the constitutional commitment to equality and dignity.

### 14. CONTRADICTION

Meaning of the Components

- Contra- = against.
- Dict = say or declare.
- -ion = act, process or condition.

Literal Meaning

The act or condition of saying something against another statement.

Semantic Evolution

The term developed into the broader idea of logical, factual or conceptual incompatibility between two statements, positions or conditions.

Modern Meaning

A situation in which two statements, ideas or facts are mutually inconsistent or opposed.

Common Usage

1. Internal contradiction.
2. Logical contradiction.
3. Structural contradiction.

UPSC application:

The persistence of extreme economic inequality alongside constitutional commitments to social and economic justice reflects a structural contradiction in society.

### 15. INTERDICT

Meaning of the Components

- Latin inter- = between or among.
- Latin dicere = to declare or say.

Literal Meaning

To declare something between or impose an authoritative prohibition.

Semantic Evolution

The word developed from an official prohibition to the act of prohibiting, restricting or preventing something.

Modern Meaning

To prohibit, forbid or prevent an activity through an authoritative order.

Common Usage

1. Judicial interdict.
2. Interdict illegal activities.
3. Interdiction of illicit trafficking.

UPSC application:

Effective border management requires interdicting transnational trafficking networks while safeguarding legitimate cross-border movement.

Related noun: Interdiction = the act of prohibiting or preventing something.

### 16. JURISDICTION

Very high UPSC relevance

Meaning of the Components

- Latin juris = of law or right (from jus, law).
- Latin dictio = saying, declaration or pronouncement.

Literal Meaning

The authority to declare or administer the law.

Semantic Evolution

The expression developed into the legal concept of a court's authority to hear and decide cases.

Its meaning subsequently expanded to include the geographical, subject-matter and institutional limits of legal authority.

Modern Meaning

The legal authority or power of a court, government or administrative body to exercise its functions within a specified territory or over a particular subject.

Common Usage

1. Territorial jurisdiction.
2. Original jurisdiction.
3. Subject-matter jurisdiction.

UPSC application:

The Supreme Court exercises original jurisdiction in certain disputes between the Union and States under Article 131 of the Constitution.

Remember: Jurisdiction is not simply geographical territory; it also concerns the legal competence to decide a matter.

### 17. VERDICT

High UPSC relevance

Meaning of the Components

- Latin verus = true.
- Latin dictum = something said or declared.

Literal Meaning

A true declaration or statement.

Semantic Evolution

The expression travelled through Anglo-French legal terminology and developed into the formal declaration of a decision by a jury.

Its modern use extends beyond jury decisions to judgments and conclusions more generally.

Modern Meaning

A formal decision or judgment, particularly one delivered by a jury; more generally, a final assessment or conclusion.

Common Usage

1. Supreme Court verdict.
2. Landmark verdict.
3. Final verdict.

UPSC application:

Landmark judicial verdicts have played an important role in expanding the interpretation of fundamental rights.

Related words:

- Veracity = truthfulness.
- Verify = establish the truth or accuracy of something.
- Verdict = formal decision.

Note that veracity and verify have a different Latin derivational route from verdict, although they share the broader idea of truth.

Speaking well or expressing good wishes.

Semantic Evolution

Meaning of the Components

Semantic Evolution

A curse or expression invoking harm, misfortune or condemnation.

Common Usage

### 20. INDICATE

Very high UPSC relevance

Meaning of the Components

- Latin dicare = to proclaim, declare or dedicate.
- Related historically to the Indo-European deik- family.

Literal Meaning

To point out or make something known.

Semantic Evolution

The original idea of formally declaring something developed into the broader meaning of showing, suggesting or signalling the presence of something.

Modern Meaning

To show, suggest, signal or point out something, either directly or indirectly.

Common Usage

1. Indicate a trend.
2. Indicate a preference.

Meaning of the Components

- Indicate = point out or show.
- -ion = act, process or result.

Literal Meaning

The act or result of pointing something out.

Semantic Evolution

The term expanded from the act of showing something to a sign, symptom or piece of evidence suggesting a particular condition or outcome.

Modern Meaning

A sign, signal or piece of evidence that suggests the existence or likelihood of something.

Common Usage

1. Clear indication.
2. Early indication.
3. Indication of structural change.

A decline in the multidimensional poverty index provides an important indication of improvements in living standards.

### 22. INDICATOR

Very high UPSC relevance

Meaning of the Components

- Indicate = show or point out.
- -or = a person or thing that performs an action.

Literal Meaning

Something that shows or points out a condition.

Semantic Evolution

The word developed from a general pointer or sign to a measurable variable used to assess a condition, process or outcome.

Modern Meaning

A measurable variable or sign used to assess, monitor or evaluate a particular phenomenon.

Common Usage

1. Key performance indicators (KPIs).
2. Socio-economic indicators.
3. Development indicators.

UPSC application:

The Human Development Index uses health, education and income indicators to assess development beyond economic growth.

Important distinction:

An indicator is not necessarily the phenomenon itself; it is a measurable representation of that phenomenon.

### 23. INDEX

Very high UPSC relevance

Meaning of the Components

- Latin index = one who points out, a sign or an indicator.
- Associated with Latin indicare and the broader deik- root family.

Literal Meaning

Something that points out or indicates.

Semantic Evolution

Originally associated with a pointer or informer, the term developed into a sign, list or numerical measure used to locate, identify or assess something.

Modern Meaning

1. A numerical measure used to track changes in a variable or group of variables.
2. An alphabetical list of subjects or entries.
3. A statistical measure representing the relative level of something.

Common Usage

1. Consumer Price Index (CPI).
2. Human Development Index (HDI).
3. Global Hunger Index (GHI).

UPSC application:

Composite indices help policymakers compare development outcomes across countries, although their methodology and choice of indicators must be critically examined.

### 24. INDEXATION

Meaning of the Components

- Index = a measure or indicator.
- -ation = the process or action of doing something.

Literal Meaning

The process of linking something to an index.

Semantic Evolution

The term developed into a technical economic and administrative concept involving adjustments linked to changes in a designated index.

Modern Meaning

The adjustment of a value, payment, tax parameter or financial variable according to changes in a specified index, often to account for inflation.

Common Usage

1. Inflation indexation.
2. Wage indexation.
3. Tax indexation.

UPSC application:

Indexation of social security benefits can help protect the purchasing power of vulnerable populations against inflation.

### 25. INDEXICAL

Meaning of the Components

- Index = something that points out or indicates.
- -ical = relating to or characteristic of.

Literal Meaning

Relating to an indicator or something that points towards another thing.

Semantic Evolution

The word acquired specialised meanings in semiotics and linguistics, where an indexical sign is connected to its referent through an actual or contextual relationship.

Modern Meaning

In semiotics, something is indexical when it indicates or points to something through a direct, causal or contextual connection.

Common Usage

1. Indexical signs.
2. Indexical meaning.
3. Indexical relationship.

UPSC application:

In anthropology, footprints, smoke and ritual objects can function as indexical signs because they point towards an event, activity or cultural meaning.

Memory examples:

- Smoke → fire.
- Footprints → the presence or movement of a person.
- A fever → possible infection.

These are examples of indexical relationships, not necessarily conclusive proof of the inferred cause.

## 6. High-value distinctions for UPSC

These distinctions will help you use the vocabulary accurately in Mains answers.

| Closely related words                | Difference                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Paradigm vs Theory                   | A paradigm is a broader framework; a theory is a specific explanatory proposition or system.                                |
| Paradigm vs Model                    | A model represents a particular system; a paradigm shapes the broader way of understanding it.                              |
| Paradigm shift vs Incremental change | A paradigm shift changes the underlying framework; incremental change modifies existing arrangements.                       |
| Indicate vs Prove                    | Indicate means suggest or signal; prove means establish through adequate evidence.                                          |
| Indicator vs Index                   | An indicator is a measurable variable or sign; an index is often a composite or organised measure.                          |

| Contradiction vs Paradox             | A contradiction involves incompatible propositions; a paradox is an apparently contradictory or counterintuitive situation. |
| Jurisdiction vs Authority            | Jurisdiction is legally defined competence; authority is the broader power or right to act.                                 |

## 7. Using the word family in UPSC answer writing

The real advantage of etymological learning is not merely vocabulary acquisition. It is the ability to express complex arguments with precision.

Consider this basic statement:

"India needs to change its approach to development."

We can elevate it using the vocabulary learned above:

Improved Mains expression:

India needs a paradigm shift from a predominantly GDP-centric development paradigm towards an inclusive, sustainable and human-centric model. Conventional economic indicators alone are insufficient to indicate the multidimensional nature of development. Therefore, a comprehensive set of socio-economic indicators must guide evidence-based policymaking.

Another example:

Basic statement:

"Some government policies go against constitutional values."

Improved Mains expression:

Policies that perpetuate structural inequalities contradict the constitutional vision of social justice and may undermine the transformative paradigm envisaged by the Constitution.

4. Evidence-based policy formulation: Policy development guided by empirical evidence and measurable indicators.

# WORD POWER MADE EASY: CELIBACY

Etymology • Semantic Evolution • Word Family • UPSC Vocabulary

The best way to learn a word is not merely to memorise its dictionary definition but to understand its linguistic construction, historical development and semantic connections.

# PART I — THE ROOT WORD: CELIBACY

## 1. CELIBACY

# CELIBACY

UPSC relevant

Pronunciation: /ˈsel.ɪ.bə.si/\
Part of speech: Noun\
Root: Latin caelebs\
Central idea: The state of being unmarried, particularly through a deliberate choice or religious commitment.

### A. Meaning of the Components

| Component | Origin                      | Meaning                     |
| --------- | --------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Caelebs   | Latin                       | Unmarried person            |
| -acy      | Latin -acia, through French | State, condition or quality |

Important etymological note: Caelebs is the historical root. Do not interpret cael and -ebs as two independently meaningful English word components. The deeper origin of the Latin word is uncertain.

### B. Literal Meaning

Celibacy = The condition or state of being unmarried.

Originally, the word was associated with the unmarried condition of a person.

Over time, it acquired a more specialised meaning involving voluntary abstention from marriage and sexual relations.

### C. Semantic Evolution

1. Classical Latin: Caelebs

   Referred to an unmarried person.
2. Latin: Caelibatus

   Denoted the condition or state of remaining unmarried.
3. Religious and institutional usage

   Became associated particularly with religious commitments to abstain from marriage and sexual relationships.
4. Modern English: Celibacy

   Refers to voluntary abstinence from marriage and, commonly, sexual activity, whether for religious, personal, philosophical or other reasons.

### D. Modern Meaning

Celibacy (noun):

The voluntary decision to abstain from marriage and, particularly in religious contexts, sexual relations.

It may arise from:

1. Religious conviction: A spiritual commitment to avoid marriage and sexual relationships.
2. Personal choice: A conscious decision to remain unmarried.
3. Philosophical commitment: A belief that sexual restraint facilitates self-discipline or spiritual development.
4. Institutional requirement: A prescribed condition for certain religious offices or vocations.

Important conceptual distinction:

Celibacy does not always mean simply being unmarried.

- An unmarried person may still be sexually active.
- A person practising celibacy deliberately abstains from sexual relationships.
- In some contexts, celibacy specifically means abstaining from marriage, without necessarily implying lifelong sexual abstinence.

### E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

| Collocation        | Meaning                                                                           |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Religious celibacy | Abstinence from marriage and sexual relations for religious reasons               |
| Clerical celibacy  | The practice or institutional requirement of celibacy among members of the clergy |
| Voluntary celibacy | Deliberate personal choice to abstain from marriage or sexual relationships       |

UPSC application:

- Sociology: Celibacy as a form of social and religious regulation of sexuality.
- Anthropology: Celibacy, kinship systems, religious institutions and reproductive behaviour.
- GS-I: Marriage, family, changing social institutions and gender relations.
- Essay: Individual autonomy versus institutional and religious expectations.

This distinction is important for both linguistic accuracy and vocabulary retention.

### E. Common Usage

Pronunciation: /ˈhɒɡ.bæk/ (HOG-back)

Part of speech: Noun

### A. Meaning of the Components

The word is a compound of two Old English-derived words:

- Hog: A pig, particularly a domesticated swine. The word is also associated with the idea of a hog's characteristic bulky body and curved back.
- Back: The rear or upper surface of the body, particularly the part extending along the spine.

### B. Literal Meaning

Hog + Back = A ridge resembling the curved back of a hog.

The name comes from the resemblance between an elongated geological ridge and the arched back of a pig.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The expression moved from a physical resemblance to a specialised geographical term:

1. Physical association: The curved, elongated back of a hog.
2. Visual analogy: An elongated ridge resembling the animal's back.
3. Geomorphological terminology: A narrow, steep-sided ridge formed by the differential erosion of inclined, resistant rock strata.

### D. Modern Meaning

Hogback: A long, narrow, sharp-crested ridge formed by the erosion of steeply inclined, resistant rock layers, usually with steep slopes on both sides.

Geographical significance:

- Associated with inclined or steeply dipping sedimentary strata.
- Resistant rock beds withstand erosion better than surrounding softer rocks.
- Differential erosion produces a prominent, elongated ridge.
- Commonly discussed in structural geomorphology and denudational landforms.

Remember: A hogback is distinguished by its relatively steep and nearly symmetrical slopes, unlike a typical cuesta, which has a gentle dip slope and a steep scarp slope.

### E. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

1. Hogback ridge — An elongated, narrow ridge produced by differential erosion.
2. Hogback topography — A landscape characterised by steeply inclined resistant rock strata.
3. Structural landform — A landform whose morphology is strongly influenced by the geological structure of underlying rocks.

UPSC application: Hogbacks illustrate the relationship between lithology, geological structure, differential erosion and landscape evolution.

### F. Etymological Word Family: HOGBACK

An important distinction in vocabulary building:

Hogback is a compound word, not a word built from a productive Latin or Greek root. Therefore, it does not have a large family of academically useful derivatives.

Its directly related forms are:

| Word       | Meaning                                     |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Hogback    | A steep-sided geological ridge              |
| Hog's-back | Alternative spelling of hogback             |
| Hogsback   | Alternative spelling                        |
| Hogbacked  | Having the shape or appearance of a hogback |

The word is recorded from the seventeenth century, with its formation transparently explained as hog + back.&#x20;

# 2. THE BACK WORD FAMILY

The word back originally denotes the rear part of the human or animal body. Through metaphorical extension, it has acquired meanings involving support, reversal, opposition, background, retreat and responsibility.

The following words share the same English lexical component, although they do not all have the same immediate historical derivation.

## 2.1 BACKBONE

Part of speech: Noun

A. Meaning of the Components

- Back: Rear portion of the body.
- Bone: Hard structural component of the skeleton.

B. Literal Meaning

The series of bones forming the central supporting structure of the back.

C. Semantic Evolution

Physical spinal support → central structural support → the most important or indispensable part of an organisation, system or institution.

D. Modern Meaning

The essential supporting element of something; the principal source of strength or stability.

E. Common Usage

1. Backbone of the economy — The principal sector supporting economic activity.
2. Institutional backbone — Core institutions that sustain governance.
3. Backbone of rural development — The essential infrastructure or sector supporting rural progress.

UPSC application: MSMEs are often described as an important backbone of employment generation and economic activity.

## 2.2 BACKWARD

Part of speech: Adjective / Adverb

A. Meaning of the Components

- Back: Towards the rear or opposite direction.
- -ward: An Old English directional suffix indicating movement towards something.

B. Literal Meaning

Moving towards the back or in a reverse direction.

C. Semantic Evolution

Reverse movement → lack of progress → relative social, economic, educational or technological underdevelopment.

D. Modern Meaning

Underdeveloped, lagging behind, or characterised by inadequate progress.

E. Common Usage

1. Backward regions — Regions experiencing relative developmental disadvantages.
2. Backward communities — Communities historically facing social and educational disadvantages.
3. Backward linkages — Connections between an industry and its suppliers of inputs.

UPSC application: Regional disparities and the development of historically disadvantaged communities are important dimensions of inclusive growth.

Terminological caution: In Indian constitutional and administrative discourse, use precise expressions such as socially and educationally backward classes where appropriate rather than casually labelling communities as backward.

## 2.3 BACKLASH

## 2.4 BACKTRACK

## 2.5 BACKSTOP

A safeguard, reserve mechanism or last line of protection.
