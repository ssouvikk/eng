## 1. CLASTIC

Pronunciation: /ˈklæs.tɪk/\
Part of speech: Adjective\
Primary domain: Geology (Sedimentology)

### A. Meaning of the Components

The word clastic originates from the Greek root:

| Component         | Origin             | Meaning                    |
| ----------------- | ------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Klastos (κλαστός) | Ancient Greek      | Broken, fractured          |
| Klan (κλᾶν)       | Ancient Greek verb | To break                   |
| -ic               | English suffix     | Pertaining to, relating to |

Central root: KLAST / KLAS = TO BREAK

The fundamental idea is something that has been broken into smaller fragments or pieces.

### B. Literal Meaning

Clastic = Pertaining to broken fragments.

Something clastic consists of fragments produced by the breaking down of larger materials.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The evolution of the word can be understood in three stages:

1. Physical breaking: The Greek root klastos referred to something broken or fragmented.
2. Geological application: Geologists adopted the term to describe sediments composed of fragments of pre-existing rocks and minerals.
3. Scientific classification: The term became established in sedimentology to classify rocks according to their fragmental composition and formation.

### D. Modern Meaning

Clastic: Describing sedimentary materials or rocks composed of fragments (clasts) of pre-existing rocks, minerals or other geological materials.

For example:

- Sandstone is a clastic sedimentary rock.
- Conglomerate is a clastic sedimentary rock containing rounded rock fragments.
- Shale is a fine-grained clastic sedimentary rock.

## WORD 1. CLAST

Pronunciation: /klæst/\
Part of speech: Noun\
Domain: Geology

### A. Meaning of the Components

- Klastos: Greek – broken.
- Clast: A fragment or particle of a previously existing rock or mineral.

### B. Literal Meaning

A broken piece or fragment.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The general Greek idea of something broken developed into a technical geological term for individual fragments of rocks, minerals or other materials.

### D. Modern Meaning

A clast is an individual fragment of a pre-existing rock, mineral or other material that contributes to the formation of sedimentary deposits or rocks.

Examples:

- A sand grain can be a clast.
- A pebble in a conglomerate is a clast.
- Fragments of older rocks transported by rivers are clasts.

## WORD 2. PYROCLAST

Domain: Volcanology

| Component | Meaning                         |
| --------- | ------------------------------- |
| Pyro-     | Greek pyr – fire                |
| -clast    | Greek klastos – broken fragment |

### B. Literal Meaning

A fragment produced by fire.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The combination of fire and breaking acquired a specific geological meaning in volcanology: fragments generated during explosive volcanic eruptions.

### D. Modern Meaning

A pyroclast is a fragment of volcanic material ejected during an explosive volcanic eruption.

Pyroclasts may consist of:

## WORD 4. BIOCLAST

Domain: Sedimentology / Palaeontology

### A. Meaning of the Components

| Component | Meaning           |
| --------- | ----------------- |
| Bio-      | Greek bios – life |
| -clast    | Broken fragment   |

### B. Literal Meaning

A fragment derived from living organisms.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The root combination came to describe fragments of biological material preserved in sediments, particularly the skeletal remains of marine organisms.

### D. Modern Meaning

A bioclast is a fragment of biological material, commonly the skeletal remains of organisms, found within sediment or sedimentary rock.

Examples include:

- Shell fragments.
- Coral fragments.
- Broken pieces of fossils.
- Skeletal remains of marine organisms.

## WORD 6. VOLCANICLASTIC

Domain: Geology / Volcanology

- Volcano: Refers to volcanic origin or association.
- Clastic: Fragmental in nature.

- Volcanic rock fragments transported by rivers.
- Deposits formed by erosion and redeposition of volcanic material.

## WORD 7. CATACLASIS

Part of speech: Noun\
Domain: Structural Geology

### A. Meaning of the Components

- Cata-: Greek prefix meaning down, against or thoroughly, depending on the formation.
- Klasis: Greek – breaking or fracture.

The concept of breaking acquired a specialised geological meaning associated with the mechanical fragmentation of rocks during deformation, especially along faults and shear zones.

Cataclasis is the mechanical fracturing, crushing and fragmentation of rocks during tectonic deformation, often under brittle conditions.

## WORD 14. CLASTOGEN

Pronunciation: /ˈklæs.tə.dʒən/\
Part of speech: Noun\
Domain: Genetics / Environmental Biology / Toxicology

### A. Meaning of the Components

- Clasto-: Breaking or fragmentation.
- -gen: Producing or generating.

### B. Literal Meaning

Something that produces breakage.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The idea of breaking was extended to chromosome damage in genetics and toxicology, producing a term for agents that cause structural breaks in chromosomes.

### D. Modern Meaning

A clastogen is a physical, chemical or biological agent that causes chromosome breakage or structural chromosomal damage.

Examples:

- Certain ionising radiation exposures.
- Certain chemical substances.
- Some environmental genotoxic agents.

### E. Common Usage

1. Clastogenic agents – Agents capable of causing chromosome breaks.
2. Clastogenic effects – Effects involving structural chromosome damage.
3. Clastogenic activity – The capacity of an agent to induce chromosome breakage.

UPSC linkage: GS-III – Environmental pollution, hazardous chemicals, radiation, biotechnology and public health.

Memory trick: CLAST = BREAK; GEN = PRODUCE. A clastogen produces chromosome breakage.

## WORD 15. CLASTOGENIC

Pronunciation: /ˌklæs.təˈdʒen.ɪk/\
Part of speech: Adjective\
Domain: Genetics / Environmental Toxicology

### A. Meaning of the Components

- Clasto-: Breaking.
- -genic: Producing or causing.

### B. Literal Meaning

Having the capacity to cause breakage.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The term emerged from genetic toxicology to describe substances or exposures that cause structural chromosome damage.

### D. Modern Meaning

Clastogenic describes an agent, exposure or effect that induces chromosome breakage or related structural chromosomal aberrations.

### E. Common Usage

1. Clastogenic potential – The capacity of a substance to cause chromosome damage.

## A. Meaning of the Components

Mechanical = Mechan(e) + -ical

| Component        | Meaning                                       |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Greek mēkhanē    | Device, contrivance, machine, ingenious means |
| Latin mechanicus | Pertaining to machines or mechanics           |

Root idea: A device or contrivance designed to accomplish something through an arrangement of parts.

## B. Literal Meaning

Mechanical = Relating to a machine, its working principles, or the operation of machinery.

At its most basic level, something mechanical involves physical forces, moving parts, machinery or an organised system of operations.

## C. Semantic Evolution

The word underwent the following conceptual development:

Stage 1: Ancient Greek

Mēkhanē — a device, contrivance or ingenious means of accomplishing something.

Stage 2: Latin

Mechanicus — relating to machines and mechanical arts.

Stage 3: Scientific development

Associated with mechanics, physical forces, motion and the operation of machines.

Stage 4: Modern figurative meaning

Something performed automatically, routinely or without conscious thought; or an explanation that reduces phenomena to mechanical causes.

## D. Modern Meaning

Mechanical (adjective) has four important meanings:

1\. Machine-related: Connected with machinery, engines or physical devices.

Example: Mechanical engineering deals with the design and operation of machines.

2\. Automatic or routine: Performed habitually, without much thought, creativity or conscious involvement.

Example: Mechanical compliance with rules does not necessarily produce good governance.

3\. Physical or force-based: Relating to the laws governing motion, forces and physical interactions.

Example: Mechanical forces influence the movement of tectonic plates.

4\. Rigid or unthinking: Characterised by a lack of flexibility, independent judgement or human understanding.

Example: A mechanical application of constitutional provisions may overlook the spirit of the Constitution.

Conceptual distinction: Mechanical is not necessarily negative. It can describe technical precision, routine behaviour or rigid thinking depending on the context.

## 3.1 MECHANICAL SOLIDARITY

Anthropology Optional – High priority

### A. Meaning of the Components

Mechanical solidarity = Mechanical + Solidarity

- Mechanical: Based on similarity, uniformity or common characteristics.
- Solidarity: Social cohesion, unity or collective bonding.

### B. Literal Meaning

Social cohesion arising from the similarity of individuals and their shared characteristics.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The expression became prominent through the sociological work of Émile Durkheim.

Durkheim used it to explain the nature of social integration in relatively traditional societies, where individuals share common beliefs, values, occupations and ways of life.

The social bond operates through collective consciousness rather than extensive occupational specialisation.

### D. Modern Meaning

Mechanical solidarity refers to a form of social cohesion based on similarity among members of society, particularly their shared beliefs, values, traditions and collective conscience.

It is generally associated with societies characterised by:

- Low division of labour.
- Strong collective conscience.
- Relatively high similarity among individuals.
- Repressive law.
- Limited occupational specialisation.

### E. Common Usage

| Collocation           | Meaning                                                           |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mechanical solidarity | Social cohesion based on similarity                               |
| Collective conscience | Shared beliefs and moral sentiments of a society                  |
| Repressive law        | Law emphasising punishment and the protection of collective norms |

UPSC Anthropology application:

Durkheim contrasted mechanical solidarity with organic solidarity.

| Mechanical solidarity                           | Organic solidarity                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Based on similarity                             | Based on interdependence                           |
| Limited division of labour                      | Extensive division of labour                       |
| Strong collective conscience                    | Greater individual differentiation                 |
| Repressive law                                  | Restitutive law                                    |
| Generally associated with traditional societies | Generally associated with modern complex societies |

Answer-writing sentence:

Durkheim argued that the transition from mechanical to organic solidarity reflects the transformation of social integration from homogeneity and collective consciousness towards functional interdependence arising from an advanced division of labour.

Important conceptual caution: Mechanical solidarity does not mean that society is literally operated like a machine. Here, mechanical signifies cohesion through likeness and common consciousness.

## 3.2 MECHANICAL ADVANTAGE

### A. Meaning of the Components

Mechanical advantage = Mechanical + Advantage

- Mechanical: Relating to machines and physical forces.
- Advantage: Benefit or favourable position.

### B. Literal Meaning

The advantage obtained by using a machine to perform work.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The concept developed through classical mechanics and the study of simple machines such as levers, pulleys and inclined planes.

### D. Modern Meaning

Mechanical advantage is the ratio of the output force produced by a machine to the input force applied to it.

\\[ MA=\frac{\text{Output force}}{\text{Input force}} \\]

A machine with a mechanical advantage greater than one multiplies the applied force, although it does not create energy.

### E. Common Usage

| Collocation                     | Meaning                                         |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Mechanical advantage of a lever | Force multiplication achieved by a lever        |
| Simple machines                 | Basic devices such as levers and pulleys        |
| Force multiplication            | Increasing output force relative to input force |

UPSC application: Useful in basic science and technology, particularly when discussing simple machines and engineering principles.

# PART I — DEROGATORY

## 1. Meaning of the Components

Derogatory originates from the Latin verb derogare.

| Component | Meaning                                           |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| de-       | Away, down, removal or diminution                 |
| rogare    | To ask, propose or put forward (especially a law) |
| -ory      | Pertaining to, or having the nature of            |

The crucial root is ROGARE = TO ASK / PROPOSE.

In Roman legislative terminology, rogare was associated with proposing a law, while derogare referred to taking away or modifying part of an existing law.

## 2. Literal Meaning

Derogatory = Having the tendency to take away from, diminish or detract from something.

Originally, the idea was that of partially withdrawing or diminishing the force of something, particularly a law.

## 3. Semantic Evolution

The word underwent an interesting transformation:

1. Roman legal context: Derogare meant to repeal, modify or diminish the authority of an existing law.
2. Generalisation: The meaning expanded to include taking away from the value, dignity or importance of something.
3. Social and linguistic usage: It acquired the sense of expressing disrespect, belittlement or contempt.
4. Modern usage: It primarily describes remarks, statements or attitudes that show disrespect or diminish someone's dignity, reputation or standing.

## 4. Modern Meaning

Derogatory (adjective): Expressing disrespect, contempt or disparagement; tending to belittle or undermine someone's dignity, reputation or worth.

In simple words:

Something derogatory does not merely criticise; it tends to lower someone's dignity or standing.

Important distinction:

- Critical = Expressing an assessment of shortcomings.
- Disparaging = Expressing a low opinion of someone or something.
- Derogatory = Showing disrespect or belittling someone or something.
- Defamatory = Making a false statement that harms someone's reputation.

## 5. Common Usage: UPSC-Relevant Collocations

1. Derogatory remarks — Statements that insult or belittle an individual or group.
2. Derogatory references to marginalised communities — Disrespectful references that undermine the dignity of vulnerable groups.
3. Derogatory language — Language expressing contempt, prejudice or disrespect.

UPSC application:

"Constitutional morality requires public institutions to uphold individual dignity and refrain from derogatory expressions that reinforce social discrimination."

Memory hook: DEROGATORY → DECREASE IN DIGNITY.

# 10. PREROGATIVE

Noun



### Meaning of the Components

- rogare = To ask or propose.

### Literal Meaning

## C. Semantic Evolution

The semantic development can be understood in three stages:

1. Original Latin sense: To entangle, perplex or involve in difficulties.
2. Intermediate sense: To make something complicated through multiple interconnections.
3. Modern sense: Characterised by elaborate details, complexity and interconnected components.

The word gradually moved from the idea of physical entanglement to intellectual, administrative, legal and structural complexity.

## D. Modern Meaning

Intricate (adjective): Extremely detailed, elaborate or complicated because of numerous interconnected elements.

It can have both positive and negative connotations depending on context.

- Positive: An intricate architectural design reflects craftsmanship and sophistication.
- Neutral: An intricate constitutional arrangement contains multiple institutional relationships.
- Negative: An intricate bureaucratic procedure creates administrative difficulties.

Important distinction:

Intricate does not necessarily mean poorly designed or unnecessarily complicated. It can describe something beautifully elaborate as well as something difficult to comprehend.

# 3. INTRICACY

NOUN | Pronunciation: IN-tri-kuh-see

Meaning: The quality or state of being intricate; complexity arising from numerous interconnected details.

### A. Meaning of the Components

- In- = Into, within.
- Tricae = Entanglements, perplexities.
- -acy = A suffix forming nouns that denote a quality, condition or state.

### B. Literal Meaning

The condition of being entangled in numerous interconnected details.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The adjective intricate describes something complicated or elaborate. Its noun form, intricacy, denotes the characteristic or quality responsible for that complexity.

### D. Modern Meaning

The quality of being complicated, elaborate or composed of numerous interconnected details.

Example: The intricacy of India's federal structure reflects the need to balance national unity with regional autonomy.

### E. Common Usage

1. Intricacies of governance – Complexities involved in administration and policymaking.
2. Intricacies of constitutional law – Detailed relationships among constitutional provisions and judicial interpretations.
3. Intricacies of social relations – Complex interactions among social groups and institutions.

UPSC keyword: Institutional complexity.

# 4. INTRICATELY

ADVERB | Pronunciation: IN-tri-kit-lee

Meaning: In a highly elaborate, detailed or interconnected manner.

### A. Meaning of the Components

- Intricate = Complicated or elaborate.
- -ly = In a particular manner or way.

### B. Literal Meaning

In an entangled or elaborately interconnected manner.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The adjective intricate developed an adverbial form to describe the manner in which something is constructed, organised or connected.

### D. Modern Meaning

Involving numerous interconnected elements, often with considerable detail or sophistication.

### E. Common Usage

1. Intricately interconnected systems – Systems with multiple interdependent components.
2. Intricately woven social fabric – A society whose communities, identities and institutions are closely interconnected.
3. Intricately designed institutional mechanisms – Institutional arrangements involving detailed procedures and relationships.

UPSC application:

"Environmental sustainability is intricately linked to economic development, social justice and intergenerational equity."

# 5. EXTRICATE

Important antonym of INTRICATE

VERB | Pronunciation: EK-stri-kayt

Meaning: To free someone or something from a difficult, complicated or entangled situation.

### A. Meaning of the Components

| Component | Meaning                     |
| --------- | --------------------------- |
| Ex-       | Out of, away from           |
| Tricae    | Perplexities, entanglements |

### B. Literal Meaning

To pull something out of an entanglement or free it from difficulties.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The Latin extricāre conveyed the idea of disentangling or freeing something from complications.

Its modern meaning expanded beyond physical entanglement to include freeing individuals, institutions and countries from difficult circumstances.

### D. Modern Meaning

To remove oneself or another person from a complicated, difficult or undesirable situation.

Example: India must extricate vulnerable communities from the cycle of poverty through inclusive development.

### E. Common Usage

1. Extricate oneself from a crisis – Free oneself from a difficult situation.
2. Extricate the economy from a debt trap – Help an economy escape unsustainable debt.
3. Extricate oneself from a diplomatic impasse – Find a way out of a diplomatic deadlock.

UPSC application:

"Structural reforms are necessary to extricate the rural economy from the vicious cycle of low productivity, indebtedness and poverty."

# 6. EXTRICATION

NOUN | Pronunciation: EK-stri-KAY-shun

Meaning: The act or process of freeing someone or something from a difficult, complicated or entangled situation.

### A. Meaning of the Components

- Ex- = Out of.
- Tricae = Entanglements, perplexities.
- -ation = The process or act of doing something.

### B. Literal Meaning

The process of getting out of an entanglement.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The verb extricate developed a corresponding noun, extrication, to describe the process or outcome of becoming free from a difficult situation.

### D. Modern Meaning

The process of removing oneself or something from a complicated, restrictive or problematic condition.

### E. Common Usage

1. Economic extrication from a debt trap – Escaping unsustainable indebtedness.
2. Diplomatic extrication from a geopolitical crisis – Finding a way out of an international political predicament.
3. Extrication from structural poverty – Overcoming deeply rooted socioeconomic disadvantages.

UPSC application:

"Financial inclusion, skill development and social protection are essential for the extrication of vulnerable households from chronic poverty."

# 7. INEXTRICABLE

High-value UPSC vocabulary

ADJECTIVE | Pronunciation: in-EK-stri-kuh-bul

Meaning: So closely interconnected that separation is extremely difficult or impossible.

### A. Meaning of the Components

| Component | Meaning                    |
| --------- | -------------------------- |
| In-       | Not (negative prefix here) |
| Ex-       | Out of                     |
| Tricae    | Entanglements              |
| -able     | Capable of being           |

Important: The first in- in inextricable is a negative prefix, unlike the in- in intricate.

### B. Literal Meaning

Not capable of being disentangled or separated.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The term developed from the idea of an entanglement that cannot be undone to describe relationships, connections and circumstances that cannot easily be separated.

### D. Modern Meaning

So closely connected or intertwined that the constituent elements cannot be separated without fundamentally altering the whole.

It can describe both positive interdependence and problematic relationships.

### E. Common Usage

1. Inextricable link between development and environment – The two cannot be understood independently.
2. Inextricable relationship between caste and politics – Deeply intertwined social and political structures.
3. Inextricable connection between rights and duties – Two closely interconnected normative concepts.

UPSC application:

"Human development and environmental sustainability share an inextricable relationship, making ecological considerations integral to development planning."

# 8. INEXTRICABLY

ADVERB | Pronunciation: in-EK-stri-kuh-blee

Meaning: In a manner that makes separation extremely difficult or impossible; inseparably.

### A. Meaning of the Components

- In- = Not.
- Ex- = Out of.
- Tricae = Entanglements.
- -able = Capable of.
- -ly = In a particular manner.

### B. Literal Meaning

In a manner that cannot be disentangled.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The adverbial form of inextricable developed to describe the nature of relationships or connections that are inseparable.

### D. Modern Meaning

Inseparably or so closely interconnected that one cannot be considered independently of the other.

### E. Common Usage

1. Inextricably linked to economic growth – Closely and inseparably connected with economic growth.
2. Inextricably intertwined with social identity – Deeply interwoven with social identity.
3. Inextricably connected with national security – Closely connected with national security.

UPSC application:

"Internal security is inextricably linked to inclusive development, effective governance and public trust."

# 9. TRICATE (Historical / Rare)

VERB | Obsolete or extremely rare

Meaning: An old English form associated with entangling or perplexing.

### A. Meaning of the Components

- Derived from Latin tricae = Difficulties or entanglements.
- Related historically to intricate and extricate.

### B. Literal Meaning

To entangle or involve in difficulties.

### C. Semantic Evolution

The form appeared in older English usage but did not survive as a productive word in contemporary standard English.

### D. Modern Meaning

It is not a practical modern vocabulary word. It is included here only to illustrate the historical root family.

### E. Common Usage

No meaningful contemporary UPSC collocations. Prefer entangle, complicate or perplex.

# 12. SEMANTIC NEIGHBOURS: WORDS OFTEN CONFUSED

These words are useful alternatives in UPSC writing, but they do not belong to the same etymological family.

| Word        | Meaning                                                         | Difference from Intricate                         |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Complex     | Consisting of many interconnected parts                         | Broadest and most neutral term.                   |
| Complicated | Difficult to understand or deal with                            | Emphasises difficulty.                            |
| Elaborate   | Detailed and carefully developed                                | Often carries a positive connotation.             |
| Convoluted  | Excessively complicated and difficult to follow                 | Usually negative.                                 |
| Byzantine   | Excessively complex, especially in procedures or administration | Suggests labyrinthine complexity.                 |
| Tangled     | Confused or entangled                                           | Emphasises disorder and difficulty of separation. |
| Interwoven  | Closely interconnected                                          | Emphasises mutual relationships.                  |
| Intertwined | Inseparably connected                                           | Emphasises interdependence.                       |
| Involved    | Complicated or requiring considerable attention                 | Often used for processes and situations.          |
| Perplexing  | Causing confusion or uncertainty                                | Emphasises the mental effect on the observer.     |

Answer-writing tip: Avoid using complex, intricate and convoluted interchangeably in every context.

- Use complex for multifaceted problems.
- Use intricate for detailed, interconnected structures.
- Use convoluted for unnecessarily complicated processes or arguments.
- Use inextricably linked for inseparable relationships.
